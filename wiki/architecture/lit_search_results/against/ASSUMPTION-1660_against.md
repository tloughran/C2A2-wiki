  Sources:
    1. Pitkin, R.M., Branagan, M.A., & Burmeister, L.F. (1999). "Accuracy of Data in Abstracts of
       Published Research Articles." JAMA 281(12):1110-1111. [Fetched: full text via psychrights.org
       mirror; the PubMed page was blocked by a reCAPTCHA and was not bypassed.] In 6 major medical
       journals, 18%-68% of author-written abstracts had data that was inconsistent with the body
       or missing from it. Inconsistency was more common than omission. The authors note that they
       did not test the reverse question, whether the important content of the article appears in
       the abstract at all. That untested question is exactly the one that matters for a summary
       used in place of a source.
    2. Peters, U. & Chin-Yee, B. (2025). "Generalization bias in large language model summarization
       of scientific research." Royal Society Open Science 12(4):241776; arXiv:2504.00025. [Fetched:
       arXiv abstract page.] Across 4,900 summaries, LLM summaries dropped scope-limiting qualifiers
       and overgeneralized in 26-73% of cases for several models. They were about 5 times more likely
       than human summaries to generalize broadly (OR 4.85). This applies if the host's summary or
       chapter blurbs were machine-assisted, and also to the step where C2A2 turns a summary into
       triplets.
    3. Sumner, P. et al. (2014). "The association between exaggeration in health related science news
       and academic press releases." BMJ. [Seen in search results only; not fetched.] Distortion
       introduced at the first secondary layer (the press release) was carried into the next layer
       (news) at high rates. A host's summary is the analogue of a press release: written by an
       interested party and read as a proxy for the primary source.
    4. Boutron, I. et al. (2014). "Impact of spin in the abstracts of articles reporting results of
       RCTs in the field of cancer: the SPIIN randomized controlled trial." J Clin Oncol. [Seen in
       search results only.] Spin in summary text measurably changed expert readers' interpretation
       of results. Summaries shape claim structure, not only detail.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate

  Summary: No study tests podcast show-notes or chapter lists against their recordings. The nearest
  literature (abstract vs full text; press release vs paper; LLM summary vs source) consistently
  finds that summary layers (a) have high rates of factual inconsistency, (b) drop qualifiers so
  that claims read broader than the source supports, and (c) pass distortions downstream. The
  qualifier-loss finding hits the triplet format hardest. A triplet is subject, relation and
  object. The hedges, conditions and scope that make a Kastrup claim defensible are the parts most
  likely to be lost. Pitkin also finds many discrepancies were minor. That supports a narrower
  claim: the gist and topic structure usually survive, while the modality and scope of individual
  claims do not. A chapter list gives topics, not claims.

  Specific risks: Triplets from a host summary may record an overgeneralized or host-framed version
  of the thinker's position. Once in the tradition record, it will be cited as Kastrup's own. The
  secondary provenance recorded in the confidence rating may not travel with the triplet
  downstream, which repeats Sumner's pattern of carry-forward. The host's framing is itself an
  interested reading.

  Mitigations available: Keep these triplets in a "secondary-derived" tier that cannot be promoted
  without primary confirmation. Cross-check each triplet against Kastrup's own published writing on
  the same topic. Record the relation with explicit modality ("host reports K. argues…"). Avoid
  relations stronger than the summary's verbs.

STEELMAN:
  Item: ASSUMPTION-1660
  Strongest counterargument: Summaries are not neutral compressions. They are written by an
    interested party, and the evidence shows they systematically drop the qualifiers that set a
    claim's scope. A triplet keeps subject-relation-object and discards modality, so it is the
    format most exposed to that loss. Even in top journals, where authors summarize their own work,
    one-fifth to two-thirds of summaries contain unverifiable data. A host summarizing a guest
    will do no better. Recording such triplets as the tradition's position turns the host's reading
    into Kastrup's claim.
  What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: The triplets are tagged and weighted as secondary.
    They are restricted to topic-level assertions, not modal or causal claims. They cannot be
    promoted until matched to a primary Kastrup text.
  How to test: For a guest whose episode is freely available, build triplets both ways (summary
    only and transcript) and score agreement on relation direction, modality and scope.
