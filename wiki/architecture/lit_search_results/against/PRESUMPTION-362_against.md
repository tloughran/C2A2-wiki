    1. Relational-insight primacy in sociograms — the point of a sociogram is the TIES; in a project whose telos is the INTERACTION of traditions, putting all explanatory weight on nodes under-annotates the cross-connections that carry the central insight. Edges may be where the meaning lives.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate

  Summary: Node placement of descriptive bios is conventional (15a), but the presumption that summaries belong ONLY on nodes and NOT on edges/cross-connections is challenged: in a sociogram, and especially in a project about tradition INTERACTION, the relationships often carry the key insight, and edge/tie annotation is a standard, here arguably essential, layer. The node-only choice may under-serve the project's telos.

  Specific risks: The most project-relevant information (how traditions relate/critique/inform one another) goes unexplained; the sociogram becomes a gallery of actors rather than a map of interactions.

  Mitigations available: Add lightweight edge/relationship annotation (at least for the salient cross-connections) without cluttering; treat node summaries as layer one and tie explanations as a complementary layer two; prioritize annotating the connections that embody the research question.

  STEELMAN:
    Strongest counterargument: Annotating ties first risks clutter and presumes the relationships are already well-characterized; nodes are the stable, well-understood unit, so summarizing them first is the correct sequencing — edge annotation can follow once the tie semantics are settled.

    How to test: Ask whether a viewer can learn from the sociogram HOW two traditions interact; if only WHO is present is explained, the node-only scheme is under-serving the telos.
