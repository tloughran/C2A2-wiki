**Polarity note (explicit inversion).** 14b's residual claim is that self-audit is inadequate and that an independent reader is required. The proposition I am challenging is **that residual claim**, and I must report at the outset that the challenge is the weakest of the six items in this batch. What the literature supplies is not evidence that self-audit is sufficient — I found none — but evidence that the *proposed remedy* is substantially less effective than its reputation, that the largest share of detection comes from individual preparation rather than from collective review, and that second passes show sharply diminishing returns. That is a challenge to the remedy, not to the diagnosis. I state this plainly rather than dressing a weak challenge as a strong one.

### Challenging evidence found: Partial

### Sources

1. **Votta, L. G., 1993. "Does Every Inspection Need a Meeting?" *ACM SIGSOFT Software Engineering Notes* (FSE '93), DOI 10.1145/167049.167070; and Porter, A. A. & Johnson, P. M., 1997, "Assessing Software Review Meetings," / Porter & Votta, 1998, "Does Every Inspection Really Need a Meeting?" *Empirical Software Engineering* 3(1):9–35, DOI 10.1023/A:1009787822215.** [verified this run — both titles, venues and DOIs confirmed; abstracts and secondary summaries read, full texts not read. The exact author split between the 1993 and 1998 papers is standard but I have not re-verified it against the papers themselves.] — The principal challenge, and it is well replicated. In controlled experiments with over a hundred subjects inspecting requirements documents, **nominal teams outperformed real teams**; meeting losses (defects found by an individual but not reported by the team) exceeded meeting gains; **approximately 90% of defects were detected during individual preparation and 10% during the meeting**; and meetings extended the inspection interval by roughly 30% in a large project. The consequence for this item is direct: the marginal value of the *collective, independent* stage is far smaller than the folk model assumes, and detection is overwhelmingly a function of one person reading carefully.
2. **Bacchelli, A. & Bird, C., 2013. "Expectations, Outcomes, and Challenges of Modern Code Review." ICSE 2013.** [verified this run — authors, title and venue confirmed across Microsoft Research, TU Delft and ZORA repositories; abstract and hosted PDF located, full text not read.] — The challenge to the modern form of the remedy. Observing, interviewing and surveying developers and managers at Microsoft and manually classifying hundreds of review comments, the study found that although finding defects remains the main stated motivation, **"reviews are less about defects than expected"**, and that the realised value lies in code and change understanding, knowledge transfer, and team awareness. Applied here: the assumption that an independent reader would have caught the eight extractor defects is exactly the expectation this study measured and found overstated.
3. **Biffl, S. & Halling, M., 2000/2001. "Investigating the effect of a second software inspection cycle: cost-benefit data from a large-scale experiment on reinspection of a software requirements document" (IEEE conference, document 883793); and "Investigating the Cost-Effectiveness of Reinspections in Software Development," ICSE 2001.** [verified this run — titles and venues confirmed via IEEE Xplore and IEEE Computer Society digital library; author attribution inferred from the dblp record for Michael Halling and from the surrounding literature, **not** directly confirmed on the papers. Abstracts only.] — Bears on 14b's explicit question of whether repeated detection converges or plateaus. In the most comprehensive experiment available — a requirements document reinspected by 31 teams after first-round defects were removed — **benefits and net gain were significantly lower for the reinspection than for the initial inspection**, though still positive for most teams under conservative assumptions. The literature notes candidly that "very few empirical results can be found on re-inspections." So a plateau is the documented shape, which is at least consistent with a self-audit finding the same four defects twice.
4. **Fagan-inspection aggregate effectiveness figures.** [verified this run through secondary and tertiary sources only — Fagan's 1976 *IBM Systems Journal* paper was **not** read this run, and the numbers below should be treated as indicative.] — Reported ranges: inspections detect 80–90% of defects in artefacts under favourable conditions; Fagan's original work reported 82% across design and code inspection; IBM phase-specific rates of 60% for source code, 80% for pseudocode, 88% for module and interface specifications; and across the broader literature, inspections find **"as little as 20% to as much as 93%"**, averaging around **57%** of defects in code and design documents. **This source runs partly against my own direction and is reported as such:** a 57% average means a single detection pass, however conducted, leaves a large residual.
5. **Capture–recapture in software inspections: Eick et al., 1992/1993 (first application); Wohlin, C. & Runeson, P., 1995, "An experimental evaluation of capture-recapture in software inspections," *Software Testing, Verification and Reliability* 5(4); Briand et al., 2000; Wohlin & Runeson, 2004, "Capture-recapture in software inspections after 10 years research — theory, evaluation and application," *Journal of Systems and Software*.** [verified this run — the Wohlin 1995 STVR article, the JSS 2004 review, and the Briand comprehensive evaluation were all located with venues; abstracts and hosted PDFs only.] — Included for a specific and unwelcome structural reason. Capture–recapture is the method 14b's search direction named for estimating residual defects, and it **requires at least two inspectors searching independently** — "prior to each inspection meeting, the inspectors search independently for defects." It cannot be applied to a single self-audit at all. The same literature reports that its assumptions (notably model Mt's requirement of equal detection probabilities across defects) "are not completely fulfilled in software development, leading to an underestimation of the residual fault content." So the estimator 14b hoped for is both inapplicable to the current configuration and known to be biased optimistic where it does apply.

### Strength of challenge: Weak-to-Moderate

### Summary

I did not find evidence that self-audit is adequate, and I should say so first: no source located in this search supports the presumption directly. What the literature does challenge is the assumed remedy and the assumed magnitude of the gap. The inspection-experiment literature has found repeatedly that nominal teams outperform real teams, that meeting losses exceed meeting gains, and that roughly 90% of defects surface during individual preparation rather than in the collective stage — so the detection work is done by one careful reader, and the independence of that reader is a smaller factor than the folk model credits. The largest observational study of modern code review reinforces this: reviews at Microsoft turned out to be "less about defects than expected," with their realised value in understanding and knowledge transfer, which directly undercuts the counterfactual that an independent reader would have caught the eight extractor defects. On 14b's own question of convergence, the reinspection experiments show a plateau — second-cycle benefits significantly lower than first-cycle, though still net positive — which is consistent with the observed pattern of a self-audit re-finding the same four defects. Against all of this stands a number that runs the other way and that I will not soften: the aggregate inspection literature puts average detection at around 57% of defects, with a range from 20% to 93%. Whatever the reader's identity, one pass leaves roughly two-fifths of the defects in place. And the estimator 14b's search direction pointed at is unavailable: capture–recapture needs two independent detection streams by construction, so a fleet that audits only from inside cannot even measure how much it is missing — which is PREMISE-144's structural point reappearing as a measurement impossibility rather than as an argument.

### Specific risks

If the presumption is false — and the balance of this evidence suggests it is at least incomplete — the exposure is as 14b states it: every corpus-level number the project publishes derives from an instrument audited only by the run that wrote it, and on the aggregate figures roughly two-fifths of that instrument's defects have never been found by anyone. The 50% two-night recurrence rate admits two readings that the current configuration cannot distinguish: a detector converging on a stable defect set (benign) or a detector with a fixed blind spot re-finding only what it is able to see (severe), and no internal measurement separates them. If the presumption is challenged and the remedy is a mandated independent reader, the counter-risk is that the fleet buys the least effective part of the inspection process at its full cost — the meeting-stage yield is around 10%, the interval cost around 30%, and modern code review's measured defect yield is lower than practitioners expect — while the effective lever, careful individual preparation with a defined reading technique, goes unaddressed. That is a real risk of remediating in the wrong place, and it is the strongest thing I can say in the AGAINST direction on this item.

### Mitigations available

(a) **Seed known defects.** The one measurement that works with a single auditor is defect seeding: inject N known faults into the extractor's input, count how many the self-audit finds, and estimate residual content from the recapture rate on seeded versus natural defects. This gives the quantitative answer 14b wants without requiring a second reader, and it is the standard fallback where capture–recapture's independence assumption fails. (b) **Invest in reading technique before investing in readers.** Given that ~90% of detection occurs in individual preparation, a defined reading procedure — checklist-based or perspective-based — applied by the owning run is likely to yield more per unit cost than adding an independent reviewer, and the comparison is itself measurable. (c) **Where independence is bought, buy it as a second independent *reading*, not a meeting.** The experimental result is specifically that the collective stage is where value is lost; two independent preparations with mechanical merge dominates two preparations plus a meeting, and it simultaneously creates the second detection stream that makes capture–recapture applicable. (d) **Treat the recurrence rate as a designed measurement rather than an incident.** Recording, for each night, which defects are new and which are repeats converts the 50% figure into a convergence curve; a curve that flattens with new defects still appearing is a blind-spot signature, and one that flattens with no new defects is convergence. (e) **Do not rely on a capture–recapture estimate from a single stream, and treat any such estimate as optimistic** — the method's own literature reports that its assumptions are unmet in software settings and that residual fault content is consequently underestimated.

### Recommendation: PARTIALLY-CHALLENGED

## STEELMAN

**Item:** PRESUMPTION-792

**Strongest counterargument:** The independent reader is a weaker instrument than the item assumes, and the evidence for that is thirty years old and replicated. Controlled inspection experiments have repeatedly found that nominal teams — individuals whose findings are merged mechanically, never meeting — outperform real teams; that meeting losses exceed meeting gains; and that around 90% of defects are found during individual preparation, with the collective stage contributing about a tenth of the yield while adding roughly 30% to the interval. The largest observational study of modern code review, covering hundreds of manually classified review comments at Microsoft, found that reviews "are less about defects than expected," with their realised value lying in understanding and knowledge transfer rather than in defect detection. The reinspection experiments answer 14b's convergence question in the same direction: a second cycle's net gain is significantly lower than the first's, so a plateau is the expected shape and a self-audit re-finding the same four defects on the second night is what the literature predicts of *any* second pass, independent or not. On this reading the finding is not that self-audit is uniquely blind but that one detection pass is one detection pass, and mandating an independent reader would purchase the least productive component of the inspection process at its full cost while leaving the productive component — careful individual reading with a defined technique — untouched. **Where this stops, and it stops short of vindicating the presumption:** none of the above shows self-audit is sufficient. The aggregate figure for inspection effectiveness is roughly 57% of defects on average, ranging from 20% to 93%, so a single pass by anyone leaves a large residual, and the item's claim is about adequacy, not about who reads. Worse, the estimator that would settle it is structurally unavailable: capture–recapture requires two independent detection streams by construction, so a fleet auditing only from inside cannot measure its own residual defect content at all, and the 50% recurrence rate is therefore compatible with both a converging detector and a detector with a permanent blind spot. That is PREMISE-144's structural claim reappearing as an arithmetic obstacle rather than an argument, and it is the strongest thing the FOR-14b side has.

**What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe:** Either (1) a defect-seeding experiment establishes that the self-audit's detection rate on known injected faults is high enough that the residual is tolerable for the corpus-level numbers the project publishes, or (2) a second independent reading exists for at least a sample of nights, so that capture–recapture becomes applicable and the residual is estimable rather than assumed. In the absence of either, the presumption is not merely untested — it is *unmeasurable from inside*, which is a stronger statement than "untested" and should be recorded as such.

**How to test:** Seed the extractor's input with a known set of injected defects spanning the classes seen across the two nights, and run the ordinary self-audit blind to the seeding. The recapture rate on seeded defects estimates the detection rate, and the number of natural defects found alongside them estimates the residual. This is a single night's work and it does not require a second agent. Then, separately, decompose the recurrence: for each of the eight defects, record whether it was new or a repeat and whether the second-night audit found anything the first did not. If the second night produced no new defect classes at all, the detector has plateaued and the question is whether it plateaued at 57% or at 93% — which only the seeding experiment answers. Finally, and as the cheap comparative: have one night's audit performed with a defined checklist-based reading procedure and compare the yield to the unstructured baseline. If the structured self-audit closes most of the gap, the effective remedy is technique and not independence, and the fleet can avoid buying the expensive part of the wrong fix.
