    1. Xu, T., Jin, X., Huang, P., Zhou, Y., Jin, L., Pasupathy, S., & Lu, S. (2016). "Early Detection of Configuration Errors to Reduce Failure Damage." *OSDI '16* (Jay Lepreau Best Paper). — The decisive citation. Names **latent configuration (LC) errors**: settings in mature, widely-used systems that are wrong but produce no symptom, because the code that would read them is not exercised at startup. The paper's central point is that a limit which never fires provides *no information about compliance* — it may be unenforced, misconfigured, or dead. C2A2's "no limit warning emitted" is a textbook LC condition, not evidence of legality.
    2. Yuan, D., Luo, Y., Zhuang, X., Rodrigues, G., Zhao, X., Zhang, Y., Jain, P. U., & Stumm, M. (2014). "Simple Testing Can Prevent Most Critical Failures: An Analysis of Production Failures in Distributed Data-Intensive Systems." *OSDI '14*. — 198 production failures across Cassandra, HBase, HDFS, Hadoop MapReduce, Redis. The failure population is dominated by **untested error- and check-handling paths**. A guard that has never fired in production is, statistically, one of the least trustworthy pieces of code in the system.
    3. Aghajani, E., Nagy, C., Vega-Márquez, O. L., Linares-Vásquez, M., Moreno, L., Bavota, G., & Lanza, M. (2019). "Software Documentation Issues Unveiled." *ICSE 2019*. — 878 documentation artifacts, taxonomy of 162 issue types; outdated, incomplete and *inconsistent* information are among the most frequent categories. In an accompanying analysis of 3,000+ GitHub projects, **most projects contain at least one outdated code-element reference at some point in their history**. Documentation-to-code divergence is the base rate, not the exception. C2A2's 2,000/3,000 (docs) vs 20,000/30,000 (code) is a 10× divergence — well inside the observed distribution.
    4. U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (2013). *In the Matter of Knight Capital Americas LLC*, Admin. Proc. File No. 3-15570, Order of Oct. 16, 2013 (SEC Release 34-70694). — Canonical configuration-drift catastrophe: one of eight servers did not receive the new code; no deployment checklist and no automated version-consistency check existed; a dormant code path activated and produced ~$460M in losses in 45 minutes. The documented configuration and the enforced configuration diverged silently, and nothing detected the divergence until it manifested.
    5. Rasmussen, J. (1997). "Risk Management in a Dynamic Society: A Modelling Problem." *Safety Science*, 27(2/3), 183–213. — The drift model. Systems migrate toward the boundary of acceptable performance under efficiency and workload gradients. Crucially: "as accidents are not happening we don't know how far from the accident boundary we are." The absence of a limit warning is precisely the condition under which drift is invisible.

  Strength of challenge: **Strong**

  Summary: The presumption is refuted both in principle and by a directly matching named failure mode. Xu et al. (2016) establish that a limit which does not fire carries no evidence about compliance — latent configuration errors are precisely the class where the check exists on paper, exists in code, and is never reached. Yuan et al. add that rarely-exercised check and error-handling paths are the single most failure-prone category in production systems, so the guard C2A2 is implicitly trusting is in the worst-tested part of the codebase. Aghajani et al. make documentation-to-code divergence the base rate rather than an anomaly, which means the 10× gap between the documented (2,000/3,000) and coded (20,000/30,000) limits is unsurprising and should have been expected. Knight Capital supplies the consequence: silent divergence between documented and deployed configuration, undetected because no surface compared the surfaces, catastrophic when the dormant path finally activated. Rasmussen, Snook and Dekker generalise it: written limits that no longer bind are the normal drift state of any operating system, and the absence of alarms is exactly what makes the drift invisible. C2A2's three-surface disagreement — with the shipped artifact (3,864/98,201) legal on none of them — is not an anomaly to be explained; it is the predicted outcome.

  Specific risks:
    (a) **The guard may be dead code.** No warning was emitted while the artifact exceeded every stated limit. Either the guard does not run, or it runs against a limit no surface documents, or it runs and its output is discarded. All three are LC errors under Xu et al.
    (b) **Silent breach of the real constraint.** The documented limits (2,000/3,000) were presumably chosen for a reason — the crash-proofing note in the project's own CLAUDE.md ties them to browser stability. The shipped artifact carries 98,201 edges against a documented ceiling of 3,000, a 32× overrun. Whatever the limits were protecting is not protected.
    (c) **Erosion of the limit as a concept.** Once a limit has been exceeded 32× with no consequence, the organisational reading becomes "the limit was too conservative" rather than "the limit is not enforced." Rasmussen's boundary has moved and nobody has measured where it now is.
    (d) **Reasoning on the wrong surface.** The day's reports read two of three surfaces. Any downstream agent making a capacity decision from the docs is reasoning from a number that has been wrong by 10× at minimum.
    (e) **Generalisation.** If three surfaces disagree here, the question is not whether this limit is enforced but how many other documented constraints in C2A2 are also decorative.

  Mitigations available:
    (a) **PCHECK-style early checking (Xu et al. 2016):** assert the limits at the start of the regeneration run, against the actual data, and fail loudly. Do not defer to a path that may not execute.
    (b) **Force the failure path.** Yuan et al.'s prescription and, notably, a principle C2A2 has already articulated in-house ("force the failure, do not wait for it," ASSUMPTION-720). Add a test that deliberately exceeds the limit and asserts that the warning fires. If it does not fire in the test, the guard is dead.
    (c) **Single source of truth for the limits,** with docs generated from the constant rather than restating it — removes the documentation-decay channel entirely (Aghajani et al.).
    (d) **A cross-surface consistency assertion in the validator:** `docs_limit == code_limit` and `artifact_count <= code_limit`, run at build time. This is one assertion; it is the direct analogue of the deployment-consistency script Knight Capital did not have.
    (e) **Decide which number is correct before reconciling.** The three surfaces cannot be made to agree by editing docs; someone must determine the real safe ceiling empirically.

  Recommendation: **CHALLENGED (Strong)** — a documented limit is not an enforced limit; an unfired guard is not evidence of compliance. Remediation is cheap (one build-time assertion plus one forced-failure test) and the literature is unambiguous.

  STEELMAN:
    Item: PRESUMPTION-675
    Strongest counterargument: The best-tested result in this area is Xu et al.'s: in mature, heavily-used, well-maintained systems, safety-relevant configuration is *routinely* wrong and *routinely* silent, because the code that reads it is not on the hot path. The silence is not weak evidence of compliance — it is the *defining symptom* of the failure. C2A2 has all three preconditions simultaneously: a limit stated in prose, a different limit stated in code, and an artifact that violates both, with no diagnostic. Yuan et al. then sharpen it: the guard C2A2 is implicitly relying on lives in exactly the code category that dominates production failures, because it has never been exercised. And Rasmussen closes the argument — the system cannot know how much margin remains, because the only instrument that would tell it is the alarm that has never sounded. The uncomfortable inference is not "one limit is stale." It is that C2A2 currently has no working method for distinguishing an enforced constraint from a described one, and therefore no basis for trusting *any* documented constraint in the system until each is independently exercised.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (a) the code-level limits (20,000/30,000) are the real ones, are actually reached and checked at runtime, and the artifact is under them — but the artifact is at 98,201 edges, so this is already false; (b) the limits protect against a hazard that has since been eliminated by other means, and this has been established rather than assumed; (c) the guard has been demonstrated to fire, at least once, in a forced test.
    How to test: Three commands. (1) Run the regeneration with a synthetic dataset that exceeds the code limit and confirm a warning is emitted — if none is, the guard is dead. (2) Grep the generator for the limit constants and compare to the documented values. (3) Open the shipped artifact in a browser at 98,201 edges and observe whether the hazard the limit was written to prevent actually occurs. Test (1) is decisive on its own.
