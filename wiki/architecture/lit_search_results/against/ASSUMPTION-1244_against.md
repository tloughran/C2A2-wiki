  Sources:
    1. Pöppelbuß, J. & Röglinger, M. 2011. "What makes a useful maturity model? A framework of general
       design principles for maturity models and its demonstration in business process management."
       *ECIS 2011 Proceedings*. (Snippet-level.)
       — The decisive source. The design literature distinguishes *purposes of use*: a **descriptive**
       maturity model determines current maturity and yields an as-is assessment; a **prescriptive**
       model additionally identifies desirable levels and gives guidance on reaching them. Only the
       prescriptive purpose carries the ascent commitment. A model can describe levels and their
       items without prescribing how or whether to move between them. This falsifies "by
       construction": ascent is a purpose-of-use choice, not a structural entailment.
    2. Lasrado, L., Vatrapu, R. & Andersen, K.N. "A Set Theoretical Approach to Maturity Models:
       Guidelines and Demonstration." *ICIS* (cbsbda.github.io / ResearchGate; snippet-level).
       — Attacks the ascent frame from a second direction: the assumption of a single linear path to
       maturation with no possibility of *equifinality* is a widely criticised design defect, not a
       necessary feature. Progression may occur through configurations of multiple organisational and
       environmental conditions rather than a linear sequence. Higher levels are not uniformly better
       across dimensions, and the same entity may be advanced on one dimension and early on another.
    3. "Maturity Models in IS Research" (ECIS; authorship not confirmed from snippet — likely Becker,
       Knackstedt & Pöppelbuß or colleagues) and "A Survey of Maturity Models from Nolan to DevOps and
       Their Applications in Process Improvement," arXiv:1907.01878.
       — Record that maturity models *in industry practice* are normative and prescriptive by nature,
       and that this is precisely the target of intense debate and critique — lack of theoretical
       foundation, structures inherited from CMM without conceptual grounding, weak empirical
       validation of dimension selection. So the ascent frame is documented as the *dominant* pattern
       and simultaneously as a criticised one. That combination is fatal to "by construction" but
       supportive of 14a's practical concern.
    4. "E-government Stage Models: A Contextual Critique" (authorship not confirmed from snippet).
       — A domain case of the same critique: stage models are faulted for progressive, sequential,
       prescriptive logic driven by life-cycle motors with teleological drivers toward an envisioned
       end state. The critique is coherent only if that logic is contingent and avoidable.
    5. Bjorgaard, P. 2020. "Stage Models of Adult Development: A Critical Introduction." *Integral
       Review* 16(2); and Churchill & Murray, "Integrating Adult Developmental and Metacognitive
       Theory," *Integral Review* 16(1). (Snippet-level.)
       — Critiques of teleological "upward and onward" assumptions in developmental stage models, and
       of the reintroduction of 'higher' and 'more advanced' into stadial development. Suggestive
       that developmental modelling need not be ascending, though these sources are internal to the
       movement they critique and should be weighted accordingly.
    6. St John of the Cross, *Dark Night of the Soul* (16th c.). — Accessed only via a tertiary
       encyclopaedia entry; flagged as weak. The contemplative counter-case is that a later stage is
       characterised as passive purification, confusion, helplessness, withdrawal and "unselfing" —
       i.e. described as subtraction and descent rather than accumulation and ascent. This is the
       strand 14a's claim would most need to accommodate, and I did not reach adequate scholarship on
       it within budget. Carried as an open lead rather than as evidence.
    7. Practitioner design guidance encountered across several maturity-model sources (blog/consulting
       tier, cited only as evidence that the norm is articulated, not as scholarship): explicit
       instruction that a maturity model is a diagnostic and development tool rather than a judgment,
       that Level 2 is not "bad," and that value judgments discourage honest assessment.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate

  Summary: The claim fails in its universal form. The maturity-model design literature draws exactly
  the distinction the claim denies — descriptive models assess an as-is state without prescribing
  movement, prescriptive models add the ascent commitment — so ascent is a purpose-of-use decision
  made by the model's designer, not a structural entailment of having ordered stages. The equifinality
  critique reinforces this: the single-linear-path assumption is treated in the literature as a
  well-known design defect to be avoided, which presupposes it can be. That said, the challenge is
  Moderate rather than Strong for a reason that matters. The same literature concedes that maturity
  models in practice are normative and prescriptive by nature, and that CMM-descended structures import
  ascent without conceptual grounding. So 14a is right about the default and wrong about the necessity.
  For this intake, that is the useful correction: the ascent frame is a real and near-universal risk
  that must be actively designed out, and the design moves for doing so are documented — but rejecting
  stage structure wholesale on grounds of necessity is not supported.

  Specific risks: If the claim is treated as true, C2A2 forecloses a class of instruments it could use
  safely — descriptive, multi-dimensional, equifinal stage descriptions — and loses the ability to
  describe its own trajectory at all, since any ordered account of change would be pre-judged as
  smuggling in ascent. If the claim is treated as false without the accompanying caution, the
  near-certain failure is that C2A2 adopts a maturity model, inherits the CMM default silently, and
  ends up ranking states of the project as better and worse on a single axis it never chose. The
  contemplative counter-case sharpens the stakes: on some accounts of development the later state has
  *less*, and a model that cannot represent subtraction as development will misdescribe it as regression.

  Mitigations available: If a stage model is used, state its purpose of use explicitly (descriptive
  only) and say in the model itself that levels are not rankings. Make it multi-dimensional so no
  single ordering exists. Build in equifinality — allow more than one route and more than one
  terminal configuration. Include at least one dimension where a later stage has less than an earlier
  one, which tests whether the frame can represent subtraction. Retain 14a's caution as a design
  constraint even though its stated justification does not hold.

  STEELMAN:
    Strongest counterargument: The descriptive/prescriptive distinction may be a distinction without a
    difference in use. Even a model that formally disclaims ranking presents its levels in an order and
    names the top one "mature"; readers reliably read ordering as evaluation, and organisations set
    targets against descriptive assessments regardless of the designer's stated purpose. On that view
    "ascending by construction" is a claim about the pragmatics of the artefact rather than its formal
    structure, and the design literature's disclaimers are exactly the kind of caveat that does not
    survive contact with users. The persistence of the critique across four decades — the fact that the
    linear-path assumption keeps having to be re-criticised — is itself evidence that the frame is
    difficult to escape in practice.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: that a stage description C2A2 adopts can be shown,
    empirically rather than by stipulation, not to be read as a ranking by its actual readers; or that
    C2A2 uses no ordered stage vocabulary at all.
    How to test: Two moves. (1) Naming test: strip the level labels and replace them with
    non-ordinal names; if the model still does its work, the ascent frame was decorative, and if it
    collapses, ascent was load-bearing and 14a is vindicated in substance. (2) Subtraction test:
    attempt to place a genuine case of development-as-loss — a capability deliberately given up — in
    the model. If the model can only record this as regression, it is ascending in construction after
    all, whatever its stated purpose of use.
