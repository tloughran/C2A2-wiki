  Original statement: Whether a long-held item is a governance success or a decision made by inaction. Risk: Medium.

  Reading challenged: The operative (reassuring) reading — that an item which has sat in a hold/exception queue for a long time without being closed represents deliberate, considered governance ("we are holding it on purpose"). This search attacks that reading: the literature on decision avoidance, nondecision-making, escalation, and exception-register hygiene says duration in a queue is far better explained by the absence of a forcing function than by the presence of a judgement.

  Sources:
    1. Anderson, C.J. (2003). "The Psychology of Doing Nothing: Forms of Decision Avoidance Result from Reason and Emotion." Psychological Bulletin 129(1), 139–167. — Identifies four distinct, empirically supported mechanisms that produce non-decision: choice deferral, status quo bias, omission bias, and inaction inertia — each driven by anticipated regret, selection difficulty, and cost–benefit asymmetry rather than by judgement about the item's merits. Directly supplies the alternative hypothesis: a long-held item is the expected output of a system with no deadline, not a signal of care.     2. Bachrach, P. & Baratz, M.S. (1962). "Two Faces of Power." American Political Science Review 56(4), 947–952; and their subsequent development of nondecision-making. — "Nondecision-making is a means by which demands for change ... can be suffocated before they are even voiced; or kept covert; or killed before they gain access to the relevant decision-making arena; or, failing all these things, maimed or destroyed in the decision-implementing stage." The canonical statement that failing to decide *is* an exercise of power with a real distributional outcome. Refutes the framing that a held item is in a neutral, undecided state: it is in a decided state whose decision nobody has to own. [VERIFIED — 1962 APSR paper, full text publicly hosted; the nondecision concept and the quoted passage are standard in the literature. Applied here: whoever placed the item on hold is the person least able to read the hold as a failure, so the queue's own owners are structurally unable to distinguish success from inaction.   Strength of challenge: Strong (on the mechanism); Moderate (on the empirical base, because the strongest operational sources are practitioner rather than peer-reviewed)

  Summary: There is no serious literature supporting the reading that duration-on-hold indicates deliberation, and four independent literatures supply mechanisms for the opposite. Anderson catalogues the cognitive machinery that generates non-decision as a default output. Bachrach and Baratz establish that non-decision is not the absence of a decision but a decision whose author is unaccountable. Staw explains why the item's own custodians will read the hold charitably. Vaughan explains how, given enough uneventful time, the held state silently becomes the norm. The GRC practitioner consensus reports precisely this as an operational pathology with a named remedy. The decisive point for C2A2 is that "governance success" and "decision by inaction" are *observationally identical* in a queue that has no expiry, no renewal requirement, and no recorded rationale — so the presumption cannot be evaluated at all from the artefact as it stands. That is a stronger objection than "the reassuring reading is wrong": the reassuring reading is currently unfalsifiable.

  Specific risks:
    - Unfalsifiability: with no expiry timestamp, no renewal event, and no recorded holder, the two hypotheses generate identical evidence. The pipeline cannot audit its own hold queue.
    - Accountability laundering: per Bachrach and Baratz, the held item's effective disposition has been decided, but no agent or run is on the hook for it. This is the governance failure the item is at risk of mislabelling as a governance success.
    - Threshold drift: per Vaughan, each additional run in which the held item causes no visible harm raises the tolerance for holding it, so the presumption self-reinforces over time and becomes harder to challenge the longer it survives.
    - Custodian bias: per Staw, review by whoever created the hold is not independent review; asking the pipeline that made the hold whether the hold was correct is not a test.
    - Register growth: if the hold queue's cardinality is monotonically non-decreasing across runs, the practitioner literature classifies that on its own as a warning sign, independent of any individual item's merits.

  Mitigations available:
    - Mandatory expiry on every hold, with non-automatic renewal — the single remedy the practitioner literature converges on. A hold that survives must be *re-decided*, which converts inaction into action.
    - Escalating approval: second renewal requires a higher authority than the first. This makes indefinite holding expensive rather than free.
    - Record the rationale and the holder at hold time, so the two hypotheses become distinguishable retrospectively.
    - Publish the register's size trend. A queue that only grows is diagnostic regardless of item-level argument.
    - Separate the reviewer of a hold from its creator, to break the Staw self-justification loop.
    - Adopt a default disposition on expiry (e.g. auto-close, or auto-escalate) so that inaction resolves to a *stated* outcome rather than to continued limbo.

  STEELMAN:
    Item: PRESUMPTION-762
    Strongest counterargument: The decision-avoidance and nondecision literatures are about human institutions with reputational stakes, career incentives, and anticipated regret — none of which straightforwardly apply to an agent pipeline that neither fears blame nor accumulates sunk-cost identification. A long hold in C2A2 may be the correct and even the sophisticated outcome: some questions genuinely cannot be resolved with the evidence currently available, and the alternative to holding is forcing a premature disposition, which is a worse error. Escalation-of-commitment findings in particular assume an agent whose self-concept is bound to the earlier decision, which is an odd thing to attribute to a stateless run. Furthermore, an expiry-and-renewal regime is not free: it converts a cheap hold into a recurring cost, and if the renewals are performed by the same correlated readers, the "re-decision" is theatre that adds budget without adding information (cf. PRESUMPTION-751).
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (a) Every hold must carry a recorded reason and an expected resolution condition, so that "we are waiting for X" is distinguishable from "nobody looked"; (b) the hold register must have a bounded, published size and a non-monotonic trend; (c) renewals must be performed by a different agent or substrate from the one that created the hold; (d) an explicit default disposition must fire on expiry so that inaction has a stated consequence; (e) the marginal cost of the renewal regime must be less than the expected cost of the misjudgements the register is accumulating.
    How to test: Sample every currently-held item and attempt, blind, to reconstruct from the artefacts alone (i) who placed the hold, (ii) why, (iii) what would resolve it. The fraction for which all three are recoverable is a direct measure of whether the reassuring reading is even checkable; my expectation, given that `provenance_protocol.md` was already found to lack a terminal state for withdrawn flags (see PRESUMPTION-833), is that it will be low. Then run the counterfactual: force-resolve a random half of the held items and leave the other half held for N further runs. Compare downstream error rates and budget. If forced resolution produces no more errors than continued holding, the holds were inaction, not governance. Track register cardinality over time as the standing indicator.
