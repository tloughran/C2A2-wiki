  Challenging evidence found: Partial

  Sources:
    1. "Beyond Generalist LLMs: Specialist Agentic Systems for Structured Code Workflow Execution." arXiv:2607.14456 (July 2026) / OpenReview Fjd2O8RIqu. — New. Specialist systems offer more predictable and operationally manageable behaviour than general-purpose agents on structured tasks; generalist systems demand more planning and incur higher token/cost overhead. Supports the existence of a real generalist-fallback penalty, though it measures predictability and cost rather than domain fidelity.
    2. "Specialists or Generalists? Multi-Agent and Single-Agent LLMs for Essay Grading." arXiv:2601.22386 (Jan 2026). — New and the most interesting result for this item: the specialist/generalist advantage is *task-region dependent* — multi-agent specialist configurations are significantly better at identifying weak items while single-agent generalists do better on mid-range items. Transferred to C2A2: generalist fallback will not degrade uniformly; it will degrade selectively, most likely on the extremes (the most distinctive or most anomalous tradition-specific content) — which is exactly the content the project most wants. Uniform-degradation assumptions understate the harm.
    3. ICLR 2026 blogpost, "Ready For General Agents? Let's Test It." — Reported honestly because it cuts partly *for* the presumption: small general agents achieve 70–95% of specialist performance in scientific and software settings (Mini SWE-Agent 65% vs specialised SWE-Agent 67%, at 30× smaller and ~7× cheaper). The generalist fallback is therefore viable — but the residual 5–30% gap is precisely the quality penalty the presumption assumes away, and it is not negligible when the fallback is used repeatedly.
    4. 2026 agent orchestration reliability literature (Augment Code build-vs-buy; ranksquire orchestration blueprint; Knowlee 2026 orchestration guide). — The orchestration layer's handling of task decomposition, state persistence and failure recovery separates the ~11% of agent systems reaching production from the 89% that do not; static orchestration breaks when situations fall outside programmed rules. A fallback path that has not been exercised and measured is an assumption, not a control.
    5. Cemri et al. MAST taxonomy. — ~42% of multi-agent failures are specification/system-design failures, explicitly including *ambiguous role definitions* and *duplicate agent roles*. A rotation in which the orchestrator silently absorbs non-rotating specialists' roles is a textbook ambiguous-role configuration.
    6. Cycle-1 baseline retained: fixed rotation with overflow produces systematic under-representation of non-rotating items (scheduling fairness, Baker 2004); 12 slots over 6 days for 11+ traditions provides no redundancy for failure or overrun (Kleinrock 1975 queueing under arrival variance); rotation schedules that systematically under-sample produce biased aggregates; empirical coverage studies show specialist under-representation correlates with quality drops for under-sampled categories.
    7. Interaction with ASSUMPTION-026 sources (CHI 2026; arXiv:2601.22812). — Persona instability means specialist agents are already imperfectly distinct from the generalist; if traditions drift toward the base model's generic voice, the measured "fallback penalty" would be artificially small for the wrong reason — the fallback looks adequate because the specialists were never that different. This is a confound that must be controlled before any test result is trusted.

  Evidence trajectory (challenging): stable

  Summary: This remains the weakest-evidenced item in the batch and the assessment is unchanged in strength since April. The core structural objection stands and requires no literature: 12 rotation slots over 6 days for 11+ traditions provides no redundancy, so any single failure, overrun or schedule slip is absorbed by the generalist orchestrator, and there is no mechanism recording when this happened or measuring what was lost. The 2026 literature supplies a real but moderate average fallback penalty (5–30%) and one genuinely new and concerning finding: the penalty is concentrated at the distribution's extremes rather than spread evenly, so fallback preferentially damages the most distinctive tradition-specific content. Confidence is limited by the absence of any literature on rotation scheduling of LLM specialists specifically; this is transfer reasoning, not direct evidence, and should be labelled as such.

  Specific risks: If false, coverage is silently uneven: some traditions receive specialist treatment and others receive generalist paraphrase, with no record distinguishing them. Downstream aggregates — cross-tradition comparisons, the wiki's tradition-balance claims, the narration tracks — are then computed over a non-uniformly-sourced corpus, producing biased conclusions about which traditions are rich, connected or productive when the real driver is which ones happened to get a specialist. Because the fallback is invisible in the output, this bias is undetectable after the fact unless provenance is logged now.

  Mitigations available: (a) Log provenance — record for every artefact whether it came from a specialist or the orchestrator fallback; this is trivially cheap and makes the entire problem measurable; (b) add redundancy — 14 slots for 11 traditions rather than 12, or a catch-up day; (c) measure the fallback penalty directly on a held-out sample rather than assuming 15–30%; (d) rotate the rotation order across cycles so the same traditions are not systematically last; (e) route the extremes to specialists preferentially, per arXiv:2601.22386, since that is where the penalty concentrates; (f) resolve the role ambiguity explicitly (MAST) — define what the orchestrator is and is not authorised to produce in a specialist's absence.

  STEELMAN:
    Strongest counterargument: The measured generalist penalty is smaller than the project assumed — 70–95% of specialist performance, and in the SWE case 65% vs 67%, a two-point gap at 30× lower cost. If the fallback recovers most of the value at a fraction of the cost, then a rotation with generalist backstop is not a compromise but close to the efficient frontier, and adding redundancy would buy little for real cost. The rotation also has a benefit the challenge ignores: it forces breadth. A schedule that guarantees every tradition gets attention within six days is strictly better than an unscheduled process where attention follows the operator's current interest, which is the realistic alternative and is far more biased than any rotation.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (1) Fallback invocations are logged, so coverage can be audited rather than assumed — this single condition converts the presumption from unverifiable to testable and should be implemented regardless of everything else; (2) the fallback penalty is measured on this project's actual task, not imported from code-agent benchmarks; (3) specialists are genuinely distinct from the generalist (see ASSUMPTION-026 — currently unverified, and if false this presumption is trivially true for the wrong reason); (4) downstream aggregates either control for source or are not computed across traditions with differing provenance.
    How to test: Paired-output test. For three traditions, generate the same day's output twice — once with the specialist, once with the orchestrator fallback. Have a blinded rater score both for tradition-specific depth and for content the other missed. This directly measures the penalty on the real task. Run it alongside the ASSUMPTION-026 blind distinctiveness test, since the two share the same instrument and the second controls the confound in the first. Half a day for both.
