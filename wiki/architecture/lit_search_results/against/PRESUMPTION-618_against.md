  Sources:
    1. Walters, W.H. & Wilder, E.I., 2023. "Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT." Scientific Reports 13, s41598-023-41032-5. — 55% of GPT-3.5 citations were fabricated versus 18% of GPT-4 citations. The base rate this control is calibrated against moves by a factor of three between models, which undercuts any single fixed floor: a floor set for one generator is either wasteful or inadequate for another.
    2. Chelli, M. et al., 2024. "Hallucination Rates and Reference Accuracy of ChatGPT and Bard for Systematic Reviews: Comparative Analysis." Journal of Medical Internet Research 26:e53164. — Hallucination rates of 39.6% (GPT-3.5), 28.6% (GPT-4) and 91.4% (Bard). Same conclusion, wider spread: the risk is generator- and task-conditional, i.e. exactly the kind of quantity a floating, risk-weighted rate is designed for.
    3. Royal Society, 2020. "Quotation errors in general science journals." Proceedings of the Royal Society A 476(2242):20200538. — 25% total quotation error rate in peer-reviewed general science journals, where every reference verifiably exists. A floor on existence-verification cannot touch this class of error at all.
    4. Jergas, H. & Baethge, C., 2015. "Quotation accuracy in medical journal articles — a systematic review and meta-analysis." PeerJ (PMC4627914). — Major, minor and total quotation error rates of 11.9%, 11.5% and 25.4%; 64.8% of content errors were major, i.e. the cited source fails to substantiate, is unrelated to, or contradicts the assertion. The residual risk after a verification floor is met is concentrated here.
    5. Mogull, S.A., 2017. "Accuracy of cited 'facts' in medical research articles: A review of study methodology and recalculation of quotation error rate." PLOS ONE 12(9):e0184727. — Confirms that these error rates persist *after* editorial and peer review, i.e. after a de facto 100% verification pass by trained humans. Verification passes do not converge on zero.
    6. Quality Magazine, "The 100% QA Myth: The More You Review, The Less You Catch"; accendoreliability, "100% Inspection" (engineering practice literature). — Human 100% visual inspection is empirically only ~80-85% effective, missing 10-20% of defects through fatigue and monotony; error rates rise 3-10x under time pressure. A 100% verification floor does not deliver 100% assurance and can induce false confidence in a rate that was never achievable.
    7. Ewald, H. et al., 2022 / Bramer, W.M. et al., 2017 (see PRESUMPTION-628 file for full citations). — Context: the same literature that sets high recall targets does so with explicitly *conditional* requirements (which databases, for which topic), not a single universal floor.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate

  Summary: The fabrication base rates strongly justify *some* mandatory verification — that part of the claim is not challenged here. What is challenged is the design choice of a fixed floor and the inference that meeting it reduces downstream error. Fabrication rates vary from 18% to 91% across generators and tasks, so a single floor is miscalibrated for most of its operating range. More seriously, the floor almost certainly measures existence-verification, while the empirically dominant and most persistent error class is substantiation failure: a 25% quotation error rate that survives full peer review, of which nearly two thirds are major. Human 100% inspection is only 80-85% effective, so a satisfied floor is not the assurance it reads as. No located study demonstrates that a verification-*rate* floor reduces downstream error; the evidence supports risk-weighted verification targeted at load-bearing claims.

  Specific risks: The floor becomes the target (Goodhart) and verification effort concentrates on cheap existence checks that raise the numerator, while the expensive substantiation checks — where the residual 25% error lives — are skipped. Reported verification coverage then rises while actual evidential quality is flat or falling. A second risk is false confidence: "100% verified" is read as "0% error" when the achievable ceiling for human checking is 80-85%. A third is misallocation: a uniform floor spends the same effort on a decorative citation and on the one reference a structural claim rests on.

  Mitigations available: (a) Split the metric — report existence-verified and substantiation-verified separately, and never aggregate them; (b) make the rate floating and risk-weighted: 100% for load-bearing citations, sampled for the rest, with the sample rate set from the measured fabrication base rate of the generating channel; (c) re-measure the base rate per generator/model rather than assuming it; (d) publish an assurance level, not a coverage percentage — i.e. state the estimated residual error given inspection effectiveness of ~0.85, not the fraction checked; (e) require the verifier to be a different channel from the producer (see PRESUMPTION-617).

  Search scope: Comprehensive for AI citation fabrication base rates and for the quotation/citation-accuracy literature. Preliminary for the specific question of whether verification-rate floors reduce downstream error — no direct experimental evidence on verification-rate floors as a control was located, in either direction. That absence is itself a finding: the floor is an untested design.

  STEELMAN:
    Strongest counterargument: A floating rate is a rate the system sets for itself, and the whole problem class here is a system grading its own homework. A floor is a bright line that cannot be negotiated downward under deadline pressure, which is precisely when verification collapses. The fabricated-reference base rates — roughly 1 in 5 AI-generated references untraceable, and a sixfold rise in fabricated citations in the published record between 2023 and 2025 — describe a risk severe enough that the crude control is the right control. Objecting that the floor does not catch quotation errors is an argument for adding a second control, not for removing the first. And a floor's failure mode (wasted effort) is far cheaper than a floating rate's failure mode (a rate that drifts to zero).
    What would need to be true for the system to be safe: (i) the floor covers substantiation, not just existence; (ii) the floor is set from a measured, current base rate for the actual generating channel; (iii) the reported coverage figure is never read as an assurance figure; (iv) load-bearing citations are at 100% regardless of what the aggregate floor is.
    How to test: Draw a random sample of citations already recorded as verified. Independently re-check each for (a) existence and (b) whether the source substantiates the assertion it is attached to. The gap between the recorded verification rate and the measured substantiation rate is the size of the control's blind spot; run the same sample against a risk-weighted policy to compare error caught per unit of effort.
