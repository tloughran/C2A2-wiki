**Polarity note.** The claim-to-test is worded as the defective belief. The AGAINST direction challenges 14b's residual position, which I read as two joined propositions: **(i) treating schedule position as an environment fact is an error, and (ii) the remedy is to record the dependency graph the cron times implicitly encode.** I found essentially no challenge to (i) — the orchestration literature agrees that implicit time-encoded dependencies are a defect. I found a substantial challenge to (ii): the established practice is not to *document* the implicit graph but to *dissolve* it, and documenting it is a recognised weaker option. So this file's challenge lands on the remedy, not the diagnosis, and the reconciliation step should not read PARTIALLY-CHALLENGED as doubt about the observation.

### Challenging evidence found: Yes (partial — against the implied remedy, not the diagnosis)

### Sources

1. **Apache Airflow documentation — "Asset-Aware Scheduling" (Airflow 3.x) and "Airflow Datasets" (Astronomer Learn, Airflow 2.4+).** [verified this run via airflow.apache.org stable documentation and Astronomer docs] — The direct challenge to the implied remedy. Asset-based scheduling "expands orchestration capabilities beyond time-based methods such as cron": DAGs are triggered by *updates to an asset* rather than by a clock, and the documentation states the point explicitly — DAGs that access the same data acquire "explicit, visible relationships" so that dependencies are **made explicit rather than implicit**. The established answer to a time-encoded dependency graph is to replace the trigger, not to write the graph down beside the crontab.
2. **Dagster documentation — "Asset freshness policies."** [verified this run via docs.dagster.io] — The complementary primitive. Freshness policies are declared on assets, and upstream nodes are triggered to deliver data to a target node on time; time-window and cron freshness policies are the supported forms. This converts "how stale is what I just read" from an unrecorded property of schedule position into a **declared, checkable contract on the artefact itself**. It is the mechanism by which "what I can truthfully report" stops depending on where in the day I run.
3. **Astronomer Cosmos / dbt-Airflow integration documentation — emission of Airflow Datasets from dbt runs; dbt source-freshness snapshotting.** [verified this run via astronomer.github.io/astronomer-cosmos and dbt/Paradime documentation] — Evidence that the asset-triggered pattern is not aspirational: an entire mainstream toolchain emits dataset events so downstream work is data-aware rather than clock-aware, and `source freshness` is a first-class, scheduled, recorded check.

### Strength of challenge: Moderate

### Summary

The observation is well founded and I found no literature defending implicit time-encoded dependencies — the orchestration field treats them as a defect and has built two generations of tooling to eliminate them. The challenge is to the remedy the item's search direction gestures at. Asking whether periodic-batch systems have "an established practice for recording the dependency graph their cron times encode" presupposes that the graph should be recorded; the established practice is instead to stop encoding it in the clock. Airflow's asset-aware scheduling and Dagster's asset freshness policies both exist for exactly this reason, and Airflow's own documentation states the goal as making dependencies explicit rather than implicit by triggering on asset updates. The operational corollary is equally standard: wrap sources in reschedule-mode sensors with timeouts and alerting callbacks so that a late upstream pages someone rather than hanging silently — silent staleness is a named failure with a standard countermeasure that is a control primitive, not a document. A recorded graph is also under-inclusive: at least one common way schedule position becomes load-bearing is resource contention among DAGs scheduled at the same minute, which no dependency record addresses. Finally, and most cheaply, the three cases may be repairable without touching the schedule at all: a run reporting "nothing new" is telling the truth about its own execution instant, and what is missing is the as-of stamp that would let a later reader distinguish a measurement from a scheduling artefact. That reframing is the highest-value item here and it is also the least well sourced in this file.

### Specific risks

If the implied remedy is adopted, the fleet builds a document describing which runs depend on which others by virtue of their cron times. That document is off the execution path by construction — nothing enforces it — and it will drift the first time a schedule changes, at which point the fleet holds a dependency record that is confidently wrong. This is the same class of hazard as PRESUMPTION-795's register: an artefact that records an obligation without executing it. It is also under-inclusive against contention-induced staleness.

If the item is dismissed, the concrete hazard is precisely what the item identified and it is not small for a fleet whose outputs are read as evidence: assertions whose truth is a function of execution time, recorded without execution time, and consumed later as if they were measurements. 14b's own framing — "which of the fleet's daily assertions are true by construction and which by measurement" — is the right question, and nothing currently answers it.

A further risk specific to this fleet: PRESUMPTION-787 reports seven runs concluding their assigned work was exhausted, and PRESUMPTION-790 reports four DB sizings and seven queue counts in one day that were never reconciled. Both are candidate instances of exactly this mechanism — a reading whose value depends on when it was taken. If schedule position is load-bearing across these items, the disagreements 790 records may be temporal rather than instrumental, which changes what 790's remedy should be.

### Mitigations available

(a) **Stamp every assertion with its as-of time** and, where applicable, the as-of time of the newest input it read. This is one field, it is on the execution path, it survives schedule changes, and it converts "true by construction" versus "true by measurement" from an unanswerable question into a comparison of two timestamps. Highest value per unit cost of anything in this file.
(b) **Replace clock triggers with artefact triggers** where a run genuinely depends on another's output — the asset-aware pattern. This dissolves the implicit graph instead of recording it, and it is the field's established answer.
(c) **Declare freshness contracts on the artefacts that matter** rather than on the runs, so that staleness is a property with a stated tolerance that can be violated loudly.
(d) **Make waiting explicit**: a run that requires an upstream product should wait with a timeout and escalate, not read whatever is present and report on it. This is the reschedule-mode sensor pattern and it is the direct fix for "a run's position determined what it could truthfully report."
(e) Only after (a)–(d), if a dependency record is still wanted, generate it *from* the declared triggers rather than writing it by hand — a hand-written record of an implicit graph is the option the tooling was built to replace.

### Recommendation: PARTIALLY-CHALLENGED

## STEELMAN

**Item:** PRESUMPTION-797

**Strongest counterargument:** The diagnosis is right and the remedy is a generation behind. The orchestration field agrees entirely that dependencies encoded in cron times are a defect — but its answer is not to document the encoded graph, it is to stop encoding it. Airflow's asset-aware scheduling exists to move triggers from the clock to the data, and its documentation says so in as many words: DAGs that touch the same data get explicit, visible relationships instead of implicit ones. Dagster's freshness policies do the same from the other direction, attaching a declared staleness tolerance to the artefact rather than leaving staleness as an unrecorded consequence of who ran first. The operational rule is standard and blunt: wrap each source in a reschedule-mode sensor with a timeout and an alerting callback so a late upstream pages someone instead of hanging silently. Against that, a written record of which cron times imply which dependencies is an artefact with no enforcement, guaranteed to drift on the first schedule change, and blind to the contention case where forty-seven DAGs at midnight starve a thirty-two-slot pool — schedule position was load-bearing there too and no dependency graph would have said so. There is also a cheaper reading of all three cases that the item skips past. A run that reports "nothing new" at 03:00 is not reporting a falsehood and is not wrong to treat its execution instant as an environment fact; it is making a true statement indexed to a time. What is missing is the index. Stamp every assertion with its as-of time and the as-of time of its newest input, and the fleet can answer 14b's actual question — which daily assertions are true by construction and which by measurement — for one field per assertion, with no schedule change, no new document, and nothing to drift.

**What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe:** Either every run that depends on another's product must be triggered by that product rather than by a clock, with a declared freshness tolerance and an explicit wait-and-escalate on absence; or, at minimum, every assertion the fleet publishes must carry its as-of time and the as-of time of its newest input, so that schedule-induced staleness is visible to any reader downstream. With neither, the item's hazard is fully live. With a hand-written dependency record and neither, the hazard is live *and* covered by an artefact that will read as if it were addressed.

**How to test:** Take the three same-day cases and re-run each at a different position in the schedule — earliest slot and latest slot — and compare what each reports. If the reports differ, schedule position is load-bearing and demonstrably so, with the size of the effect measured rather than inferred. That is the experiment the item's observation deserves and it costs two runs. Second test, for the remedy question: check whether any of the three runs' outputs currently carries an as-of timestamp for its newest input. If none do, the cheap fix is available and the expensive one is unnecessary. Third, and it connects this item to two others in the same intake: check whether PRESUMPTION-790's four disagreeing DB sizings and seven queue counts were taken at different times of day. If they were, that disagreement may be temporal rather than instrumental and 790's remedy changes.
