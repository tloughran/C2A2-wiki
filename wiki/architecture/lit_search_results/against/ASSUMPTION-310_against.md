  Sources:
    1. Strawson, G., 2004. "Against Narrativity." Ratio 17(4): 428–452. — Strawson argues directly against both the psychological claim that humans typically experience themselves narratively and the normative claim that narrative is necessary for authentic selfhood, proposing instead an "episodic" self that individuates without diachronic narrative. If the episodic self is real and coherent, narrative cannot be the sole principle of individuation.

    2. Lamarque, P., 2004. "On Not Expecting Too Much from Narrative." Mind & Language 19(4): 393–408. — Lamarque argues that personal narratives are never as neat as their literary paradigms and that the model illegitimately borrows the coherence-conferring properties of fiction. Narrative, being a representational form rather than a constitutive one, cannot bear the ontological weight of serving as the uniquely necessary individuation principle.

    3. Chalmers, D., 2010. "Idealism and the Mind-Body Problem." In W. Seager (ed.), The Routledge Handbook of Panpsychism. — Discusses the decombination/individuation problem for monist idealist views. Any position holding that differentiated perspectives derive from a single consciousness (as CRm does) must explain how individuation occurs; narrative is one candidate, but the problem places a heavy explanatory burden on whatever mechanism is invoked.

    4. Millikan, R. G., 2000. On Clear and Confused Ideas. Cambridge University Press. — Argues that individuation of intentional objects requires causal-historical anchoring, not narrative coherence. Individuation grounded purely in the internal structure of narratives is subject to duplication arguments: two numerically distinct agents could share identical narratives, yet this account provides no further fact to distinguish them.

    5. Olson, E., 2007. What Are We? A Study in Personal Ontology. Oxford University Press. — Defends animalism: persons are individuated by biological-organismic continuity, not psychological or narrative continuity. On this view narrative is epiphenomenal to individuation.

  Strength of challenge: Strong

  Summary: Strawson's empirical-psychological challenge demonstrates that many persons experience themselves episodically rather than narratively, undermining the claim that narrative is the universal principle of individuation. Lamarque's representationalist critique further weakens the ontological move from narrative structure to individuation fact. The decombination problem in cosmopsychism/analytic idealism shows that any monist view needs a far more specified account of how unity de-combines into distinct perspectives — the bare assertion that "narrative does this" defers rather than solves the problem. Finally, animalism and haecceity-based accounts provide coherent alternative individuation criteria that require no narrative at all. The claim that there is "nothing else" doing individuation is thus multiply contested.

  Specific risks: If narrative-as-individuation fails, the CRm architecture loses its account of what distinguishes one agent-perspective from another, threatening both the identity of the AI agents instantiating traditions and the coherence of the human-AI dyad concept. The M7 milestone may be grounded in an undefended strong metaphysical assumption.

  Mitigations available: CRm could retreat to the weaker claim that narrative is a sufficient but not uniquely necessary individuation principle, preserving the theoretical role without the exclusivity claim ("nothing else"). The "strongest" reading could be reserved as a programmatic conjecture under investigation rather than an operative assumption.

  STEELMAN:
    Strongest counterargument: Strawson's episodic individuals still have a public identity and a social history — even he concedes this. One response in CRm's favour is that narrative operates at the level of the distinction of perspectives-within-consciousness (the metaphysical question), not at the level of empirical psychology, so whether a person consciously tells themselves a story is orthogonal to whether narrative structure is what ontologically differentiates one perspective-centre from another within a monist frame. The claim may be best read as a structural/formal claim about CRm's ontology, not a claim about how agents subjectively experience time.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: The CRm notion of "narrative" would need to be distinguished sharply from narrative-as-empirical-psychology (what Strawson attacks) and shown to be doing genuine individuation work at a formal ontological level, with some account of why it cannot be reduced to or replaced by spatiotemporal or haecceitistic individuation.
    How to test: Construct two CRm-internal agent-perspectives that are stipulated to share identical "narratives" but arise from different instantiation events; ask whether CRm's ontology can still distinguish them. If yes, narrative is not load-bearing; if no, this confirms the role but also exposes the duplication vulnerability.
