### Challenging evidence found: Partial

### Sources

1. **Pauker, S. G. & Kassirer, J. P., 1980. "The Threshold Approach to Clinical Decision Making." *New England Journal of Medicine* 302(20):1109–1117.** [verified this run — title, authors, journal, year and NEJM DOI confirmed; abstract and multiple secondary summaries read, full text not read.] — The direct answer to "when does an explanation stop needing to be tested," and it is forty-six years old. Two thresholds are derived from the test's reliability and risks and the action's benefits and risks: below the *testing threshold* neither test nor act; above the *test-treatment threshold* act without further testing; between them, test. The framework's substantive content is that at sufficiently high posterior probability **further testing has negative expected value** — testing is not free, and demanding it unconditionally is itself an error. A cold-cache explanation for a universal `fidelity: fail` is a high-prior, single-cause account of a fully-correlated symptom; that is exactly the region where the threshold model says to act.
2. **Norman, G., Monteiro, S., Sherbino, J., Ilgen, J., Schmidt, H. & Mamede, S., 2017. "The Causes of Errors in Clinical Reasoning: Cognitive Biases, Knowledge Deficits, and Dual Process Thinking." *Academic Medicine* 92(1):23–30.** [verified this run — full citation confirmed across SciRP, Semantic Scholar, Oxford Academic and AHRQ PSNet; abstract read, full text not read.] — The challenge to part (ii), and it is blunt. Reviewing the medical literature on two questions — whether errors originate in intuitive or analytic processing, and whether they stem from bias or from knowledge deficits — the authors conclude that although biases such as availability can be induced experimentally, "the extent to which these biases actually contribute to diagnostic errors is not well established"; that "interventions directed at error reduction through the identification of heuristics and biases have no effect on diagnostic errors"; and that most errors instead "originate from a limited knowledge base." If that transfers, then diagnosing C2A2's cold-cache attribution as a confirmation-bias problem both misidentifies the mechanism and points at a remedy class with a null empirical record.
3. **Klein, G., Calderwood, R. & Clinton-Cirocco, A. — the Recognition-Primed Decision model (originally 1986; canonical presentation Klein, G., 1998, *Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions*, MIT Press).** [canonical, not re-verified — the model, its originators and its satisficing structure were confirmed this run through secondary sources including the Wikipedia entry, ShadowBox Training's primer and Mindtools; no primary source read, and the specific accuracy statistics I would want were **not** located.] — Supports the same direction from the descriptive side. Experts in time-pressured domains do not generate and compare options; they recognise a situation as a familiar pattern, mentally simulate the first workable response, and act, and "in most cases the expert recognizes a situation as a familiar one and immediately engages in appropriate actions." Naming an explanation and proceeding is therefore the documented signature of expertise, not of laziness — though note that RPD's protective element is the *expectancy check*: the decision-maker commits only "if subsequent expectancies are confirmed," which is a test, and a specific one.
4. **Parsimonious set-covering / abductive diagnosis; Occam's razor versus Hickam's dictum in multiple-fault diagnosis.** [verified this run via secondary sources — "Abductive Reasoning in Multiple Fault Diagnosis" and Markov-logic root-cause work (arXiv:1511.05719) located; the primary Reggia–Peng set-covering literature not read.] — Formalises why a single high-coverage explanation is rationally privileged: hypotheses are weighted jointly by parsimony (number of causes) and explanatory coverage (number of symptoms explained), and a single cause that covers a *universal* symptom scores extremely well on both axes. **Honest counter-note from the same sources:** this literature also carries the warning that multiple conditions frequently converge to produce one visible failure and that the single-fault assumption does not always hold — which is 14b's point, stated by the field I am citing against it.

### Strength of challenge: Moderate

### Summary

Two established literatures cut against the item as stated, and neither reaches the defect it actually describes. Against the epistemics: Pauker and Kassirer's threshold model has held since 1980 that "test further" is a decision with a computable answer, and that above a threshold determined by test cost and action risk, further testing has negative expected value — so an explanation ceasing to be tested is not per se a failure, and a system that tested every named explanation indefinitely would be making a different, quantifiable error. Abductive diagnosis reinforces this by explaining why a single cause covering a universal symptom is rationally privileged on both parsimony and coverage. Against the mechanism: the strongest recent synthesis in the diagnostic-error literature finds that cognitive-bias accounts are not well supported as causes of real diagnostic error and that bias-recognition interventions have *no measured effect*, locating errors instead in knowledge deficits — which means that framing this item as confirmation bias risks prescribing a remedy class with a null record. But the item's own facts are narrower and survive both challenges. What 14b reports is not six runs that stopped testing; it is four runs that "confirmed only on cases where the explanation predicts what they found." That is a selection problem, not a stopping problem, and neither the threshold model nor RPD licenses it — the threshold model assumes tests are informative, and RPD's protective mechanism is precisely an expectancy check, which by construction must be capable of failing. A confirmation restricted to cases the hypothesis predicts is an expectancy check with the disconfirming cases removed, and it raises the posterior by construction rather than by evidence. The literature therefore challenges the framing but underwrites the finding.

### Specific risks

If 14b's claim is right in its strong form and the cold-cache account is partly wrong, the exposure is the one the item names: the transcript corpus is unchecked for drift while six runs a day record `fidelity: fail` as a known artifact, meaning a real drift signal is being absorbed into a benign explanation at a rate of six recordings per day. That is a classic explaining-away failure and its cost compounds. If the claim is adopted in its strong form and generalised — no explanation may be relied on until tested — the counter-risk is quantifiable rather than rhetorical: the threshold model says the fleet would be paying for tests with negative expected value, and, as PRESUMPTION-786's search independently found, unaffordable universal controls degrade into controls recorded as performed and not executed. The subtler risk sits between the two: if the item is remediated as a *bias* problem — by asking runs to be more careful, to consider alternatives, to slow down — the Norman et al. synthesis predicts that remedy will have no effect, and the fleet will have spent its remediation budget on the intervention class with the weakest evidence base while the actual selection defect goes untouched.

### Mitigations available

(a) **Require one disconfirming case, not more confirming ones.** The entire defect is asymmetric case selection, and the fix is a single case the cold-cache account predicts should *pass* — a warm-cache run of the same fixture. If it fails, the explanation is wrong, and one observation settles it. This is cheap, decisive, and does not require testing the explanation continuously. (b) **State the threshold explicitly.** Following Pauker and Kassirer, record the posterior at which the fleet will stop testing an attribution and act on it, and record the test's cost; this converts an unstated presumption into a stated and reviewable parameter. (c) **Adopt the RPD expectancy check as the standing form:** when an explanation is named, name simultaneously what would be observed if it were *false*, and check that. The naming and the falsifier cost the same keystrokes. (d) **Do not prescribe debiasing.** On the strongest available synthesis, bias-recognition interventions have no measured effect on diagnostic error; the effective lever in that literature is knowledge and structured checking, not exhortation. (e) **Separate the count.** Six daily recordings of "known artifact" should be a monitored quantity in its own right — a rising count of symptoms absorbed into a standing explanation is exactly the observable that distinguishes a correct attribution from a growing blind spot, and it requires no new tests at all.

### Recommendation: PARTIALLY-CHALLENGED

## STEELMAN

**Item:** PRESUMPTION-789

**Strongest counterargument:** The demand that a named explanation keep being tested has no natural stopping point, and the field that has thought hardest about this settled it in 1980: Pauker and Kassirer showed that testing has a computable threshold, that above it further testing carries negative expected value, and that the error of over-testing is as real and as costly as the error of under-testing. A cold cache producing a universal `fidelity: fail` is a single cause with total explanatory coverage and a high prior — on parsimony-and-coverage grounds it is the best-scoring hypothesis available, and abductive diagnosis privileges it for good formal reasons. Expert practice agrees: the recognition-primed model, built from observations of fireground commanders and other experts under time pressure, finds that skilled people recognise a pattern and act on the first workable account rather than enumerating alternatives, and that they are usually right. Worse for the item's framing, the mechanism it implicitly blames is one the evidence does not support. The most careful recent synthesis of diagnostic error concludes that cognitive-bias accounts are not well established as causes of real-world error, that bias-recognition interventions have *no effect* on diagnostic errors, and that errors trace mainly to knowledge deficits. So the item risks a double miss: naming a mechanism the evidence disfavours and prescribing a remedy class with a null record, while the fleet pays for tests the threshold model says it should not run. **Where this stops:** the item's actual observation is not that testing stopped but that four runs "confirmed only on cases where the explanation predicts what they found." No framework cited here licenses that. The threshold model presumes tests are informative; RPD's protective element is an expectancy check that must be able to fail; and the abductive literature warns in the same breath that multiple causes routinely converge on one visible failure. A confirmation drawn only from predicted-positive cases raises the posterior by construction, so the threshold it clears is one it moved itself. The item has found a real defect and mislabelled it: this is asymmetric case selection, and it needs one disconfirming case, not a testing regime.

**What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe:** That at least one case has been checked which the cold-cache account predicts should *pass* — a warm-cache re-run of a failing fixture — and that it passed. Given that single observation, stopping is warranted and the six runs' reliance is sound. Additionally, the count of symptoms absorbed into the standing explanation should be monitored, so that a widening scope of "known artifact" is visible without re-testing.

**How to test:** Run one warm-cache execution of a fixture that produced `fidelity: fail` under cold cache. This is a negative control in the strict sense — a case whose answer the hypothesis predicts and which is capable of contradicting it — and it is a single run. If it fails warm, the cold-cache account is refuted and the corpus-drift exposure 14b fears is real and immediate. If it passes, the attribution is confirmed on a case that could have gone the other way, and the presumption is retired as sound. A second, cheaper measurement: retrieve the four runs' confirmations and classify each case by whether the cold-cache account predicted the observed outcome. If all four are predicted-positive cases with no predicted-negative among them, the selection defect is established as a matter of record and no judgement about anyone's reasoning is required.
