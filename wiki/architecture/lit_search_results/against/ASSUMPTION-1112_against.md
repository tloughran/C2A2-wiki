### Summary

The item's observation is right and its two headline claims are both wrong in the same way — they measure novelty against the two guards C2A2 happens to run rather than against the field or against C2A2's own register. On novelty: Walters & Wilder measured this exact class in 2023 across 636 generated citations and found that among **non-fabricated** citations, **43% (GPT-3.5) and 24% (GPT-4)** carried substantive metadata errors — wrong volume, issue, page numbers. Real source, wrong metadata is not a new class; subtract whole-source fabrication and it is the *dominant* one. Bibliometrics is older still: the field's major/minor taxonomy names dates explicitly — "errors in the dates of publication, journal volumes, titles and page numbers... can prevent papers from being retrievable" — and has reported reference-error rates of 25–50% for decades, entirely without LLMs. On "no URL-resolves check would have stopped it": this is true of a check that resolves a URL and discards the response, and false of URL resolution as a technique. The whole remedy programme launched by Klein et al.'s reference-rot study — Memento, Perma.cc, robust links — works by binding a citation to an **archival snapshot with an observation datetime**, so it records the date as a matter of construction; and for any DOI-bearing work, Crossref's free public API returns the publication date on a single GET. The strongest challenge, though, is in-house. **PREMISE-132** already holds that citing is not verifying, that a string-counting audit "establishes nothing about the property it claims to audit," and — load-bearing — that the requirement is **decorrelation, not externality**, because a differently-prompted instance detects hallucinated and misattributed references without external resources and is "available in-house today." **PREMISE-148** already requires provenance to be a structured machine-readable field on every citation; **PREMISE-142** already requires every claim to carry an evidence pointer with a timestamp. Any one of those, applied, catches an invented date. The item's novelty is therefore novelty relative to an unchecked register, which is the exact defect REVISE-340 flagged for this cohort. Finally, "invented" is doing unearned work. Live web resources routinely carry several legitimate dates, and I verified this directly on the vault's own subject matter: the Franciscan Media page carrying Rohr's Scotus material declares `published_time` 2023-01-01 and `modified_time` 2026-04-12, and its content is adapted from a book with 2014 and 2024 editions — four defensible dates, none invented. PREMISE-101's "(scope, method, time) tuple" is the general statement of the same thing. What does survive, and deserves the item's alarm: PREMISE-148's clause (5) narrowed the register's citation worry to *numeric and inferential* content and explicitly allowed that secondary sources "may be perfectly adequate for **identity-level facts** — who, what." A publication date is an identity-level fact. So 1112 is a failure inside the class the register had judged safest, and *that* — not novelty — is the finding worth recording.

### Specific risks

**The false-novelty risk, and it is the main one.** If 1112 is filed as a new class, C2A2 designs a bespoke guard for a problem with three years of published detection work behind it (Walters & Wilder's taxonomy; the 2024–2026 reference-hallucination detection benchmarks; CheckIfExist and CiteAudit, both already logged in this register). The cost is not just duplicated effort — it is that the bespoke guard will be calibrated against one observed instance rather than against measured field rates of 24–43%.

**The wrong-remedy risk.** "No URL-resolves check would have stopped it" points the fleet away from URL resolution when the correct move is to *upgrade* it: resolve to an archival snapshot and record the observation datetime, and resolve DOIs against Crossref and compare the returned date. Both are cheap. Concluding that resolution is inadequate risks replacing a nearly-sufficient guard with an expensive one.

**The over-triage risk from clause (D).** If any date divergence is classified as fabrication, the fleet will generate false accusations at a high rate against exactly the source type it cites most — CAC daily meditations, blog posts, republished book extracts — all of which carry multiple legitimate dates. The Franciscan Media page is a worked example. False fabrication findings are expensive: they consume the credibility of true ones.

**The register-bypass risk, which is systemic rather than local.** Four ACTIVE premises bear on this item and none was consulted before it was filed, because the intake gate does not require it (OPEN-153). This is the mechanism, not the instance: an item can be graded High for novelty while its remedy sits minted in the register.

**Risk of dismissing the item, at full strength.** The exposure is real and measured: roughly one in four GPT-4-class citations that survive an existence check still carries a substantive metadata error. And PREMISE-148 clause (5) had explicitly reasoned that identity-level facts were the safe class — this instance is a counterexample to an ACTIVE premise's own narrowing clause, which is a genuine and consequential finding. Dismissing 1112 because it is not novel would discard that.

### Mitigations available

(a) **Upgrade the URL-resolves check rather than replacing it.** Resolve to an archival snapshot (Memento/Perma-style) and store the observation datetime alongside the citation. This is the reference-rot literature's standard remedy and it makes the date checkable by construction.
(b) **Add a Crossref/DOI metadata match for any DOI-bearing source** — one free GET, comparing the cited date, title and first author against the registry. Log a mismatch as NO-EVIDENCE (PREMISE-142's third value), not as a fabrication finding.
(c) **Apply PREMISE-132's decorrelated check to metadata fields, not just to whole references.** The register already holds that a differently-prompted instance catches misattributed references without external resources; extend the query from "does this source exist" to "what is this source's date, asked cold."
(d) **Adopt a multi-date schema for web sources**, per PREMISE-101: record `published`, `modified`, `accessed`, and `edition` separately. A mismatch against one field is not a mismatch against the source. My Franciscan Media observation is the test case.
(e) **Reserve "fabrication" for cases where no defensible date exists.** Introduce a distinct, lower-severity class — *metadata divergence* — and escalate to fabrication only when the cited date matches none of the source's declared dates and no edition explains it.
(f) **Amend PREMISE-148 clause (5) rather than minting a new premise.** Its narrowing says secondary sources may be adequate for identity-level facts; this instance is a counterexample. The honest move is a scope note on 148 — identity-level facts are adequate for *who* and *what*, and **not** for *when* — not a fifth citation premise standing beside four existing ones. PREMISE-138 bars re-minting.

## STEELMAN

**Item:** ASSUMPTION-1112

**Strongest counterargument:** Everything the item reports is real; everything it claims about the report is not. Take the novelty claim first, because it is the load-bearing one. Walters & Wilder ran 42 topics through GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, collected 636 citations, and separated two failure modes: whole-source fabrication (55% and 18%) and — the item's "new" class — **real sources carrying substantive errors, at 43% and 24% of the non-fabricated remainder**, with wrong volume, issue and page numbers given as the exemplars. That was published in *Scientific Reports* in September 2023. Subtract invented sources and this is not a new class; it is the *majority* of what is left. Bibliometrics had it long before that: the field's severity taxonomy is drawn at retrievability, and its canonical statement is that "errors in the dates of publication, journal volumes, titles and page numbers all individually and in combination can prevent papers from being retrievable," with reference-error rates reported at 25–50% across decades of studies. Now the second claim: "no URL-resolves check would have stopped it." That is a statement about the check C2A2 built, not about the technique. Klein et al.'s reference-rot study — one in five STM articles, seven in ten among those citing the web — launched an entire remediation programme (Memento, Perma.cc, robust links) whose whole mechanism is to bind a citation to an **archival snapshot carrying an observation datetime**. Such a check records the date as a structural consequence of resolving. And for any DOI-bearing source, Crossref's free public API over 150M+ works returns the publication date on a single GET. The right conclusion is not that resolution is inadequate; it is that C2A2's resolver throws away the response. And then the part that should sting, because it is in-house and it is ACTIVE: **PREMISE-132** already holds that "citing is not verifying," that an audit counting citation strings "establishes nothing about the property it claims to audit," and that the requirement is **decorrelation, not externality** — a differently-prompted instance detects hallucinated and *misattributed* references with no external resource, and is "available in-house today," on the authority of Agrawal et al. (2024, *Findings of EACL*). **PREMISE-148** already requires provenance as a structured machine-readable field on every citation. **PREMISE-142** already requires an evidence pointer with a timestamp on every claim. Any one of the three, applied, catches an invented date. So the novelty is novelty against two named guards and an unread register — which is precisely the defect REVISE-340 flagged over this whole cohort. Finally, "invented" presumes a unique right answer. I fetched the Franciscan Media page carrying Rohr's Scotus material today and read its metadata: `published_time` **2023-01-01**, `modified_time` **2026-04-12**, body adapted from *Eager to Love* (2014, 2024 edition). Four defensible dates for one source, none of them a fabrication, and a naive comparator would flag three of them. PREMISE-101 states the general form — a date is a property of a (scope, method, time) reading, not of the artefact. **The thing that does survive, and it is worth more than the novelty claim:** PREMISE-148 clause (5) explicitly narrowed the register's citation concern to numeric and inferential content and allowed that secondary sources "may be perfectly adequate for identity-level facts." A date is an identity-level fact. This instance is a counterexample to an ACTIVE premise's own safe-harbour clause — and that, rather than a new taxonomy, is the finding.

**What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe:** Four conditions, all cheap and none requiring a new class of instrument. (i) **The resolver must keep what it fetches** — an archival snapshot plus observation datetime, so that resolution and date-verification are the same operation rather than two. (ii) **DOI-bearing sources must be metadata-matched against Crossref**, and a mismatch must be reported as NO-EVIDENCE with an evidence pointer (PREMISE-142), not as a pass and not as a fabrication verdict. (iii) **Web sources must carry a multi-date schema** — published, modified, accessed, edition — because otherwise the fleet will generate false fabrication findings against the exact source type it cites most; PREMISE-101 already licenses this and my Franciscan Media reading demonstrates the need. (iv) **PREMISE-132's decorrelated check must be extended from source-existence to metadata fields**, since the register already holds that this catches misattribution and is available today. If those four hold, the exposure is closed with existing components and the item's alarm is fully answered. If instead a bespoke "date fabrication" guard is built against a single observed instance, C2A2 will have a detector calibrated to n=1 against a field rate the literature puts near 24%.

**How to test:** (1) **The retrospective metadata sweep, and it is decisive.** Take every citation in `validated_premises.md` and the tradition wikis that carries a DOI or a resolvable URL, and machine-compare the recorded date against Crossref (for DOIs) and against declared `published_time`/`modified_time` (for web pages). Report the mismatch rate. If it is near zero, the incident was isolated and the Critical framing is over-set; if it is anywhere near the literature's 24%, the fleet has a systemic exposure and the single instance was the tip. Hours of scripted work, and it converts an anecdote into a rate — which PREMISE-168 requires anyway. (2) **The decorrelated-date probe.** Ask a differently-prompted instance for the date of thirty already-cited sources, cold, with no access to the citation. Measure agreement. This directly tests PREMISE-132's second clause on the metadata field rather than the source field, and it is the cheapest possible confirmation that the existing remedy reaches this failure. (3) **The multi-date false-positive check.** Run the proposed date-verification rule over fifty existing web citations and count how many trip on `modified_time` versus `published_time`. That number is the false-accusation rate the fleet would have adopted. (4) **The 148 clause-(5) audit.** Enumerate the identity-level facts the register currently accepts from secondary sources — dates, titles, publishers, editions — and check a sample. If dates fail and titles do not, the amendment to 148 is narrow and precise; if all fail, the safe-harbour clause needs withdrawing rather than narrowing.
