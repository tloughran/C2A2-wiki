### Polarity note — what I am challenging

The item's *defective* belief is "waiting is a form of checking." **14b's corrective — the proposition I searched AGAINST — is: "The variable is the search, not the time"; cadence ticks record nothing about the monitored item; and a schedule built out of cadences (next_check dates, weekly carry-overs, cycle counters, auto-escalation dates) is therefore built on a mechanism shown to record nothing.**

Four clauses:

- **(A) A cadence tick is not an observation.** Searched disconfirmatorily; **found nothing against it,** and nothing could be found — it is true by definition. Recording "cycle 5" when nobody looked is a record about the calendar.
- **(B) "The variable is the search, not the time."** **Challenged, Strong.** The experiment that produced this sentence cannot separate the two variables. It is a single-arm design with time and search perfectly confounded.
- **(C) Therefore the cadence-built schedule is defective.** **Challenged, Strong.** In the one domain C2A2 monitors — external literature — there is **no event channel**, and every practical alternative to a clock requires one. Fixed-interval regimes are the standing, evidence-based norm in the two fields the item's own testability clause names.
- **(D) The zero-yield finding means intervals are meaningless.** **Challenged, and this is the productive reframe.** The empirical literature on re-check intervals reads the same data the opposite way: it says C2A2's interval is set roughly two orders of magnitude too fast, which is a fixable parameter, not a broken mechanism.

### Sources

1. **The identification failure, which is the core of this file.** [internal to the fleet; methodological] — The claim "the variable is the search, not the time" is a causal claim about two variables, derived from a design in which the two are perfectly confounded. The 17 items had accumulated roughly five cycles of *elapsed time* before anyone searched; the search then found new material. That observation is exactly what a *time-driven* model predicts — material accumulated over five cycles and was harvested when someone finally looked. To license the item's conclusion you need the missing arm: **search the same items at cycle 0 and show the same yield.** If a cycle-0 search returns nothing and a cycle-5 search returns 10 of 17, time is the dominant variable and the item's sentence is exactly backwards. Nobody ran that arm; the item calls the design "today's second disjoint draw," but two draws at the same lag are two replications of one condition, not two conditions. **"Falsified twice" is n=2 at a single value of the treatment variable.**
2. **Shojania, K.G. et al. (2007), "How Quickly Do Systematic Reviews Go Out of Date? A Survival Analysis," *Annals of Internal Medicine* 147(4):224–233.** [SNIPPET LEVEL — acpjournals.org abstract and secondary summaries read; **primary NOT fetched**] — The direct empirical answer to "is there a basis for re-check intervals," and it reframes the item's finding. 100 quantitative systematic reviews, survival analysis of time-to-signal-for-updating: **median survival free of a signal 5.5 years (CI 4.6–7.6); a signal within 2 years for 23%; within 1 year for 15%; and already present at publication for 7%.** Three things follow, none of them the item's conclusion. **(i) The interval question has an evidence base and the answer is time-indexed** — the hazard is a function of elapsed time, which is what a survival curve *is*. **(ii) The right interval is measurable per item class** — heterogeneity and topic volatility shifted the hazard by a factor of ~2.7, so the correct design is stratified intervals, not no intervals. **(iii) C2A2's weekly cadence is off by a very large factor.** If the analogous hazard for a wiki monitor item is measured in months, then a weekly tick *should* return nothing almost every time, and "five cycles of waiting resolved nothing" is the expected behaviour of a correctly-functioning but grossly over-frequent monitor, not evidence that cadence records nothing. The 7%-already-at-publication figure also warns that some items are stale on arrival — which is an *intake* defect, not a cadence defect.
3. **Fixed-interval regimes are the standing norm in population screening; risk-stratification is the frontier, not the practice.** [SNIPPET LEVEL — PLOS One review of risk-stratified screening acceptability (journals.plos.org/plosone, pone.0279201); interval-cancer literature via PMC12507088, PMC4204862, PMC9691207; Wiley/IJC 2025 FIT upper-age-limit study] — "**Most cancer screening programmes operate a fixed regime** where eligibility is based on age and/or sex and screening intervals are determined by the screening results, rather than additional individual level risk factors," with risk-stratified approaches discussed as something that "could theoretically achieve" better targeting and studied for acceptability and cost-effectiveness rather than deployed. The field the item's own testability clause nominates as the source of surveillance-interval evidence has spent forty years on fixed intervals, knows about interval cancers (events between rounds, with worse prognosis than screen-detected ones), and has *still* not replaced the clock — because the clock delivers population coverage, staffing predictability, and equity of access that condition-triggered regimes do not.
4. **Condition-based maintenance has documented, enumerated failure conditions — several of which C2A2 meets.** [SNIPPET LEVEL — MaintainX CBM learning-centre guide; Fiix; getmaintainx; gofmx; coastapp; rzsoftware CBM guides] — CBM is the maintenance world's "the variable is the condition, not the time," and the practitioner literature lists exactly when it fails: **"Fatigue or uniform wear failures are not easily detected with CBM measurements"** (no condition signal precedes the event); **high upfront cost** of sensing; **specialised expertise** to read the signal, since "a misread signal means a missed failure or needless work"; **not cost-effective for inexpensive, easily replaceable assets**; and — the one that bites hardest here — **"Unlike a preventive maintenance program, CBM doesn't have a maintenance schedule. The data may indicate a problem at any time, which means there's a lack of certainty on when maintenance needs to be performed."** For a fleet whose binding constraint is human review capacity and one scheduled run per day (PREMISE-095), unpredictable arrival is a real cost, and the standing advice is that fixed-interval preventive maintenance remains appropriate where these conditions hold.
5. **The no-event-channel problem, and it is decisive for clause (C).** [analytical, grounded in source 4 and PREMISE-053] — Every alternative to a clock — condition-based, event-driven, threshold-triggered — requires a **channel that emits an event**. PREMISE-053's own formulation is careful about this: prefer event-triggered regeneration "for **append-driven sources** where feasible." An append-only file emits an append. The objects in C2A2's monitor queue are *claims about the external literature*: no journal, preprint server or author notifies this vault when something relevant appears. There is no event to subscribe to. In that regime the only available trigger for "go and look" is a clock, and a clock is not a claim that time causes change — it is a **coverage mechanism for objects with no event channel**. The item is therefore right that the tick records nothing and wrong that the tick is doing nothing: its job is not to observe, it is to *schedule an observation*. The observed defect is that the scheduled observation did not happen — which is PRESUMPTION-819's mechanism (a step that did not execute reporting as clean), not a defect in scheduling.
6. **PREMISE-095 already holds the operational finding, with the remedy space enumerated.** [internal to the fleet] — "The refresh queue grows without bound absent a cadence change, admission cap, or throughput/provisioning increase." The 151 carry-overs are that premise operating exactly as predicted. The item reports them as new evidence of a conceptual error; the register has them as a known queueing result with three named levers, none of which is "abolish cadence." I record this as a challenge because the framing difference changes what gets built: a queueing problem gets an admission cap and a right-sized interval; a conceptual error gets a redesign.

### Summary

The item takes a real and damning measurement and draws from it a causal conclusion its design cannot support. "The variable is the search, not the time" compares a searched condition against an unsearched one — but the searched condition also had five cycles of elapsed time behind it, so the finding that a search after five cycles yields new material is precisely what a time-driven model predicts. The arm that would discriminate — search the same items at cycle 0 — was never run, and "falsified twice" is two replications at one value of the treatment variable rather than two conditions. The literature the item's own testability clause nominates then runs against the conclusion in three separate places. Shojania et al.'s survival analysis of 100 systematic reviews is the field's quantitative answer to "on what basis do you set a re-check interval," and it is a *hazard function of elapsed time*: median 5.5 years to a signal for updating, 23% within two years, 15% within one, 7% already stale at publication, with topic volatility shifting the hazard by a factor near three. That is an evidence base for time-indexed re-checking and a method for stratifying it — and read against C2A2's weekly tick it says the interval is off by a very large factor, so five cycles of nothing is the expected output of a correctly-functioning, grossly over-frequent monitor. Population screening, the item's other nominated field, has known about interval cancers for decades and still operates fixed regimes, with risk stratification studied for acceptability and cost rather than deployed. Condition-based maintenance, the maintenance world's version of the item's rule, comes with an enumerated list of conditions under which it fails, and C2A2 meets several — no precursor signal for the relevant failure mode, expertise required to read the signal, and the specific cost that "CBM doesn't have a maintenance schedule... a lack of certainty on when maintenance needs to be performed," which is expensive for a fleet whose binding constraint is one run a day. And the structural point that decides clause (C): every alternative to a clock needs an **event channel**, and the objects here are claims about external literature that nothing notifies anyone about. PREMISE-053's own wording is careful about this — prefer event-triggered "for append-driven sources where feasible." A clock is not a theory that time causes change; it is the coverage mechanism of last resort for objects with no event channel. The tick's job is to schedule an observation, and the failure C2A2 measured is that the scheduled observation did not execute — which is PRESUMPTION-819's mechanism wearing a scheduling costume. Finally, the register already holds the queueing half: PREMISE-095 says the arrival rate exceeds the service rate and the queue grows without bound absent a cadence change, an admission cap, or more throughput. Three levers, none of them abolition.

### Specific risks

**Abolishing the clock removes coverage of no-event-channel objects, and this is the main risk.** If cadence is discredited as a mechanism, monitor items whose subject emits no signal — which is most of them — have no trigger at all. The predictable end state is that only items someone happens to think about get re-checked, which is worse than a badly-tuned clock and is invisible, because nothing records the items nobody thought about. Screening programmes retain fixed intervals for exactly this reason.

**Mis-parameterisation is diagnosed as design failure.** The evidence is at least as consistent with "weekly is 50–100× too fast" as with "cadence records nothing." Those two diagnoses have completely different fixes — a parameter change versus a redesign — and only one of them is cheap. Adopting the wrong one costs a rebuild and does not stop the queue growing, because PREMISE-095's arithmetic is about arrival versus service rate and is indifferent to the trigger's philosophy.

**Confounded inference entering the register as a rule.** "The variable is the search, not the time" is quotable, memorable and unidentified. Once minted it will be applied to other cadences — the re-check dates on premises, the quarterly re-validations — where the same confound applies and where the consequence of abandoning the clock is that ACTIVE premises stop being re-examined at all.

**Risk of dismissing the item, at full strength.** 17 of 17 items reported "stable, no new sources" had new material the moment anyone looked. That is a coverage failure of the monitoring step, it is measured on a real denominator, and it is unchallenged by anything in this file. "Recorded cycles were cadence ticks, not searches" is a precise and correct description of a step that did not execute and reported clean, and the 151 carry-overs advancing without remark is a real governance smell. None of my challenge touches those.

### Mitigations available

(a) **Run the missing arm before adopting the rule.** Search a sample of items at cycle 0 and compare yield against the cycle-5 yield. One run, and it identifies the causal claim the item asserts.
(b) **Right-size the interval empirically, per class,** on Shojania's method: record time-to-first-new-material for each item and fit the survival curve. The result will almost certainly be months rather than a week, and it converts an argument into a parameter.
(c) **Fix the step, not the trigger.** The measured failure is that the search did not execute and the tick reported clean — PREMISE-142's NO-EVIDENCE state and PRESUMPTION-819's deadline assertion both apply, and both are cheaper than a scheduling redesign.
(d) **Use PREMISE-095's enumerated levers** — cadence change, admission cap, or provisioning increase — rather than treating the 151 carry-overs as evidence for abolition.
(e) **Keep the clock for no-event-channel objects and add events where a channel exists.** Some items genuinely do have a feed (arXiv alerts, author RSS); those can be event-triggered. The hybrid is the standard answer, and it is what PREMISE-053 already says.
(f) **Publish the yield with its denominator** (PREMISE-168): 10 of 17 resolved by one search is a rate; "five cycles resolved nothing" needs the number of items and the number of cycles attached, or it cannot be compared against (b)'s curve.

### Recommendation: CHALLENGED

## STEELMAN

**Item:** PRESUMPTION-824

**Strongest counterargument:** The item's memorable sentence is a causal claim from a design that cannot produce one, and every field it nominates as its own evidence base has retained the mechanism it wants to discard. "The variable is the search, not the time" was derived by searching items that had already waited five cycles and finding new material. That is exactly what a time-driven model predicts: material accumulates, and the first person to look harvests it. The discriminating comparison — the same items searched at cycle 0 — was never run, so time and search are perfectly confounded, and "falsified twice" is two replications of one condition, not two conditions. Then the literature. Shojania et al. is the quantitative answer to the very question the item says has no basis, and the answer is a survival curve *in elapsed time*: median 5.5 years to a signal for updating, 23% within two years, 15% within one, 7% stale at publication, hazard multiplied ~2.7× by topic volatility. That is simultaneously an evidence base for time-indexed re-checking, a method for stratifying intervals by item class, and — read against a weekly tick — a strong indication that C2A2's interval is wrong by one to two orders of magnitude, in which case "five cycles of waiting resolved nothing" is the expected output of a correctly-functioning, over-frequent monitor rather than proof that cadence records nothing. Cancer screening, the item's other nominated field, has studied interval cancers for decades and still runs fixed regimes; risk stratification appears in the literature as acceptability studies and cost-effectiveness models, not as practice. Condition-based maintenance publishes the conditions under which the item's rule fails, and C2A2 meets several — no precursor signal, expertise needed to read one, and the specific and under-appreciated cost that CBM has no schedule, so demand arrives unpredictably against a fleet with one run a day and a human-capacity constraint. And the structural point that settles it: every alternative to a clock needs an **event channel**. This register's own PREMISE-053 says prefer event-triggered regeneration "for append-driven sources where feasible" — an append-only file emits an append. Nothing emits an event when a relevant paper appears. For no-event-channel objects the clock is not a theory that time causes change; it is the only available coverage mechanism, and its job is to schedule an observation rather than to be one. Which means the failure the item measured — 17 of 17 ticked without being searched — is a step that did not execute reporting as clean, i.e. PRESUMPTION-819's mechanism, and the fix is a deadline assertion and a NO-EVIDENCE state, not a scheduling redesign. Meanwhile the queueing half is already minted: PREMISE-095 holds that arrival exceeds service and the queue grows without bound absent **a cadence change, an admission cap, or more throughput**. The 151 carry-overs are that premise working as specified, and the levers are named.

**What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe:** The item's rule is safe to adopt only where (i) the monitored object **has an event channel** to subscribe to, so that abandoning the clock does not abandon coverage; (ii) the causal claim has been **identified** by running the cycle-0 arm, so that the fleet knows whether search or elapsed time is doing the work; and (iii) the replacement trigger is **staffing-compatible**, because condition-triggered demand arrives unpredictably against a one-run-a-day service rate. Where any of the three fails — and for most monitor-queue items all three fail — the clock must stay and the correct work is parameter estimation plus repairing the search step. Conversely, my challenge does **not** protect the current configuration: a weekly interval that yields nothing is still wrong, and PREMISE-095 already says so. Safety is a right-sized, class-stratified interval with an executing search step and a NO-EVIDENCE outcome when it does not run — not the status quo and not abolition.

**How to test:** (1) **The cycle-0 arm, and it is decisive and cheap.** Take 17 freshly-intaken items and search them immediately. If the yield matches the cycle-5 yield, the item is right and time is inert; if it is materially lower, time is the dominant variable and the item's sentence is inverted. One run. (2) **Fit the survival curve** (Shojania's method, in miniature): for each monitor item, record the elapsed time until a search first returns new material. Median and quartiles give the interval directly, per class, and replace the argument with a number. (3) **The execution audit:** for the 17 items, check whether the tick record distinguishes "searched, found nothing" from "not searched." If it cannot, the finding is PRESUMPTION-819's and should be filed there. (4) **The event-channel census:** for each of the 219 backlog items, record whether any feed exists that would emit on change. The proportion with a channel is the maximum share of the queue that event-driven monitoring could ever cover, and my strong suspicion — untested — is that it is small. (5) **Apply PREMISE-095's levers and measure:** set an admission cap, lengthen the interval to the (2) estimate, and see whether the carry-over count falls. If it does, this was a queueing problem all along.
