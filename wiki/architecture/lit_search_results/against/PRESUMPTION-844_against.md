  Strength of challenge: Moderate

  Summary: The presumption does not survive, but the evidence against it is weaker and more contested than for the other items in this cohort, and that should be said plainly. The strongest conceptual case is that presentation granularity is a known determinant of review outcomes: a large-batch review is a sequence of judgements, and sequential-judgement research finds position effects independent of case merit — although the headline judicial result has been challenged by simulation work arguing its magnitude is overestimated, so it can support a direction but not a size. The most directly analogous finding, that defect detection degrades sharply past a few hundred lines per code review, is the right shape of claim but reached this file only through a secondary source and should not be quoted as established. The firmest academic footing is alert fatigue, where a whole mitigation subfield rests on the premise that volume and presentation, not reviewer capacity, govern whether items get acted on, and where the design response is triage and batching policy rather than queue reduction. Taken together, these establish that the artifact is a variable — which is precisely and only what the item claims was never considered. What they do not establish is how large the effect is for a 677 KB, 54-card page, and no located source measures anything at that scale. The item's finding that four independent escalations all framed the bottleneck as depth, with none naming the artifact, is therefore well-founded as a diagnosis of a blind spot, without being quantified as a cause.

  Specific risks:
    - Unbounded escalation on the wrong variable: if the artifact is the constraint, reducing queue depth relieves nothing, and each escalation consumes attention while leaving the cause untouched. Four escalations with no change is consistent with this.
    - Position-dependent dispositions: if a sequence effect operates at all, cards late in a 54-card page receive systematically different treatment from cards early in it, which means the review output carries a bias no one has looked for.
    - Skim-training: per the alarm-response findings, presenting 54 items of mixed value on one page trains the reviewer to treat the whole page as low-density, degrading attention to the high-value cards specifically.
    - Rubber-stamping at scale: the batch-size finding, if it transfers, predicts that beyond a threshold the review becomes approval rather than inspection — producing a review record that looks complete and is not.
    - Shared framing across four independent runs: four agents converging on one diagnosis is not four confirmations if they share a scaffold (cf. ASSUMPTION-1150). The unanimity is itself weak evidence and may be the reason the artifact went unnamed.
    - Weak evidence base for the remedy: acting hard on a contested effect and a secondary-sourced threshold risks redesigning the artifact on grounds that do not hold.

  Mitigations available:
    - Treat the artifact as a variable and test it: split the 54 cards into several smaller reviews and measure disposition rate, time-to-first-disposition, and dispositions per session. This is cheap and it converts the question from literature to measurement.
    - Order-randomise the cards. If dispositions differ by position, a sequence effect exists in this system; if they do not, the judicial literature does not transfer and the concern is retired on evidence.
    - Prioritise rather than enumerate: present the highest-value cards first and cap the page, per the standard alarm-prioritisation remedy.
    - Instrument the review, not just the queue: record where in the sequence the reviewer stops, which is the direct measurement of whether depth or form is binding.
    - Retrieve the primary source for the code-review batch-size threshold before any design decision cites it.

  STEELMAN:
    Item: PRESUMPTION-844
    Strongest counterargument: A review artifact is not a neutral container for a queue; it is an instrument with its own transfer function, and treating it as fixed while escalating about depth is optimising a variable that may not be binding. Sequential-judgement research finds that position within a session predicts outcomes independently of merit, and alarm-response research finds that reviewers probability-match their attention to the perceived value density of a channel — so a 54-card single page of mixed-value items does not merely take longer to work through, it trains skimming and produces position-dependent dispositions. The nearest quantitative analogue, that inspection quality collapses past a few hundred lines per code review, describes exactly the transition from reviewing to approving, and a 677 KB page is far beyond any comparable threshold. That an entire alert-fatigue mitigation subfield exists, whose interventions are all changes to presentation rather than to volume, is itself the argument: the field concluded long ago that the artifact is where the leverage is. Four independent runs escalating without one of them naming the artifact is not four confirmations of a diagnosis; if they share a scaffold, it is one framing repeated four times, which is precisely how a blind spot looks from inside.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (a) The review would have to be genuinely depth-bound — the reviewer reaching the end of the page and running out of items rather than out of attention, which is directly observable from where dispositions stop; (b) dispositions would have to be independent of card position, which order-randomisation tests in one pass; (c) the sequence effect would have to transfer from human judicial decisions to this reviewer and this task, which the replication controversy makes uncertain in magnitude even if the direction holds; (d) the 400-LOC analogue would have to be verified against its primary source before being used to justify any redesign.
    How to test: Three measurements, all cheap, none requiring the literature to be right. (1) Record where in the 54-card sequence the reviewer stops and how disposition rate varies by position. If the rate is flat and the reviewer reaches the end, depth is binding and this challenge fails. If the rate declines or the reviewer stops early, the artifact is binding. (2) Randomise card order across sessions; a position effect that survives randomisation of content is a property of the instrument. (3) Split-test the form: same 54 cards, one session as a single page, another as six pages of nine, and compare total dispositions and disposition quality. Any difference is the artifact's effect measured directly, and it settles the question without needing the judicial or code-review literatures to transfer at all.
