**Polarity note.** The item is worded as the defective belief and the diagnosis is, as far as I can find, unchallengeable: no literature defends inferring a population from the subset that wrote something, and the missing-data and survivorship-bias traditions both name it as a canonical error. - **(i)** absence of output is not evidence of absence of work — **not challenged**, confirmed;
- **(ii)** the fix is a **manifest of what was supposed to run**, against which absence can register — **challenged, moderately strongly**;
- **(iii)** with such a manifest the metrics in `metrics/` would mean what they say — **challenged, strongly**.

"PARTIALLY-CHALLENGED" here should be read as *the observation survives intact and the proposed instrument is the wrong one*, not as doubt about the nine mute runs.

### Challenging evidence found: Yes (partial — against the remedy and against the implied repair of the metrics)

### Sources

1. **Rubin (1976) and the MCAR/MAR/MNAR framework; Sullivan et al., "Trial arm outcome variance difference after dropout as an indicator of missing-not-at-random bias in RCTs"** (PMC7615524). [verified this run via PMC and multiple secondary summaries; Rubin 1976 itself is canonical and was **not** re-verified in primary form] — The strongest challenge to proposition (iii). The literature is explicit that **there is no statistical test that distinguishes MAR from MNAR**, and that under MNAR both complete-case analysis and naïve imputation remain biased. A manifest tells you *how many* runs are missing and *which* — it converts an unknown-unknown into a known-unknown, which is real progress — but it does not tell you whether the missingness is related to what the missing runs would have said. If runs fail preferentially when their subject-matter is hard, the mute population is MNAR and no count of it repairs any downstream rate. The manifest gives you the missingness indicator; the literature says the missingness indicator is not sufficient to remove the bias.
2. **Wald's survivorship-bias analysis (Statistical Research Group, 1943; widely re-described).** [verified this run via multiple secondary accounts including Wikipedia's Abraham Wald entry and mcdreeamie-musings; the original SRG memoranda **not** consulted] — Included because it sharpens the same point in the direction the item wants. Wald did not solve the returning-bomber problem by *listing which aircraft failed to return*; the list was already available and useless on its own. He solved it by building a **model of the selection process** that let him estimate the damage distribution over all aircraft from the distribution over survivors. The lesson transfers exactly: knowing that nine runs were mute is the easy half; knowing what their silence implies about the eight proposals that were filed requires a model of why runs go mute, which a manifest does not supply.
3. **Chandra & Toueg, "Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable Distributed Systems," JACM 43(2), 1996** (and the 1991 PODC preliminary version). [verified this run — ACM DL listing, UT Austin course PDF of p225-chandra, DTIC technical report] — The theoretical objection to absence-based monitoring as such. In an asynchronous system a detector **cannot distinguish a crashed process from a slow one**; detectors are characterisable only by completeness and accuracy, and the useful ones are explicitly *unreliable* — they suspect correct processes and fail to suspect crashed ones, possibly infinitely often. This is not a quality-of-implementation issue. Any manifest-plus-deadline scheme the fleet builds is a failure detector of the ◇P family at best, and its output is a suspicion, not a fact. Designing as though "absent from the manifest at deadline" means "did not run" imports an accuracy property the theory says is unavailable.
4. **Heartbeat-interval tuning practice in distributed systems** (GeeksforGeeks, ByteByteGo, Bhayani; convergent practitioner accounts). [verified this run; practitioner/tutorial tier, not research] — The operational form of source 3, and the direct cost argument. Short intervals with tight timeouts detect fast and produce false positives under congestion; long intervals with loose timeouts avoid false positives and delay detection. The stated rule of thumb is that **batch and storage systems should optimise for stability, not speed** — i.e. accept slow detection to avoid crying wolf — which is precisely the regime a 25-run-per-day fleet is in. The standard mitigation is *n* consecutive misses before declaring death, which further widens the window in which the item's hazard is live.
5. **Clinical-decision-support alert-override literature: providers override 49–96% of drug–drug-interaction and interruptive alerts** (Ancker et al. and the CDS alert-fatigue tradition; PMC5387195, "Effects of workload, work complexity, and repeated alerts on alert fatigue in a CDS system"). [verified this run via PMC and multiple secondary summaries] — The cost of dead-man's-switch false positives, quantified in the only domain that has measured it properly. A monitor that fires on absence in a population where legitimate absence is common becomes a monitor whose output is habituated away. The mechanism named is habituation/desensitisation, and the safety consequence is documented. For this fleet the base rate is the problem: **nine of twenty-five is 36%**, so a naïve manifest-deadline alarm would fire on roughly a third of scheduled slots on day one, which is squarely in the regime the CDS literature says produces override behaviour rather than response.
6. **Elastic Security "Fill rule execution gaps" documentation.** [verified this run via elastic.co docs] — The closest thing I found to the item's implied remedy actually shipped, and it is instructive in both directions. Elastic makes *gaps* a first-class object: "periods when a detection rule didn't run as scheduled." It supports the remedy's feasibility. But the documentation's own list of gap causes — "system updates, resource constraints, or simply turning off a rule" — makes the limitation explicit: **the gap record cannot distinguish a rule that failed from a rule that was deliberately disabled.** For a fleet where some scheduled runs legitimately have nothing to do, this is not an edge case; it is the majority of the signal.
7. **Configuration-drift and alert-coverage-gap practice** (New Relic "Alert coverage gaps and condition recommendations"; Wiz, "Configuration Drift Explained"; practitioner drift-management guidance). [verified this run; vendor documentation tier] — Evidence for the specific hazard that the manifest becomes the new single point of failure. The recurring finding across this literature is that coverage programmes must cover "100% of the environment, not just the portion that happens to be version-controlled," and that a finding arriving "without a clear path to resolution becomes another entry in an already-overloaded alert queue." A hand-maintained list of expected runs is exactly the artefact that drifts: it is off the execution path, nothing enforces its correspondence to the scheduler, and the first schedule change makes it confidently wrong. This is the same failure class flagged against PRESUMPTION-797's dependency record and PRESUMPTION-795's register.
8. **Dead-man's-switch and heartbeat practice: Prometheus `Watchdog`/`DeadMansSwitch` alert, `absent()` rules, Dead Man's Snitch / healthchecks.io / Cronitor.** [verified this run via O'Reilly *Hands-On Infrastructure Monitoring with Prometheus*, Grafana Labs metamonitoring post, and several convergent practitioner writeups] — Establishes what the established remedy actually *is*, and it is not a manifest. The pattern is **positive acknowledgement emitted by the run itself** on a schedule known to an external service, with the external service holding the deadline. One practitioner account also names the failure mode of the alternative: `absent()` rules "silently break when label sets change" — i.e. the absence-detector itself fails silently when the population definition drifts, which is PRESUMPTION-799's problem arriving inside 798's remedy.

### Strength of challenge: Moderate

### Summary

I found nothing defending the presumption. The diagnosis is correct, it is a named error in at least three traditions, and the fleet's version of it is unusually clean. The challenge is to what follows.

First, the remedy is aimed at the wrong object. The established countermeasure for "did this run happen" is not a register of what was supposed to happen but a **positive acknowledgement emitted by the run**, checked against a deadline held somewhere the run cannot reach — the dead-man's-switch pattern, in which silence is the failure signal and the alert fires all the time. This puts the instrument on the execution path. A manifest, by contrast, is a second document that must be kept in correspondence with the scheduler by hand; the drift literature is uniform that such artefacts decay, and the specific decay mode here is that the fleet ends up holding a confidently wrong list of what it believes it runs. That is worse than holding none, because it reads as though the question were settled.

Second, the manifest cannot make the distinction the fleet actually needs. Elastic, which has shipped the manifest remedy as a product feature, states that its gap records cannot separate "the rule failed" from "the rule was turned off." The fleet's harder version is "the run executed correctly and had nothing to file" versus "the run died before it could file" — and a manifest of expected runs is silent on that difference by construction. Only a positive completion record from the run itself resolves it, which is a per-run change, not a fleet-level document.

Third, and most consequential for the Critical risk grading: even a perfect manifest does not repair the metrics. Missing-data theory is explicit that there is no test distinguishing MAR from MNAR, and that under MNAR the missingness indicator does not remove bias. Wald's problem is the same shape and its solution was a model of the selection mechanism, not a list of the missing. If mute runs are mute for reasons correlated with what they would have reported — and the fleet has no evidence either way — then knowing the count of them tells you the sample is biased without telling you which direction. The honest post-manifest statement is "our rates are computed over 16 of 25 and we cannot bound the bias," which is a real improvement in candour and no improvement in the numbers.

Fourth, there is a theoretical ceiling. Chandra and Toueg establish that in an asynchronous system no detector can distinguish crashed from slow; every practical detector makes both kinds of mistake, possibly unboundedly often. Whatever the fleet builds, its output is a suspicion with a tunable error trade-off, and the tuning has a cost the CDS alert-override literature has measured: 49–96% override rates once alerts become routine. With nine of twenty-five slots silent, a naïve absence alarm starts life in the fatigue regime.

### Specific risks

- **The manifest becomes the failure locus.** A hand-maintained expected-runs list drifts on the first schedule change, and thereafter absence is measured against a fiction. The fleet then has two populations it cannot reconcile — runs that exist and runs the manifest thinks exist — which is PRESUMPTION-801's problem reproduced inside 798's remedy.
- **False comfort about the metrics.** The largest risk of a partially-good fix here is that `metrics/` gets a "coverage: 16/25" footnote and is then read as sound. Under MNAR it is not sound, and the footnote makes it look audited.
- **Alarm habituation.** At a 36% silent rate, an absence alarm on every scheduled slot will be ignored within days, at which point the fleet has both the original blindness and a monitor it believes covers it.
- **Undetectable legitimate no-ops.** Without a positive completion record, the fleet must either treat every legitimate empty run as a failure (fatigue) or suppress empties (blindness). The manifest does not offer a third option.
- **If the item is dismissed entirely**, the hazard is exactly as the item states and the survivorship-bias literature endorses the severity: every rate in `metrics/` is conditioned on having written something, and the corpus is a biased sample of itself.

### Mitigations available

(a) **Positive completion records, per run, on the execution path.** Every scheduled run emits a terminal record — started-at, ended-at, exit disposition, and an explicit `deliverables: 0` where that is the correct outcome. This is one write per run, it cannot drift out of correspondence with the scheduler because the scheduler produces it, and it is the only instrument that separates "ran and had nothing to say" from "did not run." Highest value per unit cost in this file.
(b) **Hold the deadline outside the fleet.** The dead-man's-switch pattern (Dead Man's Snitch, healthchecks.io, Cronitor, or a Prometheus `absent()` rule on a separate host) puts the timeout somewhere the failing run cannot silence. Note that this is also the whole of PRESUMPTION-799's remedy, and the same instrument serves both.
(c) **Derive the expected-runs list from the scheduler, never write it.** If a manifest is wanted, generate it from launchd/cron configuration at run time so it cannot drift. A generated manifest is a view; a written one is a claim.
(d) **Tune for stability, not latency, and require *n* consecutive misses.** The batch-systems guidance is explicit and the fleet is a batch system. Accept slow detection to keep the alarm credible.
(e) **Report coverage as a denominator, not a footnote.** Every rate the fleet publishes should carry the count it was computed over. This does not fix MNAR bias but it stops the bias being invisible, and it is the honest form of what a manifest half-delivers.
(f) **Do one MNAR sensitivity check before trusting any post-fix metric.** Once completion records exist, compare the subject-matter distribution of mute runs against filed runs. If they differ, the missingness is informative and the rates need bounds, not a footnote.

### Recommendation: PARTIALLY-CHALLENGED

## STEELMAN

**Item:** PRESUMPTION-798

**Strongest counterargument:** The observation is right and the instrument it implies is the wrong one, chosen because it is the one a documentation-producing fleet knows how to build. The field's answer to "did the scheduled thing happen" is not a register of intentions but a positive acknowledgement from the actor, checked against a deadline held by someone else — the dead-man's-switch pattern, where the ping fires constantly and silence is the alarm. That instrument sits on the execution path and cannot drift away from the scheduler. A manifest sits beside the scheduler and must be maintained into agreement with it forever; the drift literature says it will not be, and Elastic — which has actually shipped gap-tracking as a feature — states plainly that its gap records cannot tell a failed rule from a disabled one. The fleet's version of that ambiguity is worse, because "ran correctly and filed nothing" is a normal outcome here, and no manifest of expectations can distinguish it from death. Then the deeper problem: even a perfect manifest does not give back the metrics. Missing-data theory is unambiguous that no test separates MAR from MNAR and that a missingness indicator does not remove MNAR bias, and Wald's bombers make the same point positively — the list of aircraft that failed to return was never the scarce input; the model of *why* they failed to return was. Knowing that nine of twenty-five runs were mute tells you your rates are conditioned on writing, which you already inferred; it does not tell you whether mute runs were mute because their work was hard, which is the only thing that would let you correct anything. And there is a ceiling under all of it: Chandra and Toueg proved that in an asynchronous system no detector distinguishes crashed from slow, so whatever gets built emits suspicions with a tunable error rate — and at a 36% silence base rate, the tuning that avoids false negatives lands squarely in the regime where clinical systems see 49–96% override. The item has found a real blindness. The manifest would document it rather than remove it, and would then be mistaken for having removed it.

**What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe:** Every scheduled run must emit a terminal record it cannot skip, distinguishing "completed with nothing to file" from "did not complete," with the deadline for that record held outside the fleet. Every published rate must carry the denominator it was computed over. And before any post-fix metric is trusted, someone must check whether mute runs differ systematically from filed runs — because if they do, the counts are still not recoverable and the correct output is a bound, not a number. If instead a hand-written manifest is produced and the metrics keep their current form, the hazard is fully live *and* covered by an artefact that reads as though it were addressed — the worst of the three available states.

**How to test:** Three cheap tests. **(1) Instrument before designing:** add a two-line terminal record to every scheduled run for one week and simply count. This measures the true silence rate, separates legitimate no-ops from deaths, and costs no design decisions — and it will tell you whether nine-of-twenty-five was a bad day or the steady state. **(2) MNAR probe:** take the nine mute runs from 2026-08-14 and classify them by scheduled subject matter, then compare against the sixteen that filed. If the distributions differ materially, missingness is informative, `metrics/` needs bounds rather than footnotes, and the Critical grading is confirmed empirically rather than by inference. **(3) Drift test on the remedy itself:** if a manifest is built anyway, diff it against the scheduler's actual configuration weekly and record the number of discrepancies. If that number is ever non-zero, the manifest has failed in the way the drift literature predicts, and the generated-view option (mitigation (c)) should replace it.
