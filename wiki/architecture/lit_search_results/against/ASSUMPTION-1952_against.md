```
    1. Kahneman's adversarial collaborations, as reported by American Scientist ("Teams of

       — Boundary condition. Kahneman's first attempt, with Anne Treisman, never resolved the
       disagreement, and each side could explain away the joint results ("the stubborn

       more than two decades on and its promise "still largely theoretical". APA Monitor says

       wealth-and-happiness collaboration did reach a joint result.
    2. Jirschitzka, J. et al., 2017. "Inter-rater reliability and validity of peer reviews in
       an interdisciplinary field." Scientometrics 113(2). [snippet only]
       — Direct challenge to certification as a stable signal. In 145 submissions to an
       interdisciplinary conference, inter-rater reliability was "rather poor", and reviews
       from the same discipline were no better than reviews from other fields. A meta-analysis
       of 48 studies cited in the same context calls peer-review reliability "quite limited".
    3. Mallard, G., Lamont, M. & Guetzkow, J. on how panels judge interdisciplinary proposals.
       [snippet only; citation seen, venue not shown]
       — Failure mode. Reviewers outside their own field fall back on "respect for disciplinary
       sovereignty" and "deference to expertise". In C2A2 terms, a rival-tradition certifier
       may defer instead of certifying, so a "both sign" result could record deference rather
       than agreement.
    4. Seeber, M., Vlegels, J. & Cattaneo, M. (interdisciplinary proposals; 1,928 proposals,
       5,330 evaluations; apparently JASIST 73(8), 2022, per result URL). [snippet only]
       — Limits the challenge. The effect of interdisciplinarity on funding was negligible, and
       they found no evidence of disciplinary "turf wars". Cross-field judgement is not
       systematically hostile.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate. The evidence shows rival-practitioner certification is
    rare, slow, noisy and prone to deference. It does not show the gate is unworkable. All
    sources are snippets.

  Summary: Adversarial collaboration, the closest working analogue, succeeds in some cases but
    is rare and often ends without resolution, with each side able to absorb the joint
    result. Cross-field peer review has poor inter-rater reliability, so one pair of
    certifications is a noisy signal. Out-of-field reviewers tend to defer, so dual sign-off
    can measure politeness rather than convergence. None of this shows mutual certification is
    impossible; it shows the gate is costly and its output needs interpreting.

  Specific risks: Candidates pile up uncertified (77 already). Where they are certified, "both
    signed" is read as convergence when it may be deference or noise. Unresolved candidates
    stay in limbo and nothing is ever counted.

  Mitigations available: Pre-register what each side must say to count as certification
    (adversarial-collaboration practice). Require each certifier to state reasons, not just a
    tick, and code them for deference. Use more than one certifier per side and report
    agreement. Add a timeout or "unresolved" state so candidates can exit without
    certification.
```

```
STEELMAN:
  Item: ASSUMPTION-1952
  Strongest counterargument: The best-documented attempt at rival-practitioner validation,
    Kahneman's adversarial collaboration, was invented by its main advocate and still failed
    on his first try, and two decades later remains rare. Peer review across fields, the
    institutional version, is barely more reliable than chance and leans on deference. A gate
    that needs both rivals to sign therefore either rarely opens, or opens on signatures that
    mean "not my field, looks fine". In both cases the ledger does not separate real
    cross-tradition agreement from noise.
  What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: certifiers are named and committed in
    advance; the criteria for certification are agreed before the candidate is judged; a
    certifier's reasons are recorded and checked for deference; and "unresolved" counts as a
    legitimate outcome.
  How to test: Pilot 5-10 candidates with one named certifier per side. Measure time to
    decision, agreement between two certifiers on the same side, and the share of
    certifications that give substantive reasons.
```
