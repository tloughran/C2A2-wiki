  Sources:
    1. Fanelli, D., Moher, D., 2019/2021. "What difference do retractions make? An estimate of the epistemic impact of retractions on recent meta-analyses" (bioRxiv 734137); published as "What difference might retractions make? An estimate of the potential epistemic cost of retractions on meta-analyses," Accountability in Research (2021). — Compared summary effect sizes of recent meta-analyses with and without the retracted studies they contained. Retractions for plagiarism or other issues unrelated to data, methods or results had no impact on meta-analysis conclusions; since such retractions constitute over 75% of all retractions, the authors conclude the epistemic impact of most retractions is likely null. Only retractions for data/methods/results issues produced a statistically significant reduction in estimated effect size (overestimation averaging 30%, median 13%, assuming retracted results are wholly false). This is a direct, quantified challenge to blanket invalidation.
    2. Kataoka, Y., Banno, M., Tsujimoto, Y., Ariie, T., Taito, S., Suzuki, T., Oide, S., Furukawa, T.A., 2022. "The impact of retracted randomised controlled trials on systematic reviews and clinical practice guidelines: a meta-epidemiological study." medRxiv 2022.01.30.22270124 (also circulated as "Retracted Randomised Controlled Trials Were Cited and Not Corrected in Systematic Reviews and Clinical Practice Guidelines"). — Identified 98 retracted RCTs and 360 citing articles (335 systematic reviews, 25 guidelines). Excluding retracted trials reversed the direction of effect in 8.4% of meta-analyses and lost statistical significance in 16.0%. The corollary is that roughly 84–92% of downstream syntheses reached the same qualitative conclusion without the retracted input.
    3. Hsiao, T.-K., Schneider, J., 2021. "Continued use of retracted papers: temporal trends in citations and (lack of) awareness of retractions shown in citation contexts in biomedicine." Quantitative Science Studies 2(4):1144–1169. — 7,813 retracted papers, 169,434 citations, 48,134 citation contexts. Retraction did not change the way retracted papers were cited, and only 722 of 13,252 post-retraction citation contexts (5.4%) acknowledged the retraction. Descriptively, invalidation does not propagate in practice; normatively this is also a warning, but it refutes any assumption that propagation is automatic.
    4. van der Vet, P.E., Nijveen, H., 2016. "Propagation of errors in citation networks: a study involving the entire citation network of a widely cited paper published in, and later retracted from, the journal Nature." Research Integrity and Peer Review 1:3. — Traced the full citation network of a retracted Nature paper. Directly citing articles are the important source of propagation — i.e. propagation is concentrated in the first hop and in citations that actually use the result, not distributed uniformly across everything that names the identifier. Supports a dependency-typed rather than blanket rule.
    5. Greenberg, S.A., 2009. "How citation distortions create unfounded authority: analysis of a citation network." BMJ 339:b2680. — Documents "amplification": marked expansion of a belief system by papers presenting no data addressing it. A large share of citing papers make no claim whose truth depends on the cited claim; their content is therefore not a function of the cited claim's status, in either direction.
    6. Simkin, M.V., Roychowdhury, V.P., 2003/2005. "Read Before You Cite!" Complex Systems 14(3). — ~80% of citations estimated to be copied rather than read. A citation that was never read cannot have transmitted content into the citing work, so its withdrawal cannot invalidate that work's substance; it invalidates only the citation itself.
    7. Hansson, S.O., 2023. "Citation of retracted papers." Theoria 89(4). — Philosophical treatment of the norms governing citation of retracted work; relevant as a reminder that permissible and impermissible post-retraction citation are distinguished by function (historical/critical reference vs. evidential reliance), which is incompatible with a uniform invalidation rule.
    8. Note on contrary evidence (reported honestly): a 2025 figure circulating in industry commentary claims 35% of meta-analyses show ≥10% change in effect estimates after removing retracted studies, attributed to a JAMA-published meta-analysis. [Unverified — could not confirm the primary source, authors, or exact venue before search budget was exhausted. Do not cite.] If verified, it would moderate but not overturn the Fanelli/Moher and Kataoka findings, since a 10% effect-size shift is not the same as invalidation.

  Strength of challenge: Strong

  Summary: The presumption that a retired upstream claim invalidates downstream work citing it is contradicted by direct quantitative measurement in the retraction-impact literature. Fanelli and Moher find that the majority of retractions — those for plagiarism and misconduct unrelated to data, methods or results, over 75% of the total — have essentially no epistemic impact on downstream syntheses. Kataoka et al. find that removing retracted RCTs reverses direction in only 8.4% of meta-analyses and costs statistical significance in 16%, leaving the large majority of downstream conclusions intact. Van der Vet and Nijveen show propagation is concentrated in first-hop citations that actually use the retracted result, and Greenberg's amplification finding plus Simkin and Roychowdhury's copying estimate together imply that many citing works never carried the content at all. The correct model is dependency-typed: invalidation propagates where the downstream claim is load-bearing on the withdrawn content, is scoped by the reason for withdrawal, and otherwise does not propagate. A blanket rule will over-withdraw, and — given Hsiao and Schneider's finding that real-world propagation is near zero — a blanket rule is also not what the field practises.

  Specific risks:
    - Over-withdrawal: applying blanket invalidation destroys downstream material that the evidence says would survive in ~84–92% of dependency-bearing cases and in >75% of retraction reasons.
    - Reason-blindness: withdrawing an upstream claim for a provenance or attribution defect is treated identically to withdrawing it for a substantive error, though the literature shows only the latter moves downstream estimates.
    - Hop-blindness: uniform propagation ignores the finding that impact concentrates in directly-citing works that use the result.
    - Under-withdrawal (the mirror risk): the same literature shows the field systematically fails to propagate even where it should — 5.4% acknowledgement of retraction in post-retraction citation contexts — so a blanket-off rule is equally unsafe.
    - Cascade churn: automatic invalidation across a dense internal citation graph can produce large, mostly-spurious withdrawal cascades that consume review capacity.

  Mitigations available:
    - Type the dependency at citation time: distinguish evidential reliance from background/historical/contrastive mention. Only evidential reliance should trigger propagation.
    - Type the withdrawal reason: substantive (data/methods/results) vs. non-substantive (attribution, provenance, formatting, duplication). Fanelli and Moher's result licenses propagating only the former.
    - Limit automatic propagation to first-hop dependents and require re-adjudication for further hops.
    - Run the sensitivity test rather than assuming: recompute the downstream conclusion with the withdrawn input removed and check whether it changes, mirroring the meta-analytic method.
    - Mark rather than delete: annotate downstream items with the upstream withdrawal and its reason, preserving the record while flagging the dependency — this addresses the 5.4% acknowledgement problem without over-withdrawal.

  Search scope: Retraction impact on meta-analyses and systematic reviews; post-retraction citation behaviour; error propagation in citation networks; norms of citing retracted work; citation-copying estimates; citation distortion/amplification. Searched 2026-08-18. Not covered: retraction-notice indexing/discoverability infrastructure (RISRS recommendations); non-biomedical retraction domains; legal/regulatory analogues of downstream invalidation.

  Recommendation: CHALLENGED

  STEELMAN:
    Item: PRESUMPTION-756
    Strongest counterargument: The presumption is defensible under a specific and possibly applicable condition — that C2A2's downstream items are constructed by evidential reliance rather than by perfunctory mention. The retraction literature's optimistic figures are driven substantially by citations that were background, decorative, or copied; if C2A2's anchors are load-bearing by construction, then C2A2's population resembles the 8.4%/16% dependency-bearing subset, not the ~80% perfunctory bulk, and the survival rates from the medical literature do not transfer. The Kataoka finding that no citing article corrected itself after publication also argues that a conservative, propagation-on-by-default posture is a reasonable overcorrection for a system that cannot rely on human vigilance. The literature's real instruction is therefore not "stop propagating" but "type the dependency and the withdrawal reason, then propagate where the type warrants it."
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (a) each citation records whether the downstream claim depends evidentially on the cited content; (b) each withdrawal records its reason, classified as substantive or non-substantive; (c) propagation is conditional on both, and first-hop by default; (d) a sensitivity re-computation is attempted before withdrawal wherever the downstream claim is quantitative or aggregative; (e) non-propagating dependents are annotated rather than left silent, so the 5.4%-acknowledgement failure mode is not reproduced internally.
    How to test: Take the withdrawn upstream claims from the current period and, for each downstream item citing them, classify the dependency type and then independently assess whether the downstream item's conclusion changes with the upstream removed. Compute the internal analogue of the 8.4%/16% figures. If C2A2's rate is close to the published rates, blanket propagation is refuted and should be replaced with the typed rule. If C2A2's rate is dramatically higher — approaching 100% — that is evidence the anchors are genuinely load-bearing and the presumption holds for this corpus, which would itself be a significant and publishable finding about the difference between C2A2's citation practice and the literature's.
