  Original statement: Whether a source's self-revision binds a construct derived from it. (i.e. that when a source revises its own position, constructs operationalised from the earlier position are thereby invalidated or must follow.)

  Sources:
    1. Borsboom, D., Mellenbergh, G.J., van Heerden, J., 2004. "The concept of validity." Psychological Review 111(4):1061–1071. — Argues that a test is valid for measuring an attribute if (a) the attribute exists and (b) variation in the attribute causally produces variation in the measurement outcomes; validation research must be directed at the processes conveying that effect, not at relations between the measured attribute and other attributes. On this account validity is ontological and causal, a property of the measure-attribute pair, and is not underwritten by the nomological network. A source revising its theoretical position therefore does not, by itself, invalidate a construct derived from it — the causal question is untouched by the theoretical revision.
    2. Underdetermination of Scientific Theory / the Duhem–Quine thesis (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Underdetermination of Scientific Theory"; Duhem, P., 1906/1954; Quine, W.V.O., 1951). — Hypotheses have empirical implications only in conjunction with auxiliary assumptions, so a failed prediction or a revision leaves open which element of the bundle to revise. Applied here: a source's self-revision does not identify which derived construct, operationalisation or auxiliary is the one that must change. Binding the construct automatically is a determinate response to an indeterminate situation.
    3. Flake, J.K., Fried, E.I., 2020. "Measurement Schmeasurement: Questionable Measurement Practices and How to Avoid Them." Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science 3(4):456–465. — Documents pervasive "operationalization flexibility," including 22 catalogued instances in published articles and extreme cases where a scale used to assess a construct in Study 1 was combined with another scale to form a new index of the same construct in Study 2 within a single paper. This cuts against the presumption in a specific way: constructs in practice are already only loosely coupled to their originating definitions, so there is usually no tight binding for a source revision to transmit through. It also cuts the other way as a risk — a derived construct may never have faithfully tracked the source in the first place.
    4. "Persistence of measurement problems in psychological research" (editorial), and the broader measurement-crisis literature. [Author attribution not confirmed in this session — cite by theme/venue.] — Corroborates that construct definitions and their operationalisations routinely diverge and that the divergence is a standing feature, not an anomaly.
    5. Fanelli, D., Moher, D., 2019/2021. "What difference do/might retractions make?" bioRxiv 734137 / Accountability in Research. — Offers the empirical analogue: upstream withdrawal, the strongest possible form of self-revision, left the conclusions of the large majority of downstream syntheses unchanged (retractions unrelated to data/methods/results, >75% of all retractions, had no impact). If full retraction typically fails to bind downstream quantities, ordinary self-revision has weaker claim to do so.
    6. Kataoka, Y., Banno, M., Tsujimoto, Y. et al., 2022. "The impact of retracted randomised controlled trials on systematic reviews and clinical practice guidelines: a meta-epidemiological study." medRxiv 2022.01.30.22270124. — Direction of effect reversed in only 8.4% of meta-analyses on exclusion of retracted trials; 16.0% lost significance. Same inference: derived quantities are substantially robust to upstream withdrawal.
    7. Greenberg, S.A., 2009. "How citation distortions create unfounded authority." BMJ 339:b2680. — Documents conversion of hypothesis into fact through citation alone. Where a construct was derived from an amplified reading rather than from the source's actual position, the source's self-revision is not addressed to the thing the construct actually encodes — a further disconnect between revision and binding.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate

  Summary: The presumption assumes a tight transmission channel from a source's theoretical position to a construct operationalised from it, such that revision at the source propagates automatically. Validity theory in its strongest modern formulation denies this channel: on Borsboom, Mellenbergh and van Heerden's account, whether a measure is valid depends on whether the attribute exists and causally drives the measurement outcomes, not on the surrounding theory — so theoretical revision leaves the validity question formally untouched. The Duhem–Quine literature adds that even where revision is relevant, it does not identify which element of the derived bundle must change, so automatic binding substitutes a determinate response for an indeterminate one. Empirically, Flake and Fried show that operationalisations already drift substantially from their source definitions, and the retraction-impact literature shows that even outright withdrawal — a stronger signal than self-revision — leaves the majority of derived quantities unchanged. The evidence supports a conditional rule: revision binds the derived construct only where the construct's operationalisation demonstrably depends on the revised element.

  Specific risks:
    - Over-binding: invalidating derived constructs on every upstream self-revision will discard material that is formally and empirically independent of the revised element.
    - Indeterminacy laundering: choosing to bind the construct rather than an auxiliary is an arbitrary resolution of a genuinely underdetermined choice, presented as if forced.
    - Wrong target: if the construct was derived from an amplified or drifted reading of the source, the source's revision may not address what the construct actually encodes, and binding produces a change that tracks nothing.
    - Under-binding (mirror risk): where the operationalisation genuinely does depend on the revised element, the Flake/Fried transparency failures mean nobody may be able to tell — the dependency was never documented.
    - Validity confusion: treating the source's endorsement as the ground of validity, rather than the attribute-measure causal relation, entrenches a form of validity-by-authority.

  Mitigations available:
    - Record, at derivation time, exactly which claims of the source the operationalisation depends on. Flake and Fried's six transparency questions (construct definition, measure selection justification, operationalisation description, quantification, modifications, validity evidence) are a ready template.
    - Bind conditionally: check whether the revised element appears in the recorded dependency list before propagating.
    - Where dependency is present, re-derive and compare rather than invalidate — the meta-analytic sensitivity method applied to constructs.
    - Maintain the construct's own validity evidence independently of the source's current position, per Borsboom et al., so a revision at the source does not remove the construct's only warrant.
    - Where the dependency is genuinely underdetermined, record the indeterminacy explicitly rather than resolving it by default toward binding.

  Recommendation: CHALLENGED

  STEELMAN:
    Item: PRESUMPTION-757
    Strongest counterargument: Borsboom et al.'s account is a contested position in validity theory, not a settled one — the Cronbach–Meehl nomological-network tradition and its descendants hold that construct meaning is constituted partly by the theoretical network, on which view a source's revision does bear on the derived construct. More importantly, C2A2's constructs are not psychometric instruments with independent causal validation; they are interpretive derivations whose only warrant may be the source's position. Where a construct has no validity evidence independent of the source, Borsboom's argument is unavailable, because condition (a) — that the attribute exists — was being underwritten by the source's authority. In that setting the presumption is close to correct: the construct has no legs of its own, and when the source moves, nothing is left holding it up. The literature's contribution is to make this conditional explicit rather than to reject the presumption outright.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (a) each derived construct records which source claims its operationalisation depends on; (b) each construct records whatever validity evidence it has independent of the source's endorsement, and constructs with none are flagged as source-dependent; (c) propagation of a source revision is conditional on the dependency record, with source-dependent constructs propagating by default and independently-warranted constructs requiring an explicit argument; (d) underdetermined cases are recorded as such rather than defaulted; (e) re-derivation-and-compare is preferred to invalidation.
    How to test: For a sample of derived constructs, attempt to state the construct's warrant without reference to the source's current position. Constructs that cannot be so stated are source-dependent and the presumption holds for them; constructs that can are independently warranted and the presumption is refuted for them. Then take actual instances of source self-revision in the corpus, apply both the blanket rule and the conditional rule, and compare how many constructs each invalidates and how many of those invalidations survive independent review. The gap between the two counts is the measured cost of the presumption.
