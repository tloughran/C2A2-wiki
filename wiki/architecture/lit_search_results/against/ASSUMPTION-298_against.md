  Sources:
    1. Sitkin, S., See, K., Miller, C., Lawless, M., Carton, A., 2011. "The Paradox of Stretch Goals: Organizations in Pursuit of the Seemingly Impossible." Academy of Management Review. — Stretch goals succeed only with slack resources plus strong recent performance; they are most seductive precisely where riskiest. Repeated shortfalls erode self-efficacy, increase stress, reduce learning and commitment.
    2. Buehler, R., Griffin, D., Ross, M., 1994. "Exploring the 'Planning Fallacy': Why People Underestimate Their Task Completion Times." JPSP. — Inside-view forecasts systematically underestimate completion time even when the forecaster knows their past overruns; "firm expectation of over-delivery" is the textbook inside-view signature.
    3. Flyvbjerg, B., 2006. "From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right." Project Management Journal. — Across large project samples, ~86% overrun; the validated corrective is reference-class forecasting (anchoring on the outcome distribution of similar past projects), the opposite of deliberate over-promising.
    4. Staw, B., 1976. "Knee-deep in the Big Muddy: A Study of Escalating Commitment." OBHP. — Public commitments to ambitious targets drive escalation of commitment after setbacks: resources flow to defending the promise rather than the goal.
  Strength of challenge: Strong
  Summary: The planning literature challenges every component of this claim. The planning fallacy shows that confident expectation of over-delivery is the standard cognitive error, uncorrected by experience because overruns get attributed to one-off causes. The stretch-goals literature shows deliberate over-commitment helps only under conditions (slack plus a record of recent wins) that a single-human-gated, deadline-pressured project does not clearly meet, and that the same organizations most drawn to stretch goals are least positioned to survive missing them. The "accelerating capability" rider does not rescue the method: capability growth rates are themselves uncertain forecasts, and Flyvbjerg's data show optimism about favorable trends is precisely what reference-class forecasting exists to discipline. Public over-promises additionally create escalation-of-commitment dynamics.
  Specific risks: ISME-style deadlines are missed publicly; the single human gate becomes the overload point; sunk-cost escalation diverts effort to saving the promise; credibility of the broader program is spent on a schedule artifact.
  Mitigations available: Reference-class forecast from the project's own completed-task history (actual vs estimated durations already on record); promise the P50 externally while planning the stretch internally; pre-commit a minimum-viable-deliverable fallback; cap the cost of a miss in advance.
  STEELMAN:
    Strongest counterargument: The classic literature predates genuinely accelerating agentic capability: if per-week throughput is rising rapidly, naive extrapolation from past completion rates is pessimistically biased, and a target that looks like over-promising against today's capability may be a calibrated forecast against the trend. Stretch goals also have documented upside for innovation and energy when self-efficacy is high — which this dyad's recent delivery record arguably supplies (the Sitkin success conditions may actually hold here).
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: Capability growth must be measured, not assumed; the over-promise must be sized to the measured trend with a buffer; recent-performance and slack conditions (Sitkin) must actually obtain; a fallback deliverable must exist so a miss is survivable.
    How to test: Backtest: compare past C2A2 promises against deliveries to compute the historical over/under-delivery ratio. If the record does not show systematic over-delivery, the "firm expectation" is empirically unfounded for this team.
