  Summary: The literature does not say a scholar's own pages are worthless; it says voluntary
    author-maintained scholarly surfaces are incompletely filled at a measured, stable rate and that what
    does get posted is a biased sample of what was produced. That is enough to refute "exhausts the
    question" without touching the checked-zero standard. The more decisive finding is in-register: two
    ACTIVE premises (097, 140) state the exact repair — name the metric by its channel and disclose the
    scope gap — and the run that produced "both traditions are genuinely quiet" cited neither. The
    quantity that would settle the completeness limb empirically does not exist in the literature and
    14b's n=3-per-thinker test is cheaper than any search that could approximate it.

  Specific risks: If this presumption is false, the estate's inbound channel for eleven of fourteen
    traditions is reporting a *surface-scoped* null as a *tradition-scoped* null. Downstream, coverage
    claims inherit a completeness the surfaces cannot support, and the error is silent by construction —
    a missed third-party-hosted item produces exactly the same observable as a genuinely quiet week.
    Compounding risk: repeated quiet weeks harden into a prior that the tradition is dormant, which
    lowers the search effort applied to it, which lowers detection further.

  Mitigations available:
    - Rename the output, not the method. "Quiet on the checked surfaces (own pages + N feeds), as of
      DATE" is one sentence and discharges PREMISE-097 and PREMISE-140 at zero cost.
    - Add one non-author-controlled surface per thinker — a host institution's events/media page, or a
      single Google Scholar / OpenAlex author query — as a second channel with disjoint provenance
      (PREMISE-096's independence condition).
    - Run 14b's n=3 test. It is the only thing that converts this from transfer evidence to a number.

  STEELMAN:
    Item: PRESUMPTION-947
    Strongest counterargument: For the specific population C2A2 monitors — senior, self-presenting public
      intellectuals with maintained personal sites and lab pages — the incentive structure is the
      opposite of the one Harnad measured. Harnad's 15% concerns depositing an already-published article
      into a repository nobody reads, a chore with no personal return. Posting a new talk or preprint on
      one's own page IS the personal return; it is self-promotion, and these are people whose public
      profile is part of their work. Furthermore, the FROM-herself filter is not an accident of
      convenience: it is the estate's guard against ingesting commentary *about* a thinker as if it were
      the thinker, and any widening of surfaces reintroduces exactly that contamination. A checked zero
      over a narrow, high-precision surface is a better epistemic object than an unchecked
      non-zero over a wide, low-precision one.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (a) the thinkers in scope must in fact maintain their
      pages at a high update rate — testable; (b) the classes of output that bypass own-pages (host-posted
      seminars, organiser-posted talks, coauthored preprints under a colleague's account) must either be
      rare or must reliably surface on the pages later, so the null is late rather than wrong; (c) the
      report must name its surfaces, so that even if (a) and (b) fail, no downstream consumer is misled.
      (c) alone is sufficient for safety and costs one sentence.
    How to test: 14b's design, unimproved. Take three thinkers; for each, find three appearances from the
      last twelve months located via a non-author-controlled route (host institution page, conference
      programme, arXiv/PhilPapers coauthor listing); check whether each appears on the thinker's own
      pages and, if so, with what lag. n=9 gives a crude hit-rate and, more usefully, a lag distribution,
      which is the quantity that distinguishes "incomplete" from "slow."

  Search scope: comprehensive for the transferable question, preliminary for the exact question — I
    searched bibliographic-coverage studies (Scopus/WoS/Crossref/Dimensions comparisons), self-archiving
    and IR deposit-rate literature, ORCID completeness studies, grey-literature/IR content-type
    distributions, and outbreak-surveillance gap literature for the structural analogue. I found no study
    measuring academic personal-webpage completeness directly and I do not believe one exists.
