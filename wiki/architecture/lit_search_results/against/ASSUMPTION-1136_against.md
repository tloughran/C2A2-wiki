  Sources:
    1. Thinking Machines Lab, 2025. "Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference." (technical blog, widely replicated) and supporting arXiv work on non-determinism of LLMs under repetition (e.g. arXiv:2509.09705, "The Non-Determinism of Small LLMs: Evidence of Low Answer Consistency in Repetition Trials of Standard Multiple-Choice Benchmarks"). — Establishes that temperature-zero decoding makes only the token-selection rule deterministic; the system remains nondeterministic because dynamic batch size changes reduction-kernel order, so identical prompts yield different outputs across runs. Consequence: two occurrences four days apart neither establish nor could refute "reproducible" in any technical sense; the run-to-run channel is noisy by construction.
    2. Manakul, P., Liusie, A., Gales, M.J.F., 2023. "SelfCheckGPT: Zero-Resource Black-Box Hallucination Detection for Generative Large Language Models." EMNLP 2023. — The central premise is the inverse of the item's inference: when a model has genuine knowledge, sampled responses are consistent; hallucinations are detected precisely because they vary across stochastic samples. Under this framework, an output that recurs stably across independent runs is weak evidence of fabrication and is more consistent with the model having a stable (possibly wrong) source or a stable memorised association.
    3. Walters, W.H., Wilder, E.I., 2023. "Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT." Scientific Reports 13:14045. — 636 citations across 84 papers on 42 topics: 55% of GPT-3.5 citations fabricated vs 18% of GPT-4; 43% of real GPT-3.5 citations vs 24% of real GPT-4 citations contained substantive errors. Two challenges follow. First, fabrication rate varies by an order of magnitude with model version, so "the same fabrication a fourth time in five days" is confounded with model/version/routing drift unless version is pinned. Second, roughly a quarter of non-fabricated citations still carry substantive errors — so an author-list guard operating on real sources has a large residual.
    4. Liu, N.F., Zhang, T., Liang, P., 2023. "Evaluating Verifiability in Generative Search Engines." Findings of EMNLP 2023, pp. 7001–7025. — Human audit of Bing Chat, NeevaAI, perplexity.ai and YouChat found only 51.5% of generated sentences fully supported by their citations and only 74.5% of citations supporting their associated sentence. Attribution failure in search-summarised sources is the modal case, not an anomaly; four rejections in a day is inside this rate rather than evidence of a distinguishable reproducible defect.
    5. Rashkin, H., Nikolaev, V., Lamm, M., Aroyo, L., Collins, M., Das, D., Petrov, S., Singh Tomar, G., Turc, I., Reitter, D., 2023. "Measuring Attribution in Natural Language Generation Models." Computational Linguistics 49(4):777–840. — The AIS framework requires that output be verified as entailed by an independent provided source. An author-list check is not an AIS check: it verifies a metadata field, not whether the source supports the attributed statement. The guard's efficacy is therefore bounded well below the failure class it is being used to police.
    6. Simkin, M.V., Roychowdhury, V.P., 2003/2005. "Read Before You Cite!" Complex Systems 14(3). — Statistical analysis of repeated misprints in citations concludes ~80% of citations are copied from other papers' reference lists rather than read; in one heavily cited paper, 196 citations contained misprints of which only 45 were distinct, the most popular repeating 78 times. This is a direct empirical model of a reproducible attribution error whose source is the corpus, not the citer — an alternative explanation for a recurring misattribution.
    7. Circular reporting / "citogenesis": Wikipedia, "Circular reporting" and "Wikipedia:List of citogenesis incidents"; documented cases include the false claim that Mike Pompeo served in the Gulf War (repeated by the Wall Street Journal and the New Yorker before CIA correction in 2018) and the coati "Brazilian aardvark" propagation into The Independent, the Telegraph, the Daily Mail and university-press works. — Demonstrates that a false attribution can be stably present across multiple apparently independent retrievable sources. A retrieval-stage system encountering such a corpus will reproduce the error every time, and that reproducibility is a property of the world, not of the model.
    8. Greenberg, S.A., 2009. "How citation distortions create unfounded authority: analysis of a citation network." BMJ 339:b2680. — Documents "invention": conversion of hypothesis into fact through citation alone, and amplification by papers presenting no data. Provides a human-generated mechanism by which a specific false attribution becomes stably retrievable.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate-to-Strong (on the reproducibility inference specifically; the underlying frequency of attribution error is not challenged)

  Summary: The item's factual core — that author-attribution errors keep occurring and that they are being caught — is corroborated rather than challenged by the literature; measured fabrication rates of 18–55% and citation-support rates near 51–75% make repeated occurrence expected. What the literature challenges is the escalation to "demonstrably reproducible." Two observations four days apart cannot establish reproducibility in a system whose outputs are not bit-stable even at temperature zero, and the sampling-consistency framework behind SelfCheckGPT treats recurrence as evidence *against* free-floating hallucination rather than for it. The stronger alternative explanation is that the misattribution is stably present in the retrieval corpus — the Simkin/Roychowdhury misprint-copying result and the documented citogenesis cases show exactly this pattern at scale. That distinction matters operationally: if the error is corpus-resident, the model-side guard will never eliminate it, and repeated rejection is the guard working, not the defect worsening. Separately, an author-list check is a metadata check, not an attribution check in the AIS sense, so it cannot cover the ~24% of real citations that carry substantive errors.

  Specific risks:
    - Misdiagnosis of locus: treating a corpus-resident error as a generation-stage fabrication directs remediation at the wrong layer, and the error will keep recurring regardless of prompt or guard changes.
    - Unfalsifiable claim: "reproducible" cannot be confirmed or disconfirmed by non-recurrence under batch-dependent nondeterminism; the label will accumulate without an exit condition.
    - Version confounding: Walters & Wilder show fabrication rates differ threefold across model versions; a cross-day "same failure" claim silently assumes a pinned model.
    - Guard overreach: author-list verification will be credited with covering attribution failure generally, while AIS-style support verification remains undone.
    - Base-rate blindness: four rejections in a day, without a denominator of attributions checked, cannot be compared to the ambient 18–55% fabrication and ~25% substantive-error rates.

  Mitigations available:
    - Distinguish and log the two failure modes separately: non-existent source vs. real source with corrupted attribution. Walters & Wilder show they behave differently and respond differently to model changes.
    - Before labelling anything reproducible, run N≥10 repeated trials with model version, temperature and retrieval snapshot pinned, and report the recurrence fraction rather than a binary.
    - Check whether the misattribution is present in the retrieval corpus. If it is, the finding is a corpus-poisoning report, not a hallucination report, and belongs in a source-quality register.
    - Upgrade the guard from author-list matching to an AIS-style check: does the identified source entail the attributed statement.
    - Publish a denominator: attributions checked per day, so rejection counts become a rate.

  STEELMAN:
    Item: ASSUMPTION-1136
    Strongest counterargument: Even if "reproducible" is technically overstated, the operational content of the claim survives: a specific attribution error has now been rejected by the same rule on two separate dates, which is enough to justify promoting it from incident to known-hazard and adding it to a standing trap list. Under nondeterminism the correct inference is not that recurrence proves reproducibility but that recurrence raises the posterior enough to warrant a permanent guard, and permanent guards are cheap. The literature's genuine contribution here is not to weaken the item but to redirect it: the question that must be answered before remediation is whether the false attribution exists in the retrieval corpus, because that determines whether any model-side fix can work.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (a) model version, decoding parameters and retrieval snapshot are pinned and logged for every run, so cross-day comparison is meaningful; (b) the corpus is checked for the misattribution before the model is blamed; (c) the guard verifies source-supports-statement, not merely author-list-matches; (d) rejection counts are reported against a denominator; (e) "reproducible" carries a stated trial count and recurrence fraction rather than being asserted from two sightings.
    How to test: Pin the configuration and run the triggering query 20 times; record the recurrence fraction of the specific misattribution. In parallel, query the retrieval corpus directly for the false author-work pairing. Four outcomes discriminate cleanly: high recurrence + present in corpus = corpus poisoning (model-side fix futile); high recurrence + absent from corpus = memorised fabrication (weight-level, guard permanently); low recurrence + absent = stochastic hallucination (SelfCheckGPT-style sampling guard appropriate); low recurrence + present = retrieval-ranking instability. Only the second outcome supports the item as stated.
