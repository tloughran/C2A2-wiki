  Sources:
    1. Beyer, B., Jones, C., Petoff, J. & Murphy, N.R. (eds.) 2016. Site Reliability Engineering: How Google Runs Production Systems. O'Reilly. Ch. "Effective Troubleshooting" and Introduction. — Directly challenges the presumption's implicit standard that a causal link must be established before a fault is filed against a suspected cause. The chapter's stated method is to "make the system work as well as it can under the circumstances" first: "stopping the bleeding should be your first priority; you aren't helping users if the system dies while you're root-causing," and "fixing the proximate cause(s) needn't always wait for root-causing or postmortem writing." Provisional attribution with diagnosis explicitly deferred is the documented practice, not a lapse. The same book supplies the base rate that makes temporal co-occurrence a legitimate prior rather than a fallacy: "SRE has found that roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system." Two faults surfacing in the same generator on the same day, after the same change, have a materially elevated joint prior of sharing a cause, and the reported wording ("I did not diagnose why") labels the attribution as unverified rather than asserting it.
    2. Chen, J., He, X., Lin, Q., Xu, Y., Zhang, H., Hao, D., Gao, F., Xu, Z., Dang, Y. & Zhang, D. 2019. "An Empirical Investigation of Incident Triage for Online Service Systems." ICSE-SEIP 2019 (41st International Conference on Software Engineering: Software Engineering in Practice), pp. 111-120. IEEE/ACM. DOI 10.1109/ICSE-SEIP.2019.00020. — Study of 20 large-scale online services at Microsoft. Between 4.11% and 91.58% of incident reports are reassigned at least once, and reassignment raises incident-triage time by up to 10.16x. The field's empirical picture is that first-pass attribution is frequently wrong and is corrected by re-routing; the process is built to absorb provisional misattribution, not to prevent it by withholding attribution. This challenges the presumption's cost model in a second way: the expensive event is mis-routing an item, and creating a separate top-priority item for every unexplained signal multiplies the number of items exposed to that cost rather than avoiding it.
    3. Tariq, S., Baruwal Chhetri, M., Nepal, S. & Paris, C. 2025. "Alert Fatigue in Security Operations Centres: Research Challenges and Opportunities." ACM Computing Surveys 57(9), Article 224. DOI 10.1145/3723158. — Systematises alert fatigue as a three-dimensional failure: cognitive desensitisation, operational degradation and analyst burnout, driven by alert volume and false-positive load (reported false-positive rates reaching very high levels in surveyed SOCs, with analysts spending a large share of time on non-events). The presumption's remedy - promote the undiagnosed null to its own item at the same priority as the known defect, and add an existence check on scheduled outputs - is exactly the volume-increasing move this literature identifies as the mechanism by which real signals stop being read. The cost of over-triage is not neutral; it is a documented degradation of the detection capability the presumption is trying to protect.
    4. Ancker, J.S., Edwards, A., Nosal, S., Hauser, D., Mauer, E. & Kaushal, R. 2017. "Effects of workload, work complexity, and repeated alerts on alert fatigue in a clinical decision support system." BMC Medical Informatics and Decision Making 17:36. DOI 10.1186/s12911-017-0430-8. — Empirical test (112 ambulatory clinicians, EHR data Jan 2010-Jun 2013) of two mechanisms of alert fatigue: cognitive overload from workload, work complexity and the effort of distinguishing informative from uninformative alerts; and desensitisation from repeated exposure to the same alert. This is the quantitative backing for the claim that raising the count of separately-tracked, individually-prioritised signals degrades response rather than improving it. It bears directly on the "same queue at the same priority" objection: the literature's finding is that undifferentiated priority across many items is itself the fatigue mechanism, which is an argument for consolidation, not against it.
    5. Ghosh, S., Shetty, M., Bansal, C. & Nath, S. 2022. "How to Fight Production Incidents? An Empirical Study on a Large-scale Cloud Service." SoCC 2022 (13th ACM Symposium on Cloud Computing), Best Paper Award. DOI 10.1145/3542929.3563482. — Study of hundreds of recent high-severity incidents and postmortems in a cloud service used by hundreds of millions of users, answering why incidents occurred, how they were resolved, and what process gaps caused delayed response. Its headline methodological contribution is a "novel multi-dimensional analysis that correlates different troubleshooting stages (detection, root-causing and mitigation)" - i.e. the field's advance is to correlate signals across stages, which is the move the presumption objects to, and the field's diagnosed problem is delayed response, which separate-tracking without diagnosis does not fix.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate

  Summary: The presumption's substantive request - find out why nothing was generated, and add an existence check - survives the search intact and is independently endorsed by the watchdog/dead-man's-switch pattern. What the literature challenges is the epistemic complaint wrapped around it. First, provisional causal attribution pending diagnosis is not a lapse but the documented SRE method: stabilise and attribute now, root-cause later, and fix proximate causes without waiting (Beyer et al. 2016). The report's own phrasing, "I did not diagnose why," marks the attribution as unverified, which is what a labelled working hypothesis looks like. Second, temporal and component co-occurrence is a defensible prior, not a fallacy: roughly 70% of outages trace to a change in a live system (Beyer et al. 2016), so two faults in the same generator on the same day after the same change genuinely do have an elevated probability of sharing a cause, and alert correlation and deduplication - grouping co-occurring symptoms into one incident - is the industry's standard first move rather than a shortcut. Third, the presumption prices the cost of consolidation but not the cost of its alternative: alert fatigue is an empirically measured degradation across security operations (Tariq et al. 2025) and clinical decision support (Ancker et al. 2017), and the mechanism is precisely a proliferation of separately-tracked items at undifferentiated priority. Fourth, the incident-triage evidence shows that first-pass attribution is wrong between 4% and 92% of the time and is corrected by reassignment at up to 10.16x cost (Chen et al. 2019) - the field's answer is fast correction, not withheld attribution. The presumption is right that the wrong-pids defect and the fails-to-run null are different fault classes; it is not established that filing them together, with the causal claim flagged as undiagnosed, is an error.

  Specific risks: If the presumption is over-read, C2A2 adopts a rule that no fault may be filed against a suspected cause until the causal link is established. That rule is unimplementable in incident practice (Chen et al. 2019 shows attribution is usually provisional and often wrong) and it would forbid the correlation across detection, diagnosis and mitigation stages that the current literature identifies as the way to shorten response (Ghosh et al. 2022). It also implies a policy of promoting every unexplained signal to its own high-priority item, which is the documented generator of alert fatigue and therefore erodes the very sensitivity to silent nulls the presumption is defending. If the presumption is under-read, the real hazard it names goes unaddressed: if the 04:38 failure is in the scheduler rather than the generator, the pids fix will be applied, the null will be assumed closed, and other scheduled outputs may already be failing silently with no observer - and the artifact-based reconstruction method (ASSUMPTION-561) structurally cannot see an absence it was never told to expect.

  Mitigations available: The two positions reconcile cleanly if attribution and verification are separated rather than gated. (a) Keep the consolidated filing but attach an explicit confidence marker and a falsifier - "provisionally attributed to the pids defect; falsified if a review page fails to appear after the pids fix lands" - which is Google SRE practice and costs nothing in queue volume. (b) Add the existence check as a single watchdog on the whole scheduled-output set rather than one absence alert per artifact: the watchdog/dead-man's-switch pattern is designed exactly for the "nothing arrived" case and keeps the added alert count at one, which is the alert-fatigue-compatible form of the presumption's demand. Note the boundary condition the operational literature attaches: an untested dead man's switch is worse than none, because it converts a silent gap into false confidence - so the check must itself be exercised on a schedule. (c) Run the cheap disambiguating test before deciding priority at all: check whether other 04:38-scheduled outputs are also missing today. If they are, the fault is in the scheduler and the presumption is vindicated on the substance; if only the review page is missing, the co-occurrence prior stands and consolidation was correct. That single check resolves the dispute at a fraction of the cost of the queue-policy argument.

  STEELMAN:
    Item: PRESUMPTION-570
    Strongest counterargument (against the presumption): Attributing an undiagnosed fault to the loud fault next to it, while explicitly recording that the cause was not diagnosed, is exactly what incident response is supposed to look like. Google's SRE practice is to stop the bleeding before root-causing and to fix proximate causes without waiting for a completed causal account; alert correlation exists because a single cause routinely emits many co-occurring symptoms, and the base rate is on the correlator's side, with roughly 70% of outages traceable to a change in a live system. The presumption's alternative - a separate item at equal priority for every unexplained null, plus a new absence alert - is the documented mechanism of alert fatigue, empirically shown to produce desensitisation and missed real signals in both security operations and clinical decision support; and the incident-triage evidence shows the field already tolerates and corrects a high rate of first-pass misattribution rather than trying to prevent it. So the presumption demands a standard of causal establishment that operational practice does not use, and prescribes a remedy whose costs it has not counted.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: the consolidated filing carries an explicit provisional marker and a stated falsifier, so it is a hypothesis rather than a closed finding; the null is re-checked after the pids fix lands, so a wrong attribution self-corrects within one cycle; and a single watchdog covers the whole scheduled-output set, so the absence case is observable without multiplying alerts. If all three hold, the presumption's substantive concern is met without adopting its epistemic gate.
    How to test: run the disambiguation directly. Inspect the 04:38 scheduled task's run record and check whether any other scheduled outputs are missing for the same date. Then apply the pids fix and observe whether a review page appears on the next scheduled run. Three outcomes discriminate: (i) other outputs also missing -> scheduler-level fault, the causal link was wrong, the presumption is confirmed and consolidation cost a cycle; (ii) only the review page missing and it returns after the pids fix -> the co-occurrence attribution was correct and the challenge stands; (iii) only the review page missing and it does not return after the fix -> two independent faults, which is the presumption's scenario but reached by evidence rather than by a filing rule. Record which outcome obtains; it settles the general policy question with a single observation.
