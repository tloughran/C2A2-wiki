  Sources:
    1. Little, J.D.C. 1961. "A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = λW." Operations Research. [details unverified — canonical work, stated from background knowledge; not retrieved or read in this session.] The load-bearing content is not the formula but its precondition: L = λW is a statement about long-run averages in a system that reaches a steady state. Stability requires the arrival rate not to exceed the maximum service rate. Where λ > μ_max, no steady state exists, L grows without bound, and the law says nothing that can be used to plan drainage. A per-run cap fixes μ; if λ exceeds it, the queue is undrainable *by construction*, and reporting that fact is a tautology rather than a finding.
    2. Kingman, J.F.C. 1961. "The single server queue in heavy traffic." Proceedings of the Cambridge Philosophical Society. [details unverified — canonical work, stated from background knowledge; not retrieved.] The relevant result is the ρ/(1−ρ) scaling of expected wait with utilisation: as utilisation approaches one, waiting time diverges non-linearly. The consequence for C2A2 is that treating a cap as a constant is not a neutral choice near saturation — small changes in the cap have large, non-linear effects on queue behaviour, which is exactly the signature of a parameter rather than a constant.
    3. Goldratt, E.M. & Cox, J. 1984. *The Goal*. [details unverified — canonical work, stated from background knowledge; not retrieved.] Theory of Constraints: system throughput is set by the binding constraint, and effort expended anywhere other than the constraint does not increase throughput. Applied here: agents optimising within a fixed per-run cap are optimising a non-binding resource; the cap is the constraint, and it is the only place work on the problem can succeed.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate

  Summary: The analytic core of the challenge is not in doubt even though its citations are unverified in this session: a per-run cap is a service-rate parameter, and the stability condition for any work queue is a relation between the arrival rate and that parameter. If arrivals exceed the capped service rate, the queue is undrainable as a matter of arithmetic, Little's law does not apply because no steady state exists, and reporting the backlog conveys no information that the cap and the arrival rate did not already determine. Treating the cap as a constant therefore does not merely miss an option — it relocates a design decision into the environment, where it becomes unfalsifiable and unowned. Kingman's utilisation scaling sharpens this: near saturation the system's behaviour is highly sensitive to the cap, which is the empirical signature of a parameter rather than a constant, and Song (2026) supplies a concrete contemporary instance in which capacity configuration (parallel vs sequential) changes the outcome substantially. On 14b's second question — who may adjust a rate parameter in a system whose workers are barred from doing so — the honest answer from what I could reach is that the literature I retrieved locates this in governance (Pinto) and, cybernetically, in the requirement that some level of the system hold an actuator over the variable (Ashby). I did not reach the primary sources that would let me state this with confidence. I am recording PARTIALLY-CHALLENGED rather than CHALLENGED for this reason: the substance points to a real and probably serious challenge, but the evidentiary base assembled here is below the standard the other three items met.

  Specific risks: If per-run caps are parameters and are being treated as constants, then: (a) the estate is generating recurring reports whose content is analytically implied by two numbers it already has, consuming attention on a tautology — and three same-day instances suggests this is a repeating cost, not a one-off; (b) the true decision (what should the cap be, given the arrival rate) is never framed, so it is never made, and the backlog grows monotonically with no one accountable for the growth; (c) near saturation the non-linear scaling means the system's behaviour will degrade far faster than the linear intuition suggests, so the point at which the backlog becomes visibly unrecoverable will arrive abruptly; (d) agents will optimise within the cap — the non-binding resource — producing effort that cannot affect throughput, which is the Theory of Constraints failure mode and which will read locally as diligence; (e) this item compounds with PRESUMPTION-861: a queue-depth report filed into a channel that has no re-scoping authority is a flag whose duty no one can discharge, so the two presumptions together produce an unbounded backlog documented in an unbounded stack of reports; (f) it also compounds with PRESUMPTION-860 in the opposite direction — a cap treated as a constant plus a yield expectation treated as a floor squeezes the agent from both sides, and the only remaining slack is item quality.

  Mitigations available:
    - State the stability condition explicitly and monitor it: measure λ (arrival rate into the queue) and μ (achieved throughput under the cap), and surface λ/μ as a first-class metric. Where λ/μ > 1 the correct output is a re-scoping request, not a backlog report. [Little's law preconditions — details unverified.]
    - Assign an owner for the cap. The presumption survives because no role is defined as holding the parameter; Pinto (2014) is the argument that this is a governance-level assignment, and Ashby's requisite-variety condition is the reason an agent without that actuator cannot be held responsible for the outcome.
    - Add capacity by widening rather than deepening where the choice exists — Song (2026, §6.4, Appendix C) is direct evidence that parallel independent capacity outperforms additional sequential passes at equal compute.
    - Distinguish reports that convey new information from reports that restate a known constraint, so that a recurring undrainable-queue notice escalates on its second occurrence rather than repeating (the same terminating-condition remedy identified under PRESUMPTION-861).
    - Consider an explicit WIP limit with a defined admission policy, so that when λ exceeds μ the system sheds or defers arrivals under a stated rule rather than silently accumulating. [Kanban/flow WIP-limit literature — not searched this session; flagged as the obvious next query.]

  STEELMAN:
    Item: PRESUMPTION-863
    Strongest counterargument: Caps exist for reasons — cost, context window, crash-proofing, blast radius — and a worker unilaterally raising its own cap is exactly the failure the cap was installed to prevent, so "workers are barred from adjusting it" is a correct design, not a defect. The defect is narrower and therefore harder to see: the system has a rule forbidding the worker from changing the parameter but no rule routing the change request to anyone who can. That converts an adjustable parameter into an environmental constant by omission rather than by decision, and the arithmetic is unforgiving — where the arrival rate exceeds the capped service rate, the queue cannot drain, no effort inside the cap can make it drain, and every report of the backlog is a restatement of the two numbers that produced it. Three same-day instances indicate the system is already spending real attention on that restatement. What makes this a presumption rather than a policy is that nobody has had to answer the question it forecloses: given that the queue cannot drain at this cap, what is the cap supposed to be?
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (1) The arrival rate is genuinely below the capped service rate, so the queue is transiently rather than structurally backed up and Little's law applies — this is measurable and should be measured before anything else. (2) There exists an identified party with authority to change the cap, and a defined route by which an agent's re-scoping request reaches them. (3) The cap is documented as a parameter with a stated rationale and a review trigger, so that treating it as fixed is a decision on the record rather than an assumption in the plumbing. (4) Where the cap genuinely cannot move (a hard external constraint), the system has an admission or shedding policy, so that an over-subscribed queue degrades under a stated rule rather than accumulating silently.
    How to test: Straightforwardly testable from existing logs, and the test should precede any further literature work. (a) Measure λ and μ from the queue's own history over a representative window; if λ > μ the presumption is falsified analytically and no further evidence is needed. (b) Plot backlog depth over time: monotone growth is the structural case, sawtooth is the transient case, and these are visually distinguishable. (c) Trace the three same-day instances forward: did any of them result in a cap change, a re-scoping, or any state change at all? If none did, the routing gap is demonstrated. (d) Sensitivity check: re-run a bounded sample at 1.5x and 2x the cap and measure throughput and quality. If throughput scales and quality holds, the cap is a parameter with headroom; if quality degrades, the cap is doing real work and the correct response is admission control rather than a raise.
