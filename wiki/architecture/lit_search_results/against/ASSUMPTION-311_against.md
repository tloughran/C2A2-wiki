  Sources:
    1. Stegenga, J., 2009. "Robustness, Discordance, and Relevance." Philosophy of Science (supplement), 76(5): 650–661. — Stegenga argues that robustness arguments are "worse than useless" when multimodal evidence is discordant, and that apparent convergence can reflect shared problematic background assumptions rather than independently confirming a hypothesis. For convergence to carry evidential weight, the modes must be shown to be genuinely independent.

    2. Stegenga, J. and Menon, T., 2017. "Robustness and Independent Evidence." Philosophy of Science 84(3): 414–435. — Extends the critique: pseudo-robustness arises when diverse lines of evidence share a hidden common premise. If CRm, Tononi's IIT, and computationalism share foundational commitments (e.g., functionalism, the primacy of information, or structuralism about phenomenal properties), their apparent convergence may reflect this hidden overlap rather than independently pointing to the same truth.

    3. Orzack, S. H. and Sober, E., 1993. "A Critical Assessment of Levins' The Strategy of Model Building in Population Biology (1966)." Quarterly Review of Biology 68(4): 533–546. — Classic critique arguing that derivational robustness (the same result obtained from multiple models) provides no empirical confirmation, only clarifies what follows from shared modelling assumptions. This limits the inferential weight one can place on the M3–M4 convergence claim if all three starting points are theoretical frameworks rather than independent empirical streams.

    4. Weisberg, M., 2006. "Robustness Analysis." Philosophy of Science 73(5): 730–742. — While partially rehabilitating robustness analysis, Weisberg notes it only increases confidence when the independent models are genuinely uncorrelated — a condition that must be demonstrated, not assumed. The three frames cited (CRm, phenomenology, computation) are all constructed within an intellectually interconnected discourse; genuine independence is not self-evident.

    5. Chang, H., 2012. Is Water H2O? Evidence, Realism and Pluralism. Springer. — Examines how apparently converging theoretical frameworks can reflect shared conceptual commitments of a research community rather than world-tracking convergence. Applied here: all three starting points may be downstream of 20th-century information-theoretic assumptions.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate

  Summary: The core challenge is that convergence across CRm, IIT, and computationalism may constitute pseudo-robustness if these frameworks share hidden structural premises (e.g., a shared commitment to integrated information, structural realism about minds, or the explanatory priority of functional organisation). Stegenga's discordance criterion and Orzack/Sober's model-building critique together establish that derivational convergence is evidentially inert unless independence is actively demonstrated. The claim that this constitutes "M3–M4 kind convergence evidence" imports a significance it has not yet earned. However, the challenge is only moderate because there are respects in which the three traditions genuinely differ (ontological commitment, directionality of explanation) and because Weisberg provides tools for partially rescuing robustness arguments.

  Specific risks: If convergence is pseudo-robust, the M3–M4 transition milestones have insufficient evidential basis, and the system may be advancing through maturity stages on the strength of a dialectical illusion. The three starting points feeling independent may be an artefact of disciplinary separation rather than genuine independence of evidence.

  Mitigations available: Explicitly audit the hidden premises of each starting point and map which are shared versus distinct. Where premises diverge substantially, convergence carries real weight; where they converge on the same foundational assumptions, treat the convergence as coherence rather than confirmation. This would require a structured philosophical analysis as a prerequisite for the M3–M4 milestone certification.

  STEELMAN:
    Strongest counterargument: The three frameworks (CRm, IIT-style phenomenology, and the computational frame) originate from genuinely different disciplinary traditions — metaphysics/philosophy of mind, consciousness science/neuroscience, and computer science respectively — and pursue different explanatory goals with different empirical anchors. Where all three arrive at structurally similar positions (e.g., the importance of integration, irreducibility of experience, certain agency conditions), this cross-disciplinary convergence is harder to explain away as hidden-premise overlap than convergence within a single tradition. This is arguably the best case for treating it as consilience.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: A premise audit showing that the convergence is not driven by a single shared theoretical commitment (such as a functionalist background assumption) that all three frameworks happen to import. If at least two of the three can arrive at the convergent claim without that shared commitment, the robustness argument survives.
    How to test: For each pair of starting points, identify which premises are necessary for the convergent conclusion and check for overlap. A structured premise-map exercise, ideally done before M4 milestone sign-off, would serve this function.
