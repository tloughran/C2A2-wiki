The strongest challenge is the generic rules-versus-standards literature (Kaplow 1992; Bodansky 2003), which holds that for rare, novel, high-variance situations situated judgment can beat a pre-written rule. AoIR 3.0 shows a mature normative field choosing deliberative process over fixed rules, though that process is itself a stated, shared framework, which blunts the challenge. On the attribution limb I found only an organisation's self-description and an unrelated medieval-compilation literature; no code settling byline for curated devotional material, and I did not find anything specific on Armas. Search scope: preliminary, broader search recommended, especially for journalism and biography ethics (public-figure exceptions), scholarly editing norms, and AI-agent governance literature.

  Specific risks: If the presumption is false, a written policy is unnecessary overhead, could be applied rigidly to grief or biographical material it did not anticipate, and could freeze agents out of legitimate treatment of public work. The main practical risk is a policy drafted from two same-day rulings overfitting to them.

  Mitigations available: Make any policy a short standard (principles plus a documented-judgment log) rather than a rule list; require agents to record each ruling and its reasoning so that precedent accumulates, then codify only recurring patterns (Kaplow's recurrence logic); review the policy after a fixed number of cases.

STEELMAN:
  Item: PRESUMPTION-1047
  Strongest counterargument: Rules pay off only where situations recur and can be foreseen; grief-related and biographical material about particular living scholars is rare, idiosyncratic and context-laden, which is exactly where Bodansky and Cardozo say rules written without experience misfire. The best-developed field-level code (AoIR 3.0) chose structured deliberation over fixed rules for just this reason, and Zimmer notes that blanket prohibitions can be paternalistic. Individual agents exercising documented restraint, with rulings accumulating as precedent, may therefore be a fully adequate substitute, and a premature policy built on two same-day rulings could be worse than none. Separately, material that subjects have themselves made public in their own voice arguably carries little residual obligation beyond accurate attribution, which existing editorial convention already governs.
  What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: Agent restraint must be consistent across agents and runs, rulings must be logged and discoverable, the same-day rulings must not conflict, and a human review step must exist for new categories. The tracked subjects must also be unlikely to be harmed by a divergent ruling between agents.
  How to test: Audit a sample of past agent rulings on personal or grief material for inconsistency; present the same borderline cases to several agents or runs and measure agreement; compare outcomes of the two same-day rulings against a candidate policy.
