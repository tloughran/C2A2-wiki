  Sources:
    1. Pitkin RM, Branagan MA, Burmeister LF. 1999. "Accuracy of data in abstracts of published research
       articles." JAMA 281:1110-1111. [snippet/secondary] Across six major general medical journals,
       18-68% of abstracts contained data that was inconsistent with the body or absent from it. A
       secondary source reports a JAMA before/after comparison falling from 52% to 20% after abstract
       standards were introduced; this is unverified. Bearing against A: even in top venues, a large
       share of abstracts do not faithfully represent the paper.
    2. Boutron I, Dutton S, Ravaud P, Altman DG. 2010. JAMA 303(20). [snippet] In 72 RCTs with a
       nonsignificant primary outcome, the abstract conclusions contained spin in 58.3%. Boutron et al.
       2014 (J Clin Oncol, SPIIN RCT, n=300 clinicians) [snippet] found that spin in an abstract raised
       perceived benefit (3.6 vs 2.9 on a 0-10 scale). Replications are mixed: an effect in
       physiotherapy, no convincing effect in clinical psychology. Bearing against A: abstracts are
       where overstatement concentrates, and a reader of the abstract alone absorbs it.
    3. Cochrane Colloquium 2015 (Vienna): "Agreement of results data between conference abstracts and
       full reports of RCTs" [abstract]. Of 230 abstract-to-publication pairs, 36.9% had changed primary
       outcomes. Among comparable pairs, only 19.4% agreed fully, 71.9% showed quantitative discordance,
       and 8.7% showed qualitative discordance (a change of direction). A Pain-congress study of
       systematic-review abstracts found discordance in 42% and a change of direction in 17%. Scherer et
       al., Cochrane MR000005 (2018), document that many abstracts are never fully published. Bearing
       against A: talk outlines and conference abstracts are an even weaker proxy than journal abstracts.
    4. Title/abstract screening literature (e.g. Dove Press Clin Epidemiol 2013, "Titles versus titles
       and abstracts for initial screening"; Cochrane Handbook guidance as cited). [snippet] Screening on
       title and abstract achieved 100% recall of eventually included studies in the cited comparison,
       and title+abstract inter-rater agreement reached 96-97%. Standard practice relies on abstracts
       for inclusion decisions and sends "maybe" items to full text. Bearing against B: abstracts are
       fit for the purpose of deciding whether to look further, though not for extracting claims.

  Summary: The literature divides the question by purpose. For representing claims and effect sizes,
    abstracts are often unreliable: 18-68% are deficient, the majority of nonsignificant trials carry
    spin in their conclusions, and most conference abstracts differ quantitatively from the eventual
    paper. That challenges any carding step that records an abstract's claim as the paper's claim. For
    triage (is this worth reading?), systematic-review practice treats abstracts as adequate and highly
    sensitive. A blanket rule of "never card from abstracts" would therefore be over-strict. The defect
    is not carding from abstracts. It is failing to label the evidence tier.

  Specific risks: (a) Cards built from abstracts carry spun or deficient claims into the wiki at the
    same weight as claims verified against full text. (b) Talk outlines describe intended content, not
    delivered content (cf. the 37% primary-outcome change). (c) Over-correcting into a full-text-only
    rule stalls intake.

  Mitigations available: Use two-tier carding. An abstract-only card is labelled "triage/abstract-tier"
    and cannot be cited as support until full text is checked. Prioritise checks for "positive"
    conclusions on primary claims, where spin concentrates. Record the source tier in provenance.

  STEELMAN:
    Item: ASSUMPTION-1780
    Strongest counterargument (vs A): The abstract is the part of a paper most likely to be read alone
      and most likely to be spun. Its data disagrees with the body in a fifth to two-thirds of cases,
      even in elite journals. Conference abstracts and outlines usually differ from the final result,
      and sometimes in direction. A card built from an abstract records what the authors wanted
      remembered, not what they found.
    Strongest counterargument (vs B): Evidence-synthesis practice decides relevance from abstracts with
      near-complete recall. Demanding full text before carding would discard a well-validated triage
      instrument.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: Abstract-derived cards are tagged by tier, are
      excluded from support counts, and are promoted only after a full-text check.
    How to test: Sample about 20 existing abstract-carded items. Compare each against the full text, and
      record how often the card's central claim is changed, weakened, or contradicted.

    - Structured-abstract mandates improved fidelity, so abstract quality depends on the venue.
