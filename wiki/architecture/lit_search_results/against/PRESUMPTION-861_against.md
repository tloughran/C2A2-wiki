  Strength of challenge: Strong

  Summary: The literature does not treat filing as discharge, and the strongest statement of this comes from the safety-reporting field that invented the filing channel in the first place. Reporting is theorised there as the *opening* of a safety-action feedback loop, and the loop's terminating condition is a visible action communicated back to the reporter; the AHRQ PSNet material notes that only 13 US systems document such a procedure at all, and that absent feedback suppresses future reporting. The systematic-review evidence confirms that lack of feedback and insufficient organisational learning survive the digitisation of the channel — a filing system can be perfectly functional as a filing system while discharging nothing. Searching forward from Vaughan, as instructed, the descendants converge on the same point from the other side: Price & Williams's finding that deviations persist because no immediate negative outcome follows makes a ninth consecutive unactioned flag the *definition* of normalised deviance rather than evidence against it, and Pinto locates the fix in governance structure rather than reporter behaviour. On 15b's crux — is repeat-filing ever correct? — the literature's answer is a qualified yes with a sharp boundary: repeat-filing is correct when it is *escalating*, meaning each iteration routes to a different authority, carries new evidence, or is accompanied by an explicit deadline after which a different mechanism fires. Repeat-filing into the same channel with the same content and no terminating condition is not persistence; it is the futility signature that the under-reporting literature identifies, and it converts an alarm into background. I flag that the two sources that would settle this crux most directly — the stop-work-authority literature and the organisational voice/silence literature — were not reached because the search budget ran out, so the boundary condition stated here is inferred from adjacent evidence rather than read off a primary source.

  Specific risks: If filing does not discharge the duty, then five same-day instances represent five open obligations recorded as closed, and the estate's flag ledger is a systematically optimistic account of its own state. Concretely: (a) the flag count becomes a measure of diligence rather than a measure of unresolved risk, so a rising count reads as a healthy safety culture at exactly the moment it should read as an accumulating deficit; (b) the ninth consecutive flag has, by the normalisation mechanism, already stopped functioning as an alarm — subsequent filings on the same metric carry approximately zero information and consume the attention budget that a novel flag would need; (c) the 97 item-days is a measured, quantified loss with a known one-line remedy, which means the channel is demonstrably not converting information into action at any price, and any future cost-benefit argument for action will face the precedent that this one did not clear the bar; (d) the futility mechanism runs in the background — once agents observe that filing changes nothing, filing rates fall, and the estate loses the signal without ever recording that it did; (e) most seriously, there is no state in the current design that distinguishes "flagged and resolved" from "flagged nine times and untouched," which means no monitoring rule can be written over the difference.

  Mitigations available:
    - Give the channel a terminating condition: an owner, a deadline, and a defined action that fires when the deadline passes. This is the "safety action feedback loop" prescription (AHRQ PSNet) and the point on which the maternity-care systematic review (PMC12506158) finds most systems fail.
    - Close the loop visibly back to the filer, which is the intervention the under-reporting literature identifies as the one that sustains reporting rates (PMC9243549; Shorrock 2013).
    - Make repetition change the routing rather than repeat it: a second filing on an already-open flag should escalate to a different authority or a different mechanism, not append to the same queue. This is the design property whose absence 14b named.
    - Treat consecutive-filing count as a first-class monitored metric with a hard threshold. On the Price & Williams mechanism, the ordinal — ninth — is the diagnostic quantity, and it is currently visible only by manual inspection.
    - Locate the remedy in governance rather than in individual diligence (Pinto 2014): the agents filing the flags are behaving correctly given the channel they have; the defect is that the channel has no exit.
    - For the specific 97-item-day case, note the pattern the ESW-2023 material describes: throughput pressure pushing a known one-line fix past the boundary. A standing rule that a fix below some cost threshold is applied rather than filed would dissolve this class entirely.

  STEELMAN:
    Item: PRESUMPTION-861
    Strongest counterargument: A filing system's whole purpose is separation of concerns — the observer reports, an owner acts — and it is not obviously wrong for a worker barred from acting to discharge its obligation by reporting accurately. The presumption fails not because reporting is the wrong act but because the system has no second half: there is no owner, no deadline, and no mechanism that fires on non-action, so the "separation of concerns" is in fact an unbounded deferral. Price & Williams's finding gives this teeth — deviations persist precisely because nothing bad happens immediately — so a ninth consecutive flag on an unfixed metric is not evidence that the system is working slowly, it is evidence that the system has learned that not fixing carries no cost. The 97 item-days is the falsification: a measured loss, a known one-line remedy, and a channel that still did not convert. And because the presumption is unstated, no one has ever had to answer the question that would expose it — what is supposed to happen on the tenth filing?
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (1) An identified owner exists for each flag class who is both able and required to act, and whose non-action is itself visible. (2) The channel has a terminating condition — a deadline after which a *different* mechanism fires, so that repetition escalates rather than accumulates. (3) Repeat-filing carries new information (new evidence, changed severity, new affected scope); a repeat filing that is byte-identical to its predecessor should be recognised as a no-op rather than counted as diligence. (4) Filers receive feedback, so the futility mechanism does not silently erode reporting. (5) Fixes below a defined cost threshold are applied rather than filed, removing the 97-item-day class from the channel entirely.
    How to test: Testable from the existing ledger without any new instrumentation. (a) Compute the empirical discharge rate: for every flag ever filed, what fraction reached a recorded resolution, and what is the age distribution of the unresolved remainder? If the distribution has a long tail, filing is not discharging. (b) Compute the consecutive-filing distribution per metric; any metric with an ordinal above two is a channel-design failure by the criterion above. (c) Measure whether filing rate declines after unactioned flags on the same metric — that is the futility signature and it is directly observable in the timestamps. (d) For the crux specifically: classify each repeat filing as escalating (new authority, new evidence, or new severity) or non-escalating (identical content, identical route). The literature predicts the non-escalating ones have a discharge rate indistinguishable from zero; if the ledger confirms that, the boundary condition is established empirically for this estate.
