### Sources

1. **Williams, T. (2005). "The Doctrine of Univocity is True and Salutary." *Modern Theology* 21(4), 575–585.** [**VERIFIED this run — PDF fetched from frthomaswilliams.com and read in full**] — the decisive source, and it refutes clause (C) in its own words. "**The doctrine of univocity is a semantic doctrine**, and although Scotus does associate some ontological claims with that doctrine, **it is highly misleading to talk... about a 'univocalist ontology'.**" His capsule formulation, which is the exact contradictory of the vault's statement of the incompatibility: "**Univocity: Notwithstanding the irreducible ontological diversity between God and creatures**, there are concepts under whose extension both God and creatures fall, so that the corresponding predicate expressions are used with exactly the same sense in predications about God as in predications about creatures." And, against the "differ in mode or intensity" gloss specifically: "**the whole point, the very core, of Scotus's separation of the semantic from the metaphysical is precisely the claim that our possession of a concept under whose extension both God and creatures fall does not imply that there is any feature at all in extramental reality that is a common component of both God and creatures.**" Williams also quotes Scotus directly (*Ord.* 1 d.3 p.1 q.3 n.163): the relevant equivocation is "not... in the logician's sense... but in that of the ontologist, because **there is no unity of nature** in such a case... in terms of the ontological diversity of those things to which the concept is attributed, **which is compatible with there being one concept that can be abstracted from them**." The vault's premise — univocity means God and creature share one sense of "is" and differ only in mode or intensity — is the ontological reading Scotus argues at length against, and it is the reading Williams identifies as the Radical Orthodoxy misconstrual.
2. **Williams's second argument — analogy either reduces to univocity or collapses into unintelligibility.** [**VERIFIED — same fetched PDF, read in full**] — the regress argument, run over "God is wise" / "Socrates is wise": if the sense in the divine predication is wholly different, "we literally do not know what we are saying"; if it is different-but-related, we must be able to state either the sense or the relation, and either move introduces a substitute expression whose sense must in turn be univocal or start the regress again. "**We come in the end always either to univocity or to unintelligibility.**" This is a challenge to the item's framing at the root: on Williams's account analogy is not a rival ontology to univocity but a *layer above* it that requires a univocal core to be sayable at all. Note that Williams marshals Aquinas *on his own side*: ST 1a 13.5, "if everything predicated of both God and creatures were predicated equivocally, **nothing could be known or demonstrated concerning God on the basis of creatures. Instead, one would always commit the fallacy of equivocation.**"
