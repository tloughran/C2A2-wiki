  Sources:
    1. Agrawal, A., Suzgun, M., Mackey, L. & Kalai, A.T., 2024. "Do Language Models Know When They're Hallucinating References?" Findings of EACL 2024; arXiv:2305.18248 [fetched — abstract and introduction read; remainder of the ~110k-char page not read] — State-of-the-art LMs, GPT-4 included, routinely generate hallucinated book/article references. The authors' ground truth is a *search-engine lookup* that matched human experts on at least 99 of 100 references; the model's own recall is the thing being tested, not the reference standard. Nuance (see STEELMAN/Caveats): the LM "often accurately recall[s] the authors of real references" and gives inconsistent answers for fabricated ones.
    2. "Bibliographic research with large language model ChatGPT-4: instability, hallucinations and sometimes alerts" (PMC11530843) [search-result] — Reports unstable answers across repeated queries. Limitations are "even more evident for researchers with lower publication and citation indexes", which the authors attribute to sparse representation in training data. Long-tail authors, the usual case for niche thinkers, are where recall is worst.
    3. Walters, W.H. & Wilder, E.I., 2023. "Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT." Scientific Reports 13 [search-result for the figures; attribution from background-knowledge] — 55% of GPT-3.5 and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated, and many non-fabricated ones had substantive errors (wrong year, venue, authors). Even the better model gets the bibliographic details wrong at a material rate.
    4. Topaz et al., 2026, The Lancet (audit of 2.47M PMC papers) and Zhao et al., 2026 (111M references across arXiv/bioRxiv/SSRN/PMC) [search-result] — Fabricated references in the published literature rose sharply after LLM adoption: about 4 per 10,000 papers in 2023 to about 57 per 10,000 in early 2026. LLM bibliographic recall is unreliable enough to contaminate peer-reviewed output.
    5. Errami, M. & Garner, H. et al., Déjà vu duplicate-publication studies (ORI "Dejavuduplicatepaper-Garner"; PMC2872002) [search-result] — Duplicate-publication detection works by *text similarity* (title/abstract comparison). Nearly identical abstracts can sit on different bodies and vice versa, so even metadata-level matching needs textual verification. Practice does not establish reissue status from memory.
    6. OpenNovelty (arXiv:2601.01576) [search-result] — An LLM-based system that flags overlap with prior papers but *verifies* each claimed overlap against source texts by token-level alignment before accepting it. The field's own LLM tooling treats unverified LLM claims of prior existence as untrustworthy.

  Strength of challenge: Strong

  Summary: Citation-hallucination research consistently finds that LLMs fabricate or corrupt bibliographic details at material rates (tens of percent for older models, still double digits for GPT-4-class models), that answers are unstable across queries, and that accuracy is worst for less-cited authors. Rejecting a candidate as a "reissue" means asserting that a specific prior publication exists *and* matches the candidate's content. Both claims are confabulation-prone, and the second cannot be settled without the texts. Duplicate-detection practice and the more recent LLM novelty tools both require textual verification against a retrieved source. A false "reissue" verdict is also silent: the item is dropped and nobody looks again.

  STEELMAN:
    Item: PRESUMPTION-1112
    Strongest counterargument: An LLM asked "has this author published this before?" will tend to answer fluently whatever the truth, and the fluent answer can include a plausible earlier title, venue and year that do not exist or are about something else. For a niche thinker, recall is weakest exactly where the decision matters. Because rejection removes the item from the pipeline, a false positive is never checked again, so the error rate is invisible and the corpus quietly loses genuinely new work. The research community's own reference standard for this question is retrieval plus comparison, never recall.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: The reissue judgement is backed by a retrieved prior item (URL/DOI) and a text comparison, at least of title plus abstract or first paragraph; or LLM recall is used only to *flag* a possible reissue for verification, never to reject; or a measured false-reissue rate on this corpus is low.
    How to test: Take a set of candidates the LLM rejected as reissues. For each, require the cited prior item to be retrieved and compared. Count rejections where the prior item does not exist, or exists but differs substantively. Seed known-new items by low-citation authors and check whether they are wrongly rejected.

  Specific risks: Genuinely new work by tracked thinkers is discarded as "already seen"; rejection reasons cite non-existent prior publications; bias against less-prominent thinkers.

  Mitigations available: Retrieval-grounded verification (search API, DOI/Crossref lookup) before rejection; asymmetric policy where recall can include or flag but not exclude; consistency checks (Agrawal et al.'s indirect queries) as a cheap first-pass filter; logging rejected items with the claimed prior source for audit.

  Caveats: Agrawal et al. find LLMs recall authors of *real* references fairly accurately, and the hallucination rate is falling across model generations, so recall is not worthless as a signal. Most studies measure *generating* citations, not *recognising* whether a given item is a reissue. Recognition may be easier, but no study of that specific task was found. The challenge is to recall as the sole basis for rejection.
