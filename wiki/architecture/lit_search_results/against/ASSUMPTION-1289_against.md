  Note on what is and is not challenged. The diagnosis and the prescription are separable, and the
  literature treats them very differently. The **diagnosis** — that designed controls are routinely
  credited with effects nobody measured — I found no challenge to at all; the safety literature states
  it as settled. The **prescription** — that every control should carry an effect statistic or be
  demoted to "declared" — is where the challenge sits, and it is substantial. This file therefore
  challenges the second clause and reports the first as unchallenged.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate

  Summary: The literature does not dispute the diagnosis — that unmeasured mechanisms accrue unearned
  credit is close to a consensus position in safety science, and Roelen et al. report that even ICAO
  mandates control effectiveness without saying how to measure it. What the literature disputes is the
  remedy. The one large, well-instrumented case where a profession did adopt a universal per-outcome
  effectiveness statistic is the TRIR case, and Hallowell et al. measured, over 3.26 trillion
  worker-hours, that the statistic is 96–98% random variation, has no discernible association with
  fatalities, and rewards nothing but luck when used for performance evaluation. Their arithmetic
  matters more than their rhetoric: the exposure needed to report even one meaningful decimal place is
  hundreds of millions of worker-hours. C2A2 is a single-operator estate whose controls fire on the
  order of once a day; the events a control is supposed to prevent are rarer still. Any per-control
  effect value computed at that sample size is not a measurement. Alongside this, the safety-clutter,
  bureaucratisation and audit-society literatures converge on a second-order harm: a universal
  measurement requirement generalises and over-specifies, produces surface compliance, and — Power's
  point, the sharpest of the three — reshapes what gets built toward what can be verified. The
  challenge, then, is not "don't measure controls." It is that the prescription as written applies a
  uniform requirement to a population of controls with wildly different measurability, and that the
  known consequence of doing so is not better safety but more paperwork and a bias toward measurable
  controls.

  Specific risks: (a) If the prescription is adopted, the self-awareness layer acquires a per-control
  reporting obligation across every gate and register — the flag's own scope — while the layer is
  already starving its 15d re-trigger lane and carrying 2,111 bare-`[QUEUED]` tags. Muller's "cost in
  employee time" and Rae & Provan's opportunity cost are the direct prediction: the measurement
  displaces the work. (b) The numbers produced will mostly be statistically meaningless at this
  estate's event rates, and — this is the failure that actually bites — a meaningless number *looks
  like* a measurement in a register, so the prescription would convert an honestly-labelled unmeasured
  control into a dishonestly-labelled measured one. That is a strictly worse epistemic state than the
  one the flag is trying to fix. (c) Power's selection effect: over time, controls that are hard to
  instrument (judgement calls, review passes, the declining-to-act decisions that this estate depends
  on) get demoted to "declared" and thereby devalued, while trivially countable controls (did the job
  fire? was the tag written?) get promoted — which is exactly the liveness-over-freshness inversion
  that PRESUMPTION-931 is about. (d) A "declared control" label with no consumer is itself a declared
  control, so the prescription is at risk of instantiating the thing it names — the flag already says
  as much about itself.

  Mitigations available: (i) Stratify rather than universalise. Hallowell et al.'s own recommendation 6
  is instructive: replace one rare-outcome statistic with a larger number of high-frequency
  measurements of the system itself. For C2A2 that means measuring *control operation* (did the gate
  fire, on what input, with what output) at high frequency, and reserving *control effect* claims for
  the small number of controls where a counterfactual is actually available. (ii) Require the effect
  statistic to be accompanied by its uncertainty, or refuse it — Hallowell et al.'s point that a single
  point estimate is meaningless generalises. A control whose effect is "1 of 1 catches" should be
  recorded as such, not as "100% effective." (iii) Adopt a third label rather than a binary. The flag
  proposes control / declared-control; the evidence supports control / *operated-but-unevaluated*
  control / declared control — the middle category being the honest home of most of this estate's
  mechanisms, and the one that a binary would force into the wrong bin. (iv) Cap the requirement:
  apply it to the controls that carry the most credit, not to all of them, and record which controls
  were exempted and why. (v) Whatever is adopted needs a named consumer and a date, or PRESUMPTION-935
  applies to it directly.

STEELMAN:
  Item: ASSUMPTION-1289
  Strongest counterargument: The flag correctly identifies that credit is being assigned without
  evidence, and then proposes to fix it by requiring evidence — but it does not check whether the
  evidence is obtainable, and in this estate mostly it is not. The one profession that ran this
  experiment at scale is instructive: safety adopted a universal recordable-incident rate as its
  effectiveness statistic, used it for fifty years to compare contractors, evaluate managers and
  "track the impact of safety initiatives," and when someone finally analysed 3.26 trillion
  worker-hours of it the finding was that it is 96–98% random, has no association with fatalities, and
  cannot be reported to even one decimal place without hundreds of millions of hours of exposure. The
  measurement was not merely useless; it actively misled, because a number in a report is trusted more
  than an acknowledged absence. That is the specific danger for C2A2. Right now an unmeasured control
  is at least *visibly* unmeasured — the flag was able to see it and say so. Under the prescription,
  every control would carry a number, most of those numbers would be one or two events divided by one
  or two opportunities, and the register would look measured while being less informative than it is
  today. Add the second-order effects that three separate literatures agree on — clutter accumulates by
  generalisation and over-specification, bureaucratised safety yields declining marginal returns and
  numbers games, and the demand for auditability reshapes activity toward what can be audited — and the
  prescription's most likely outcome is not better-calibrated credit but a register full of noise, a
  measurement burden falling on a layer that is already failing to service its own backlog, and a
  slow drift toward keeping the controls that are easy to count. The diagnosis is right. The remedy as
  written is the move that safety science spent two decades learning to regret.
  What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: Either (a) the controls in question have enough
  firing events, and enough observable near-misses, that an effect statistic has non-trivial power —
  which requires the estate's event rate to be orders of magnitude above where it appears to be; or
  (b) the "effect value" being demanded is not an outcome rate but an operation record (this control
  fired on these inputs and changed these outputs), in which case the sample-size objection dissolves
  entirely and the challenge collapses to a labelling question; or (c) the requirement is applied
  selectively to high-credit controls with an explicit exemption register, so clutter cannot accumulate
  by generalisation. Reading (b) is available on a charitable reading of "action-changed value" and
  would be the safe form of the prescription — but the flag as written says *a control with no value is
  a declared control*, which is the universal form.
  How to test: Two cheap in-house checks, both answerable from data this estate already has. First,
  power: for each of the four controls the flag names, count how many times the control fired in the
  last 60 days and how many times the adverse event it prevents occurred or was averted. If the
  denominator is under about 20, no effect statistic computed on it can distinguish a working control
  from a broken one, and the honest label is "unevaluable," not "declared." Second, burden: instrument
  one cycle. Pick one register, require the effect value for every control in it for a single run, and
  record (i) the wall-clock cost of producing the values, (ii) how many of the values are a ratio with
  a denominator under 5, and (iii) whether any decision in that cycle changed as a result. Rae &
  Provan's opportunity-cost claim and Muller's goal-displacement claim both become measurable that way,
  and the result is exactly the kind of "action-changed" value the flag is asking for — applied, for
  once, to the flag's own prescription.
