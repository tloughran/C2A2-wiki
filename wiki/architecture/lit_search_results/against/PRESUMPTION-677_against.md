  Strength of challenge: **Strong**

  Summary: The presumption is false on three independent grounds. Arithmetically, Little's Law makes production and consumption rates non-independent by construction: when arrivals exceed service, the difference does not vanish, it accumulates, and the measured figures (307 open items across three registers, 30 days without a decision) are the direct evidence that this is C2A2's current regime. Behaviourally, the alarm-management standards supply calibrated ceilings — 6/hour acceptable, 12/hour unmanageable, 30/hour deficient — and forty years of cry-wolf research establishes the harder claim that matters here: response degradation is a function of the *stream*, not of the individual item's correctness, so adding more *true* findings past the threshold reduces response to all of them, including the critical ones (Bliss et al. 2002 on collective mistrust). Empirically, the closest software analogue is Google's: an automated producer filing findings into a human queue produced an 84% never-fixed rate, and the remedy adopted was to constrain the producer and change the delivery point, not to exhort the consumer. The clearest single statement of the challenge is that PREMISE-138's norm — err toward reporting more — is a policy on the *producer* side of a queue whose *consumer* side has been idle for 30 days, and every source found says that in that configuration the norm is actively counterproductive.

  Specific risks:
    (a) **Response collapse across the whole register, not just its tail.** Bliss et al. (2002): mistrust generalises. The Critical-risk items (e.g. PRESUMPTION-664) are competing for attention with 300+ others and are being read at the response rate the whole stream earns.
    (b) **The backlog becomes non-information.** Below the reliability/attention threshold the alarm system is worse than no alarm system (source 5). A register nobody reads is not neutral; it displaces the attention that a smaller register would have received, and it provides false assurance that risks are "tracked."
    (c) **Unbounded growth with no damping term.** With μ ≈ 0 over 30 days and λ > 0 enforced by policy, the queue diverges. No amount of consumer diligence closes a gap that is structurally maintained.
    (d) **Bug-bankruptcy endgame.** The observed organisational response to an undrainable queue is mass invalidation, which destroys the genuinely critical items along with the rest — the worst possible outcome for a system whose whole value proposition is that it surfaces what would otherwise be missed.
    (e) **Coupling to PRESUMPTION-673.** The queue's consumer is also the sole authoriser of the durable fixes that 673 shows are not being made. Backlog depth is therefore not just an attention problem; it is the mechanism by which repairs fail to land. See SYSTEMIC-RISK-FLAG.
    (f) **Reflexive risk:** the instrument that produced this measurement is instructed to produce more measurements. Absent a change, the metric being reported grows because it is being reported.

  Mitigations available:
    (a) **Rationalisation (ANSI/ISA-18.2's own term):** every item gets a defined severity, a defined required response, and — critically — items with *no possible operator action* are removed rather than logged. This is the standard's core remedy and is directly importable.
    (b) **WIP limit on the producer side (Anderson 2010):** cap new QUEUED items until the open count falls below a threshold. This is the only mitigation that acts on the actual variable; all others act on the consumer.
    (c) **Change the delivery point, not the volume (Sadowski et al. 2018):** surface findings where and when the human is already acting — at review time, attached to the artifact — rather than accumulating them in registers he must visit. Google's 84%-unfixed result was fixed by this move, not by better prioritisation.
    (d) **Batch and aggregate:** report classes of finding with counts rather than one item per instance. Twelve instances of one pattern is one alarm with n=12, not twelve alarms.
    (e) **Route to in-house tests wherever possible.** C2A2 already does this (the [IN-HOUSE] disposition), and it is the correct instinct: work that an agent can settle with one command never enters the human queue at all. Expanding this class is the highest-leverage available mitigation.
    (f) **Amend PREMISE-138 to be rate-aware:** "err toward reporting more" conditioned on the open-item count being below a threshold, and "err toward aggregating and deferring" above it. This preserves the norm's intent (do not suppress true findings) while removing its unbounded arrival rate.

  Recommendation: **CHALLENGED (Strong)** — production rate and consumption rate are not independent, and the literature specifically contradicts the stronger form of the norm: past a threshold, additional *true* findings reduce response to all findings. PREMISE-138 should be made rate-aware rather than abandoned.

  STEELMAN:
    Item: PRESUMPTION-677
    Strongest counterargument: The alarm-management standards were written after decades of process-industry accidents in which operators, surrounded by correct alarms, missed the one that mattered. Their finding is not "false alarms are bad" — it is that *the arrival rate itself* is the controlled variable, with numeric limits (6/hour acceptable, 12/hour unmanageable), because human response degrades as a function of stream volume regardless of per-item validity. Bliss's collective-mistrust result closes the escape route that C2A2 would naturally reach for: you cannot protect the critical items by labelling them critical, because mistrust generalises across the set. Little's Law then removes the possibility that this resolves itself — with arrivals exceeding service, the backlog diverges, and 30 days with no decision means service has been approximately zero while the producer has been operating under an explicit instruction to produce more. Google's experience is the direct software precedent: an automated producer of true-positive findings, filed into a human queue, went 84% unfixed, and the fix was to constrain the producer and move the delivery point. The conclusion is uncomfortable and specific: C2A2's most distinctive capability — its ability to surface genuine, previously-invisible defects at high volume — is, past the threshold it has already crossed, the mechanism by which its findings stop being acted upon. The 34/136/137 figures are not a measure of how much the system has found. They are a measure of how much it has found *and thereby buried*.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (a) the human consumer's service rate is genuinely elastic and the 30-day gap is an artifact of the measurement window rather than the steady state; (b) the registers function as a durable searchable archive consulted on demand rather than as an alarm stream requiring per-item response — in which case depth is harmless and the alarm literature does not apply; (c) a large share of items are self-servicing (routed [IN-HOUSE] and closed by agents), so the true λ at the human is far below the raw count; (d) items decay or expire, providing the damping term Little's Law requires. (c) is partly true already and is the strongest available defence of the current design.
    How to test: Directly measurable from the vault. (1) Compute λ and μ: count items added to and closed from each register per week over the last 12 weeks. If μ < λ for most weeks, the presumption is falsified formally. (2) Compute the human-facing arrival rate specifically — items requiring a human decision per day — and compare to the EEMUA/ISA thresholds scaled to a part-time single operator. (3) Test the cry-wolf prediction: check whether *high-severity* items filed during high-backlog periods have a lower or slower disposition rate than identical-severity items filed during low-backlog periods. If severity does not protect disposition rate, Bliss's collective-mistrust result is confirmed in this system.
