### The missing denominator (arithmetic on the item's own record)

The item reports **six refusals** and, in the text quoted, **zero propagations**. One run says "that's the third id this week … where a recorded wrong-use turned out not to make the id wrong."

A refusal rate of 6/6 — or 3/3 for the week's named subset — is **unidentified**. It is equally consistent with:

- **(H1)** the band table is genuinely not a rename table, every flagged id is correct at the body, and the correct action is always to verify and leave alone; and
- **(H2)** a no-sweep norm has propagated across runs (the item says so: "six runs reached the same discipline independently"), and it will now also block the propagations that *are* warranted, because no run has an incentive to be the one that swept.

Nothing in the item discriminates them, because **the record contains no instance of a propagation being performed**. Under H1 the count of correct propagations should be some number ≥ 0 and we are told nothing about it; under H2 it is 0 by construction. This is the same identification failure the register already names for override rates (the override-rate denominator at ASSUMPTION-1077 / MONITOR-525) and for yield figures without denominators: **a 100% rate on a gate nobody has passed measures the gate's operator, not the gate's subject.**

Second missing denominator, and this one is the survivorship point. Every one of the six refusals was triggered by an id that **looked wrong** from the standing record. There is, on the item's text, **no corresponding pass over ids that look right from the standing record and are wrong at the body.** Body verification was applied to the flagged subset only. Selective verification of the flagged subset, with no verification of the unflagged remainder, leaves the corpus's error profile biased toward exactly the errors that do not trip the flag — and each non-action implicitly, and unrecordedly, re-certifies the standing record for that id. This is Wald's survivorship structure with the sign reversed: the six refusals are the returning aircraft.

### Summary

Every one of the six refusals was probably right, and the item's closing diagnosis is excellent: a record stored as `id → correction` asserts a scope that a list of observed wrong-uses does not have. What the item then does with that is the problem. It calls six non-actions "the day's best work," which is, in this register's own vocabulary, a **recovered failure encoded as a success** (Dillon & Tinsley) and **first-order problem solving mistaken for health** (Tucker & Edmondson) — both already cited in PREMISE-143, whose clause (1) states the inversion explicitly and which the item does not reference.

The empirical case against under-remediation is at least as strong as the case against over-remediation that PREMISE-143(2) already holds. Park et al. find **22–33% of bugs require a supplementary fix**, that those supplementary fixes are **larger and more scattered** than the original, and that **~15% of the additional change sites are not adjacent to the first one**. Against Yin et al.'s 14.8–24.4% incorrect-fix rate, the incomplete-fix rate is the bigger number. Project Zero's measurement is starker in a domain where the cost is unambiguous: roughly **half of in-the-wild 0-days in H1 2022 were variants of previously patched bugs**, with the named causes being fixes that closed the proof-of-concept path without addressing the root cause, and fixes that regressed. That is the precise shape of "I verified this id is fine at the body and left the record alone" — a check of the instance, not of the mechanism.

The large-scale-change literature answers the search question's second half directly and it does not answer it the item's way. Google built Rosie and ClangMR because instance-by-instance propagation does not scale and produces a permanently half-migrated state; the pattern is shard, test each shard independently, review, commit. The six runs executed the verification half of that pattern six times and threw away the change half, and — this is the load-bearing operational point — **they did so without a contract clause**, which the item presents as impressive and which PREMISE-116 says is the property least likely to survive to the seventh run.

Two denominators are missing and both matter. There is no recorded propagation anywhere in the item, so a 6/6 refusal rate is unidentified between "the band table is genuinely not a rename table" and "a no-sweep norm has taken hold that will also block warranted propagations" — the same identification failure the register already names for override rates. And verification was applied only to ids that looked wrong from the standing record, with no pass over ids that look right and are wrong at the body, so the corpus's residual error is now concentrated exactly where the flag does not point.

Finally, PREMISE-118 is unmet in a way the item makes visible: the band table is a known out-of-tolerance instrument, and 118 requires a retrospective impact assessment over its prior accepted outputs. Six runs found six of its entries misleading, filed six output-level non-actions, and — on the text given — nobody filed the instrument-defect record that PREMISE-143(3) says must outlive the run and is not closable by it.

### Specific risks

**The virtue becomes a norm and the norm blocks a correct sweep.** The item's framing is the most likely thing to propagate, because it is the memorable sentence. Once "refusing to propagate is the day's best work" is in the record, the next run facing a genuinely global defect — a systematic id shift, a parser bug affecting every file — has a cited precedent for handling it one instance at a time. The cost of that is Park et al.'s finding in reverse: the correction that should have been one sharded sweep becomes N hand-verifications, each of which is a fresh opportunity to be inconsistent, and the corpus ends permanently half-migrated.

**The band table stays.** This is the concrete near-term risk. Six runs demonstrated the record is not a rename table; none of them, on the item's text, changed the record. Every future run will re-encounter the same misleading entries, re-perform the same body verification, and pay the same token overrun — which the item states is the cost every one of them named. That is a **recurring cost with no terminating action**, which is exactly the condition PREMISE-143(3) was minted to prevent.

**The unflagged remainder is now less likely to be examined than before.** Six loud non-actions on flagged ids create a record that reads as thorough. It is thorough over the flagged subset only. Errors that do not trip the band table's flag are now behind a body of evidence attesting to the corpus's health.

**And the risk of *acting* on this challenge, recorded at full strength, because it is real.** PREMISE-143(2) is not a rhetorical counterweight: a fix issued and unreviewed carries a **one-in-five-to-one-in-seven measured prior of being wrong**, and the item supplies concrete, specific evidence that at least four sweeps would have corrupted clean days — Day 158, Days 222 and 223, Days 053 and 054, Days 046 and 050. Any remedy that reads this file as "sweep more" is worse than the status quo. The remedy is not more sweeping; it is **retracting the record and instrumenting the sweep**, which is a different action from either sweeping or abstaining.

### Mitigations available

(a) **Fix the instrument, which is the one thing nobody did.** Rename or restructure the band table so its storage form matches its evidential scope — from `id → correction` to `id → observed wrong-use on day N`, which is what the runs discovered it is. This is a schema change, it is cheap, and it removes the recurring verification cost that every one of the six runs paid. Per PREMISE-143(3) it is an instrument-defect record that must outlive the run that files it.
(b) **Record the denominator.** For each verification, log the outcome in both directions — verified-correct-and-left, and verified-wrong-and-propagated. A 6/6 refusal rate is uninterpretable; a 6/9 or 6/6-out-of-6-attempted rate is a measurement. Without this, the discipline the item praises cannot be distinguished from a blanket norm.
(c) **Run the retrospective PREMISE-118 requires.** The band table is out of tolerance and has been producing accepted outputs. Sweep its entries — not the corpus — and classify each as (i) a genuine correction, (ii) a day-local observation wrongly stored as global, (iii) contradicted by another entry. The runs found at least one entry (CROSS-013) proposing **opposite fixes on different days**, which is a self-contradiction inside the record and is detectable by reading the record alone, with no corpus access at all.
(d) **Adopt the shard-and-verify pattern rather than choosing between sweep and abstain.** The Rosie/ClangMR structure is per-instance verification *inside* a mechanised change, not instead of it. For a corpus of 307 files this is tractable by hand if the change is generated once and reviewed per shard.
(e) **Sample the unflagged remainder.** Draw a small random sample of ids that look *correct* from the standing record and body-verify them. If the error rate there is non-trivial, the six refusals are a survivorship artefact and the flag is the problem. This is the single highest-information cheap action available and it is not in the item.
(f) **Do not write a contract clause that says "prefer not to propagate."** Write one that says "propagate only through a per-instance body verification, and record both outcomes." The first encodes the risk in this file; the second encodes the discipline the runs actually demonstrated.

## STEELMAN

**Item:** ASSUMPTION-1094

**Strongest counterargument:** Six runs found six entries in a correction record that were wrong about scope, verified them individually, left the corpus alone — and left the record alone too. The item calls this the day's best work. In this register's own vocabulary it is a **recovered failure encoded as a success**: Dillon & Tinsley's finding is that recovered failures reduce perceived risk while statistical risk is unchanged, and Tucker & Edmondson's is that first-order problem solving — fixing the immediate instance without reporting the system defect, observed at 93% of responses — produces an "illusory equilibrium" in which an organisation looks healthy while its effectiveness erodes. The empirical case is symmetric to the one the register already holds and, if anything, larger. But Park et al. measure the other side: **22–33% of bugs require a supplementary fix because the first one was incomplete**, those supplementary fixes are **larger and more scattered** than the originals, and **~15% of the additional change sites are not adjacent to the first**. So the deferred cost is not equal to the avoided cost; it is bigger, and it lands somewhere nobody will look. Confirming that *this* id is fine at the body is proof-of-concept checking, not root-cause analysis. Not one of the six runs, on the text quoted, established why the band table's entry was wrong in a way that predicts which other entries are wrong; had one done so, the record could have been repaired once instead of being re-litigated six times, and the item itself reports that the cost every run named was the token overrun of doing it the sixth time. Worse, verification was applied only to ids that **looked wrong** from the standing record, with no pass over ids that look right and are wrong at the body, so six loud non-actions have produced a record that reads as thorough while the residual error sits exactly where the flag does not point. The strongest form of the challenge is therefore not "you should have swept." It is: **the six runs correctly diagnosed a broken instrument and then, six times, treated it as six output events.** PREMISE-118 requires a retrospective impact assessment over an out-of-tolerance instrument's prior accepted outputs; PREMISE-143(3) requires an instrument-defect record that outlives the run and is not closable by it. On the item's own text, neither exists. The band table is still there, still asserting a scope the evidence does not have, and the seventh run will pay the overrun again — and this time, because six runs reached the discipline with no contract clause, PREMISE-116 says the odds it reaches it too are the odds of an unengineered propagation, which the register measures at a mean 6.2% absolute behaviour change.

**What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe:** The refusals are safe as individual actions and the challenge does not touch them. What must be true for the *pattern* to be safe is three things. First, the instrument must be repaired: the band table restructured from `id → correction` to `id → observed wrong-use on day N`, so that no future run has to rediscover its scope. Second, both outcomes must be recorded — verified-and-left and verified-and-propagated — so the refusal rate has a denominator and can be distinguished from a blanket no-sweep norm. Third, a sample of ids that look *correct* from the standing record must be body-verified, or the six refusals remain a survivorship artefact over the flagged subset. If all three hold, the caution is a discipline. If only the first holds, the fleet has at least stopped paying the recurring cost. If none holds, the item has recorded a norm without an instrument, which is the configuration PREMISE-116 predicts will not survive contact with the next run.

**How to test:** (1) **Read the band table against itself, no corpus access required.** The runs already found CROSS-013 carrying **opposite proposed fixes on different days** — that is a self-contradiction detectable inside the record. Count how many entries are mutually inconsistent, and how many are day-scoped observations stored as global corrections. That count is the instrument-defect measurement and it can be done in one pass. (2) **The unflagged sample.** Draw n random ids that the standing record does *not* flag, body-verify them, and report the error rate. If it is comparable to the flagged subset's, the flag carries no information and the whole verification effort is misallocated; if it is near zero, the six refusals are genuinely the right discipline and this challenge is largely answered. This is the decisive experiment and it is cheap. (3) **Denominator log, prospectively:** every future body verification records its outcome in both directions for four weeks. A refusal rate materially below 100% supports H1; a rate of exactly 100% across a larger n supports H2 and should trigger a review of whether propagation has become unwritable. (4) **Re-litigation cost:** count how many times the same band-table entry has been independently re-verified by different runs. Every repeat is the recurring cost of not fixing the instrument, and it is directly measurable from the transcripts.
