  Sources:
    1. Diamond, I. et al., 2014. "Defining consensus: A systematic review recommends methodologic criteria for reporting of Delphi studies." Journal of Clinical Epidemiology. — Review of Delphi studies found dozens of different consensus definitions in use (a related review found 33 methods across 126 studies); stopping criteria are routinely ad hoc.
    2. BMC Medical Research Methodology reanalysis (von der Gracht lineage; see Holey et al. 2007, "An exploration of the use of simple statistics to measure consensus and stability in Delphi studies"). — Reanalyzing the same Delphi data under 25 different consensus definitions yielded 0%–84% of items "reaching consensus": the stopping rule, not the data, determines what counts as agreed.
    3. von der Gracht, H., 2012. "Consensus measurement in Delphi studies: Review and implications." Technological Forecasting and Social Change. — Distinguishes consensus from stability; closing on apparent agreement without a stability check risks recording transient convergence as durable agreement.
    4. Savic, L. et al., 2023. "How to conduct a Delphi consensus process." Anaesthesia. — Stopping conditions must be pre-specified before data collection to avoid arbitrary in-flight closure decisions; criteria defined during the process are considered methodologically unsound.
  Strength of challenge: Moderate
  Summary: Structured-consensus literature challenges the adequacy claim on three fronts. (1) "Exhaust candidates" is a coverage criterion, not a consensus criterion: it bounds what was discussed, not whether agreement is stable. (2) The 0%–84% reanalysis result shows that what a closure rule "captures" is an artifact of the rule's definition; without a pre-specified, quantitative agreement threshold, "what the dyad agrees about" is undefined. (3) The rule lacks a stability check — Delphi methodology treats one revisit as insufficient to distinguish durable agreement from politeness convergence or fatigue (premature closure), especially in a two-member panel where sycophancy pressure (cf. ASSUMPTION-295) inflates apparent agreement. That said, the rule is procedurally explicit and includes a revisit step, which exceeds many informal practices; the challenge is to "adequate," not to "useless."
  Specific risks: The closure step certifies transient or performative agreement as the dyad's settled position; items closed under fatigue or session pressure enter PRS as ratified; no audit trail distinguishes strong from marginal agreement.
  Mitigations available: Pre-specify the closure criterion in the charter before sessions; add a delayed re-confirmation pass (separate session) before final close, converting revisit into a stability check; record per-item agreement strength rather than binary closed/open.
  STEELMAN:
    Strongest counterargument: Delphi stopping-rule pathology arises from multi-rater anonymous panels estimating uncertain quantities; a dyad documenting its own shared commitments is a different task — the parties can simply ask each other, and exhaustion-plus-revisit is proportionate for a LOW-stakes inventory. Over-engineering closure for a two-person table risks endless-deliberation failure, which the same literature also warns against.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: Closed items are re-openable at low cost; the revisit occurs after a genuine temporal gap; closure strength is recorded; downstream consumers treat closed items as snapshots, not permanent facts.
    How to test: Re-present a sample of closed items in a later session without reference to their closed status; measure re-endorsement rate. Substantial non-re-endorsement falsifies adequacy of the original closure.
