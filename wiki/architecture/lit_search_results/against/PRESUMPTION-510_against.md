    1. "Learning When to Automate: Queue Control in Human-AI Service Systems," arXiv:2607.06017 (retrieved 2026-07-21). Automation and human scheduling cannot be optimised separately: automated decisions shape the arrival process into the human queue while scheduling decisions determine how limited human capacity is allocated. Supports the item's core structural claim that production and judgment are coupled — and simultaneously challenges the remedy, since a fixed admission cap optimises one side in isolation, which is the thing the paper says cannot be done.
    2. "When to Screen, When to Bypass: LLM-Judges in Resource-Scarce AI-Human Workflow," arXiv:2603.13870 (retrieved 2026-07-21). Models the workflow as a queueing network with multiple resource pools; optimal allocation depends critically on which resource is the current bottleneck, and the recommended control is selective screening and bypass — routing by expected value — rather than a uniform rate cap.
    3. "Operating Imperfect AI: Reliability Drift and Human Congestion," arXiv:2601.22295 (retrieved 2026-07-21). Introduces the congestion externality: every task added to the queue increases delay for all subsequent tasks, producing a fluctuating shadow price of capacity. Argues for a dynamic, backlog-sensitive price rather than a fixed limit — a direct challenge to a static one-HIGH-item-per-day cap.
    4. Practitioner evidence on WIP limits (Flashover, "Limit Your WIP"; Uplevel, "WIP Limits for Engineering Teams"; retrieved 2026-07-21). WIP limits are "usually well understood and poorly implemented": teams adopt them, hit them on day 12 (or 6, or 2), and raise them again, which defeats the purpose. The specific failure mode the item's proposed cap is most likely to encounter.
    5. Same practitioner cluster, on WIP ranges: using ranges rather than hard limits allows adjacent processes to operate at different economically optimal batch sizes — i.e. some decoupling between production and review is intentional and beneficial, not a defect.

  Strength of challenge: Moderate
  Summary: The structural claim is well supported — the human-AI queueing literature states outright that automated production and human scheduling cannot be optimised separately, which is the item's point in formal dress, and the congestion-externality result gives a mechanism for why unbounded production actively degrades the channel. The challenge is entirely on the remedy. Three separate lines converge against a flat one-HIGH-item-per-day admission cap. The screening literature recommends value-based routing — screen or bypass by expected worth — rather than a uniform rate limit, because optimal allocation depends on which resource is currently the bottleneck and that changes. The congestion work argues for a dynamic shadow price that responds to backlog rather than a fixed number. And the practitioner record says that fixed WIP limits are characteristically abandoned within days of first binding, which in a system with 67 carried items and a service rate of zero would happen almost immediately. A further correction: with service at 0/day, admission control does not fix the problem at all. Reducing arrivals from 4/day to 1/day still yields an unbounded queue; only a non-zero service rate stabilises it. The item names admission control as the lever when the arithmetic says the review channel's throughput is the binding term.
  Specific risks: A cap is imposed, binds within days, is raised, and the episode is recorded as an attempted fix — consuming the credibility of the next proposal while leaving λ>μ untouched. Second and larger risk: attention goes to throttling production when service rate is the term that is actually zero, so the queue grows more slowly and still without bound, and the system reports a control in place. Third risk: beneficial decoupling is removed — some buffer between production and review is economically correct, and a hard cap eliminates it along with the excess.
  Mitigations available: Fix the sign before fixing the rate: with μ=0 no admission policy stabilises the queue, so establish a non-zero service rate first, even at one item per week. Prefer value-based screening over a uniform cap — route only items above an expected-value threshold to the human channel and dispose of the rest automatically, per the screen-or-bypass result. If a limit is used, use a range rather than a hard number and pre-commit to not raising it, since raising it on first bind is the documented failure. Add the "produced and unreviewable" state the item proposes; that is uncontested and useful on its own. Publish backlog-sensitive pressure (a visible shadow price of capacity) rather than a fixed gate.

    Strongest counterargument: The diagnosis is right, well supported, and points at the wrong lever. Recent human-AI queueing work states that automated production and human scheduling cannot be optimised separately, because automation shapes the arrival process into the human queue — so the coupling the item surfaces is real and formalised. But with a measured service rate of zero across fifteen days, admission control cannot stabilise anything: cutting arrivals from four per day to one per day leaves λ−μ positive and the queue unbounded, merely slowing the divergence. The binding term is service, and the item's own arithmetic shows it. Worse, the specific control proposed — a fixed one-HIGH-item-per-day cap — is the form the literature and practice both argue against. The screening work recommends value-based routing (screen or bypass by expected worth, allocating against whichever resource is currently the bottleneck) rather than a uniform rate limit; the congestion work recommends a dynamic shadow price responsive to backlog rather than a static gate; and practitioner evidence records the characteristic fate of fixed WIP limits, which is that teams hit them within days and raise them, defeating the mechanism. With 67 carried items already in the channel, a cap would bind immediately and would be relaxed immediately. There is also a real cost to eliminating decoupling entirely: WIP ranges exist because adjacent processes have different economically optimal batch sizes, and some buffer between production and review is correct.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: That a non-zero review service rate is established before any admission policy is designed, and that whatever control is adopted routes by expected value rather than by a flat count.
    How to test: Apply PREMISE-106's arithmetic to the review channel and record both terms, not one. Then run a two-week trial in which exactly one HIGH item per week is reviewed to completion — establishing μ>0 — while measuring whether the backlog's growth rate changes. If throughput stays at zero, admission control was never the lever and the item's remedy would have masked that.
