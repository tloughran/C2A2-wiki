  Sources:
    1. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Imre Lakatos" entry, and the critical literature it
       summarizes (e.g., Musgrave on the hard core). [Search-result level; not fetched.] Lakatos made
       the research programme the unit of appraisal. The standard critiques say programmes are hard
       to individuate: variants of one theory get split into different "programmes", and the hard
       core is hard to separate from the positive heuristic. The programme unit is therefore not a
       sharper individuator than the author. It moves the boundary problem to a fuzzier place.
    2. Cronin, B. (2001). "Hyperauthorship: A postmodern perversion or evidence of a structural shift
       in scholarly communication practices?" JASIST 52(7):558-569. [Search-result level. A fetch of
       the Wiley abstract page returned no content.] Large-team authorship makes it hard to recognize
       authority, allocate credit and assign accountability, and at scale this becomes "practically
       impossible". If team output is counted as the tradition, the link between a claim and the
       thinker who stands behind it weakens.
    3. Birnholtz, J. P. (2006). "What does it mean to be an author? The intersection of credit,
       contribution, and collaboration in science." JASIST 57(13). [Search-result level.] This paper
       makes the same point: in collaborative output, being an author is decoupled from endorsing
       the content.
    4. Lievrouw, L. A. (1989). "The Invisible College Reconsidered." Communication Research 16(5).
       [Search-result level.] Group-level constructs such as invisible colleges and schools are
       ambiguous because they confound structure with process. Bibliometric maps of a group do not
       show shared commitments unless those commitments are identified separately.

  Strength of challenge: Weak-to-Moderate (graded Weak for the return line)

  Summary: No source says named-author individuation is better in general, and history and philosophy of
    science mostly moved toward programme- or tradition-level units (Lakatos, and Laudan 1977 from
    background knowledge). The challenge is narrower. The programme unit has its own well-known
    individuation problems. Including team output breaks the link between a claim and an
    accountable endorser, which is what a wiki organized around 14 named thinkers relies on. The
    literature supports programme-level units for appraising progress. It does not show they are
    better for attributing positions to a person. That is the use C2A2's tradition wikis make of
    them ("what Hawkins thinks").

  Specific risks: Team reports, such as the Thousand Brains Project two-year report, get filed as
    Hawkins-tradition content. Positions the named thinker never endorsed, or has diverged from,
    then enter the tradition record, and the tradition drifts toward the institution's view. The
    question is being settled by precedent (ASSUMPTION-1700 context) rather than by a written rule.

  Mitigations available: Keep the programme scope but tag provenance for each item
    (thinker-authored, thinker-coauthored, team-without-thinker). Weight or flag team-only items in
    PRS triplets. Write the rule down in OPEN-187 rather than letting approvals set it by default.

STEELMAN:
  Item: ASSUMPTION-1700
  Strongest counterargument: A "tradition" in C2A2 exists to represent a named thinker's position,
    and the only thing that guarantees a position belongs to a thinker is that the thinker wrote or
    endorsed it. Programme-level individuation replaces that guarantee with institutional
    membership. The authorship literature shows membership becomes weaker evidence of endorsement
    as teams grow (Cronin; Birnholtz). Philosophers of science also have not solved how to draw a
    programme's boundaries. The programme unit therefore trades a crisp, checkable criterion for a
    contested one, and does so where attribution matters most.
  What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: The named thinker leads the programme and
    reliably stands behind its output, and team items are marked so that they can be separated
    from thinker-authored items.
  How to test: Sample the team-output items already accepted. Check each one's central claims
    against the named thinker's own recent statements and count the divergences.
