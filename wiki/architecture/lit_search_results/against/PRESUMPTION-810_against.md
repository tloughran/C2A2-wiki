### Polarity statement — what I am challenging

I am challenging **14b's corrective claim**, which is that a pass mark should carry its own coverage — a scope field recording *which questions were exercised* — and that without it the mark is not evidence about the file.

Specifically I challenge three things:

1. **The counterfactual** — that a coverage field would have prevented the seven rediscoveries. My challenge is that the inspection literature makes this unlikely: **a single reviewer's yield is bounded well below 100% *within* their own declared frame**, so a mark that honestly declares "synthesis frame, ASR not refetched" is still consistent with missing synthesis-frame defects. Coverage metadata addresses *frame mismatch* and does nothing about *within-frame miss rate*, and the item does not establish which of the two produced the seven.
2. **The idempotence framing** — 14b treats "review is idempotent" as the presumption. The literature's actual finding is subtler and cuts both ways: reading technique matters *less* than expected (repeated experiments find **no significant difference** between ad hoc and checklist reading on defect counts), while *reviewer count* matters a great deal. If technique is a weak variable, then a field recording technique is a weak instrument.
3. **The cost** — coverage metadata on every approval is the reintroduction of Fagan-style inspection ceremony into a workflow that industry deliberately abandoned. The best-documented large-scale modern practice is explicitly lightweight, and the ceremony has a measured yield curve that is not favourable.

**Register bearing, stated up front:** this challenge lands squarely on an **already-held premise** (PREMISE-162), and in a specific way — PREMISE-162 already identifies the *correct* remedy for residual-defect visibility, and it is **not** a coverage field.

### Strength of challenge: Moderate

### Summary

The item's diagnosis is sound and partly register-held already: PREMISE-101 holds that counts are properties of a stated *(scope, method, time)* reading, and 810 is that principle extended from counting to reviewing — a legitimate and probably correct extension. Two things are wrong with the corrective. First, **the counterfactual is unestablished and the literature makes it doubtful.** A coverage field addresses *frame mismatch* — reviewer two asked different questions than reviewer one — and does nothing about *within-frame miss rate*, which is the dominant term in every inspection experiment ever run. A mark honestly declaring "synthesis frame, ASR not refetched" is entirely consistent with missing synthesis-frame defects, and the item never establishes which of the two mechanisms produced the seven rediscoveries. That is a decidable question, from the existing records, and it was not asked. Second, **the corrective is not the remedy the register already holds.** PREMISE-162 states that residual defects are estimable only from two independent detection streams or from seeded defects, that capture-recapture requires two inspectors *by construction*, and that self-audited instruments are "unmeasurable from inside." A coverage field is a single inspector's self-report about their own scope: it creates no second stream, produces no denominator, and is itself a self-produced artefact certifying a self-produced artefact — PREMISE-096's problem one level up. The evidence on the item's own mechanism is genuinely two-sided and I record both halves: Porter/Votta/Basili found scenario reviewers better at finding the faults their scenario targets, which is 14b's thesis as an experimental result; but the same corpus repeatedly finds *no significant difference* between ad hoc and checklist reading on defect counts, and the replication picture across reading techniques is mixed. Declared technique is an unstable predictor of yield; reader count is not. Finally the cost is real and unpriced. The best-measured modern review operation — 9 million changes at Google, >80% completing in at most one iteration — is deliberately lightweight and carries no coverage field, because the industry ran Fagan's ceremony and found the formal stages bought the least productive part of the yield. And PREMISE-121, already validated here, says a saturated reviewer degrades specifically on **low-information items** — which is what a terse scope declaration is. The item proposes to lengthen the summary presented to the 37-card queue without showing that the lengthening carries information.

### Specific risks (of adopting the corrective as framed)

1. **A field that cannot do the job it is added for.** Coverage metadata cannot estimate residual defects — that requires two streams or seeded defects (PREMISE-162, by construction). If the field is adopted and the seven rediscoveries were within-frame misses rather than frame mismatches, the fleet will have paid for a field and the rediscoveries will continue, now with the field cited as though it had addressed them.
2. **Self-certification one level up.** The coverage field is written by the same run that writes the mark. PREMISE-096 bars self-certifying artefacts. Nothing external validates "synthesis frame only," and the incentive attaches to declaring wide scope, which is Goodhart's standard shape (Inozemtseva & Holmes on coverage specifically).
3. **Queue degradation on the exact queue the item is worried about.** 37 cards wait on one human whose per-item cost is not constant and whose acceptance falls with volume (PREMISE-121, override rates 49–96%, degradation concentrated on low-information items). Adding a mandatory scope line to every card is a throughput cost paid immediately against a benefit that is speculative.
4. **Ceremony creep.** Coverage metadata is the entry point of formal inspection. Once it exists, entry criteria, exit criteria and role declarations are natural next asks, and the yield curve on that apparatus is the one industry walked away from. In a fleet whose closure pipeline is already the binding constraint (PRESUMPTION-812, same batch), added review ceremony is a direct throughput tax on the bottleneck.
5. **Register inflation and false independence.** Minting 810 without noting PREMISE-101's coverage of the same principle risks the pipeline later citing 101 and 810 as two premises when they are one (PREMISE-111 non-independence, PREMISE-120 disclosure).

### Risk of dismissing the item — recorded at full strength

Seven pass-marked days were defective. That is not a modelling dispute, it is a count, and it means the marks were consumed as certifications they could not support. The scenario-reading result is real evidence for the mechanism 14b names: reviewers are demonstrably better at finding the faults their frame targets, which entails that a mark from frame A is *weaker* evidence about frame-B defects than its consumers treat it as. And there is a version of the corrective that is cheap and clearly correct and that my challenge does not touch: **runs are already declaring their scope in prose** ("synthesis frame only, no ASR refetch"; "all six raw_asr_word_count values should be presumed stale and none was checked; that's unfinished work, not a clean bill"). Those declarations exist and have nowhere to go. Capturing text that is *already being written* is not new ceremony — it is stopping the discard of an artefact the fleet already produces. My cost objection applies to a **mandatory structured coverage schema**, not to persisting a free-text scope line that runs write anyway.

### Mitigations available

(a) **Ask the decidable question first: frame mismatch or within-frame miss?** Classify each of the seven rediscovered defects as (i) outside the original mark's frame, or (ii) inside it and simply missed. **If (ii) dominates, no coverage field would have prevented them and the corrective should be withdrawn.** This is one pass over seven days' records, it costs almost nothing, and it is the pivot on which the whole item turns. **It was not done.**
(b) **Persist the scope prose that already exists; do not design a schema.** A free-text `scope_note` on the mark captures the declarations runs already write, at near-zero marginal cost and with no new obligation. Structured coverage vocabularies can wait for evidence that free text is insufficient.
(c) **Prefer PREMISE-162's remedy: seed defects.** Seeded defects are the only residual-defect estimator that works with a single auditor, and they would give the fleet its first real answer about what marks miss — which coverage metadata structurally cannot give. Second choice: one independent reader working **alone** on a sample, producing the project's first capture-recapture estimate.
(d) **Price any field against PREMISE-121 before adopting it.** State in advance what per-card reading cost is acceptable and what would count as the field carrying information rather than length. If it cannot be stated, the field fails the register's own measurement discipline.
(e) **Do not make the field mandatory on approval.** Mandatory scope declaration is the Fagan ceremony re-entry point, is self-certified and unverifiable, and attaches a gameable measure to the artefact that clears queues.
(f) **Route to PREMISE-101 rather than minting fresh.** If 810 is validated, it should be recorded as an extension of PREMISE-101 from counts to reviews, not as an independent premise.

Diagnosis: **NO-CHALLENGE-FOUND** (seven defective marks is a count; the scenario-reading result supports the mechanism). ## STEELMAN

**Item:** PRESUMPTION-810

**Strongest counterargument (the strongest case against the corrective, at full force):** The item asks what a mark would have to carry for the seven rediscoveries to be impossible, answers "its coverage," and never checks whether that is true. It is checkable, from records the fleet already has, in one pass. There are exactly two ways a pass-marked day can turn out defective. Either the second reviewer asked a question the first never asked — frame mismatch, which coverage metadata addresses — or the first reviewer asked the right question and missed the answer — a within-frame miss, which coverage metadata does not touch at all. Every inspection experiment ever run says the second term is large. A single reviewer does not find everything inside their own declared scope; that is why the entire estimation apparatus of the field exists. So a mark reading "synthesis frame, ASR not refetched" is perfectly consistent with missing synthesis-frame defects, and if that is what happened on the seven days, the proposed field would have prevented none of them. The item's central counterfactual is therefore unestablished, and the work required to establish it is smaller than the work required to build the field. Worse, the field cannot do the job the register has already determined needs doing. PREMISE-162 is ACTIVE and says it plainly: residual-defect estimation requires two independent detection streams or seeded defects, capture-recapture requires two inspectors *by construction* because the estimate is derived from their overlap, and self-audited instruments are unmeasurable from inside. A coverage field is one inspector self-reporting their own scope. It creates no second stream. It yields no denominator. It is a self-produced artefact certifying a self-produced artefact, which PREMISE-096 bars, and it attaches a self-reported unverifiable measure to the very token that clears items out of a queue — the standard Goodhart geometry, with coverage as the standard Goodhart example. Nor is the underlying mechanism as clean as the item implies. The best evidence for 14b is real and I will not soften it: scenario reviewers are measurably better at finding the faults their scenarios target, so frames do determine yield. But the same experimental corpus, replicated repeatedly, finds **no significant difference between ad hoc and checklist reading on defect counts**, and the broader picture across reading techniques is mixed in every direction. Declared technique turns out to be an unstable predictor of what a reviewer finds. Reader count is a stable one. A field that records the unstable variable while the register's own validated remedy — a second reader, or seeded defects — records the stable one is aimed at the wrong quantity. And the cost is not hypothetical. Google's study of 9 million reviewed changes describes an explicitly lightweight, tool-based process in which over 80% of changes complete in at most one iteration, with no per-approval coverage field anywhere in it. That is not an oversight by an unsophisticated organisation; it is the settled position after the industry ran Fagan's ceremony for two decades and found the formal stages bought its least productive yield at its highest interval cost. Coverage metadata is the surviving fragment of that ceremony. Meanwhile PREMISE-121, validated here, reports override rates of 49–96% and degradation concentrated specifically on **low-information items**. The item's own animating worry is the 37 cards waiting on one human. Its remedy adds a line to every card. If the line is detailed it costs reading time the reviewer does not have; if it is terse it is precisely the low-information content that the premise identifies as the thing that degrades acceptance. The item proposes to make the summary longer and does not show it makes it more informative. What survives all of this — and it does survive — is much smaller and much cheaper than what was proposed: runs are **already writing** their scope in prose, in the very quotations the item cites, and the vault discards it. Persisting text that is already being written is not ceremony. It is not throwing away an artefact you already have.

**What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe:** (1) The seven rediscoveries must be shown to be **frame mismatches rather than within-frame misses** — otherwise the corrective is aimed at a mechanism that did not operate. (2) Any coverage field must be **free-text capture of scope prose runs already emit**, not a mandatory structured schema, so that it costs nothing new and creates no gameable target. (3) The fleet must separately pursue PREMISE-162's actual remedy — **seeded defects, or one independent reader working alone** — because that is the only thing that produces a residual-defect estimate, and coverage metadata structurally cannot. (4) Any addition to what the 37-card queue presents to a human must be priced against PREMISE-121 with a stated expectation of what it buys. If all four hold, the item is discharged cheaply and the register gains its first real measurement of what marks miss.

**How to test:** (1) **The classification test — decisive, one pass, not done.** Take the seven defective pass-marked days. For each rediscovered defect, record whether the original mark's frame *would have covered it*. Prediction under 14b: mostly outside-frame, and a coverage field would have flagged the gap. Prediction under my challenge: a substantial share are inside-frame misses, against which no scope declaration helps. **This single test settles the item and costs an hour.** (2) **The seeding test.** Seed a known number of defects of known type into a batch awaiting review, then measure catch rate by frame. This produces both the within-frame miss rate and the frame-sensitivity in one experiment, and it is the register's already-stated cheapest remedy. (3) **The second-reader test.** Have one independent reader re-review a sample of pass-marked days **alone** (not in a meeting — the meeting is the stage with the poor yield). Their overlap with the original gives the project's first capture-recapture estimate, which is the number the fleet actually lacks. (4) **The gaming pre-check.** Before adopting any structured coverage vocabulary, ask what an under-pressure run's cheapest honest-looking declaration would be, and whether anything external could detect it. If nothing could, the field is unverifiable by construction and PREMISE-096 applies. (5) **The throughput test.** Prototype the field on ten cards, measure the added reading time per card, and compare against the reviewer-capacity model in PREMISE-121 before making it standard.
