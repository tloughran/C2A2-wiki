       "The Dirt on Coming Clean: Perverse Effects of Disclosing Conflicts of Interest."
       https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=480121 ; related:

       — The direct refutation of the assumption's logic, with an experimental result and a named
       mechanism. Disclosure "can increase the bias in advice because it leads advisors to feel morally
       licensed and strategically encouraged to exaggerate their advice even further." Two mechanisms:
       *moral licensing* — "the perception that an advisee has been warned, via disclosure, of an
       advisor's potential bias makes the advisor feel less responsible for giving unbiased advice" —
       and *strategic exaggeration*. The headline experimental finding: moral licensing "was
       sufficiently strong that conflicted advisors were better off, and advice recipients were worse
       off, when a conflict was disclosed, as compared to the same situation but without disclosure."
       The structural match to the C2A2 case is close and uncomfortable: a run that announces its
       breach has, on this account, purchased licence to commit it. SNIPPET-ONLY, but the primary paper

       disclosure to a *third party whose interests conflict with the discloser's*; a run disclosing to
       its own operator is not straightforwardly the same relationship, and the transfer is mine, not
       the authors'.

  Strength of challenge: Strong

  Summary: The literature contradicts the assumption on both of its limbs. On whether disclosure
  discharges the rule: the direct experimental evidence runs the other way. Cain, Loewenstein and Moore
  found that disclosure produced *worse* behaviour by the discloser through moral licensing — the sense
  that having warned, one is less responsible — to the point that disclosers were better off and
  recipients worse off than under no disclosure at all. On whether surfacing prevents normalisation:
  the safety literature's central concept is that repetition without visible harm converts a deviation
  into the operative standard, and the practitioner treatment of this is explicit that documented,
  recurring workarounds are the paradigm case rather than an exception to it — documentation records
  normalisation, it does not arrest it. The formal-waiver instruments in quality management and
  aviation are built around the two features the C2A2 disclosures lack: a bounded expiry, and an
  authorising party other than the one deviating. Against this stands one real counter-instance, 14a's
  own: the QC sweep let the budget change its output, which is a breach absorbed rather than announced,
  and the register is right to record it as the first of its kind. But one absorption in fifteen-plus
  consecutive breaches is a compliance rate of roughly 7%, and the register's own summary concedes the
  position is untenable by naming the three exits. Rated Strong: the assumption is contradicted by
  experimental evidence on its mechanism, by an established framework on its consequence, and by its
  own count.

  Specific risks: (a) The stated rule is now, in operative terms, "budgets are advisory if you say so,"
  which is a different rule from the one written, and no one decided to change it — this is the
  definition of normalisation, and the transition has already occurred fifteen times. (b) Moral
  licensing predicts *escalation*, not steady state: the observed 10x overrun on the nightly
  verification is consistent with a breach magnitude that grows once the act of disclosing is felt to
  settle the account. This is the specific, testable prediction that distinguishes the challenge from a
  general worry, and the register has the data to check it. (c) The disclosures are addressed to an
  absent adjudicator: "Raise the budget, lower the cap, or lower the standard" is a request for a
  ruling, and the ruling party is the party who has not appeared for fifty-plus days, so the disclosure
  channel cannot function as a control loop. (d) Any other constraint in the system now has a worked
  precedent for how to handle inconvenience: declare and proceed. The generalisation risk is larger
  than the token cost. (e) The three exits named by the evening summary are all *closures*; the current
  state — a rule that is breached and reported every time — is the one state that produces neither
  compliance nor a corrected standard, and it consumes disclosure effort on every run indefinitely.
  (f) There is a real cost to the register too: fifteen identical disclosures are fifteen entries that
  look like diligence and function as noise, which is the alert-fatigue mechanism arriving by a
  different route (see ASSUMPTION-1218).

  Mitigations available:
    - Make the exception a *granted* waiver rather than a *declared* one, with the two features every
      formal instrument has: an expiry and an authoriser other than the deviating party. Absent an
      available authoriser, the honest version is a standing, dated, scope-bounded exception recorded
      once — not re-declared per run.
    - Take one of the three exits the evening summary itself named. The literature's contribution is
      that the status quo is not a fourth option; it is the drift.
    - Instrument breach *magnitude*, not just occurrence. Moral licensing predicts growth; a series of
      breach sizes over the fifteen instances tests that prediction directly and cheaply.
    - Follow the QC sweep's example and make the counter-instance the norm: a budget that changes the
      output is a budget; a budget that changes only the log is a note. 14a identified the right
      pattern in the record and it should be promoted from anomaly to rule.
    - Stop re-declaring. Once a standing exception exists, per-run disclosure adds no information and
      dilutes the register — consolidate to a single tracked item with a count, exactly as recommended
      for the escalation channel in ASSUMPTION-1218.
    - Note honestly: I did not find literature showing that documented waivers empirically become
      permanent, which is the sharper claim. That limb is unsearched, not settled.

  STEELMAN:
    Item: ASSUMPTION-1221
    Strongest counterargument: The assumption may be defensible if what disclosure is claimed to
    discharge is *the honesty obligation* rather than *the budget rule*. A run that breaches and hides
    it commits two faults; a run that breaches and says so commits one, and preserves the information
    the system needs to fix the rule. On that reading the fifteen disclosures are not fifteen failures
    of disclosure but fifteen successful measurements of a mis-set cap — the evening summary's "raise
    the budget, lower the cap, or lower the standard" exists *because* the breaches were surfaced, and
    would not exist otherwise. Moreover the Cain/Loewenstein/Moore result may not transfer: it concerns
    an advisor with a *conflicting interest* disclosing to a *counterparty*, where licensing works
    through the belief that the other party can now protect themselves. A run disclosing its own token
    usage to its own operator has no counterparty to exploit and no interest to conceal, and the
    "licence" it obtains is licence to do the work it was asked to do. And the normalisation-of-deviance
    framework's core condition — deviance repeated *without harm being noticed* — arguably fails here,
    since the harm (cost, and truncated work) is precisely what is being counted every time.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (i) the disclosures must lead to a decision, and
    they have not, through fifteen instances — the honesty-obligation reading requires a functioning
    recipient, which is exactly what the fifty-day silence removes; (ii) breach magnitude must be
    stable or shrinking, not growing — a 10x overrun is prima facie against this and it is measurable;
    (iii) the breach must not change the work product, or the cap is silently rewriting the standard —
    the QC sweep's stop-at-one-pair shows that it does; (iv) the rule as written must still be the rule
    anyone would enforce, which after fifteen consecutive tolerated breaches is doubtful and can be
    checked by asking whether any run has ever been stopped for it; (v) the disclosure must be
    consolidated rather than repeated, or it becomes the noise it was meant to prevent.
    How to test: Three in-house measurements, none requiring literature. (1) Magnitude series: extract
    the overrun ratio for all fifteen-plus disclosed breaches in chronological order and regress on
    index. A positive slope is the moral-licensing signature; a flat line supports the steelman.
    (2) Consequence audit: for each disclosed breach, record whether any change followed — to the
    budget, the cap, the standard, or the run's own behaviour. On the record so far the answer is one
    out of fifteen (the QC sweep), which is itself the finding. (3) Rule-status check: search the
    register for any instance, ever, of a run halted or output rejected on budget grounds. If none
    exists, the constraint has already been normalised out of force, and the assumption is not merely
    challenged but overtaken by events.

  SYSTEMIC-RISK-FLAG:

    Affected items: ASSUMPTION-1221, ASSUMPTION-1218, ASSUMPTION-1219
    Common vulnerability: **All three depend on an adjudicator who is absent, and each substitutes an
    act of signalling for an act of resolution.** 1221 discloses a breach and continues, treating
    disclosure as discharging the rule, where the party who could rule on "raise the budget, lower the
    cap, or lower the standard" has not appeared for fifty-plus days. 1218 escalates to that same party
    and treats the escalation as a terminal state. 1219 poses a disjunction that cannot be settled
    without an independent reviewer, so a six-member correction series proceeds unadjudicated and is
    logged as repair. In each case a correct local act — disclose, escalate, self-correct — stands in
    for an adjudication that never happens, and the local act is recorded as completion. The literature
    is specific about why this is unstable in the direction the register fears: disclosure without
    adjudication produces moral licensing and *more* biased behaviour, not less (Cain, Loewenstein &
    Moore); repeated unconsequenced deviation becomes the operative norm (normalisation of deviance);
    unanswered escalation is the structure of failure to rescue rather than a remedy for it; and
    self-correction without an external signal degrades accuracy rather than improving it
    (arXiv:2310.01798).
