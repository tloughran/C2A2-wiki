  Original item: ASSUMPTION-943
  Original statement: Whether partial correction of a systematic corpus defect is worse than none. Stated independently by three reviewers as an operating principle; never tested.

  Reading challenged: The stated operating principle — that partial correction of a systematic corpus defect is worse than no correction (because it converts a known, uniform defect into an unknown, non-uniform one and destroys the ability to reason about the residual). This search attacks that principle. Note that the principle's *own* supporting literature (selective-correction bias, partial-confounder-adjustment bias amplification, survivorship artefacts) is real and is 15a's territory; my finding is that the principle is stated far too strongly to survive as a general operating rule.

  Strength of challenge: Strong (against the general principle); Weak (against a narrowly-scoped version — see steelman)

  Summary: As a general operating principle, "partial correction is worse than none" is contradicted by the closest available empirical analogues. Corpus deduplication is the most direct: it is unavoidably partial, it is applied to a systematic corpus defect of exactly the kind at issue, and Lee et al. show two different partial regimes both improving memorisation, training efficiency and evaluation validity. The misinformation-correction literature attacks the underlying intuition: the backfire effect that would make partial correction actively harmful fails to replicate across 10,000+ subjects and 52 polarised issues, and two independent meta-analyses find corrections net-beneficial. The residual-influence finding (Walter & Tukachinsky) shows corrections are *incomplete*, which is not the same claim — incompleteness is the normal case, and the principle under test would forbid the entire practice of correction on those grounds. The important qualification is that the principle is not empty: there is a narrow, defensible version concerning *inferential* corpora, where a known uniform bias supports valid downstream reasoning that a partially-repaired non-uniform bias would silently invalidate. That version is worth keeping. The version three reviewers stated as a general operating principle is not, and it is dangerous precisely because it licenses inaction — see the connection to PRESUMPTION-762 in the systemic flag.

  Specific risks:
    - Inaction licence: an untested principle whose practical effect is "do not fix things you cannot fix completely" will systematically suppress remediation. Combined with 762's absent forcing function, this is a mechanism for permanent defect retention dressed as methodological rigour.
    - Unfalsifiable defence: because the principle predicts harm from an action not taken, it can never be disconfirmed by observation of the status quo. Three reviewers independently stating it is evidence of a shared prior, not of its truth (and see PRESUMPTION-751 — independent statement by correlated reviewers is weak evidence).
    - Overgeneralisation from a real narrow case: the principle is true for some corpora and some downstream uses. Applying it corpus-wide without identifying which uses are inferential is the error.
    - Opportunity cost: Lee et al. quantify what is forgone — 10× reduction in verbatim emission, faster convergence, and cleaner evaluation, all from a partial fix.
    - Comparator error: the principle implicitly compares partial correction against *full* correction. The decision-relevant comparison is against *no* correction, which is the state that actually obtains.

  Mitigations available:
    - Scope the principle explicitly to inferential uses: state it as "partial correction is harmful where downstream reasoning depends on the defect being uniform and characterised," and require that dependency to be named.
    - Record the correction, not just perform it: most of the feared harm comes from the residual being *unknown*. A partial correction with a logged manifest of what was and was not corrected leaves a characterised, not an unknown, residual — this dissolves the objection in most cases.
    - Prefer monotone corrections: apply only corrections that cannot introduce a new selection axis (e.g. exact-match deduplication), which is why Lee et al.'s EXACTSUBSTR result is the cleanest counterexample.
    - Supply replacement information, per Chan et al.: corrections that leave a gap are the weak ones; corrections that substitute a correct account are effective.
    - Test rather than assert: the item's own note says the principle was never tested. It is cheaply testable (below).

  STEELMAN:
    Item: ASSUMPTION-943
    Strongest counterargument: The counterexamples I found are all cases where the corpus is used *generatively* or where the correction is *monotone*. Deduplication removes redundancy along an axis orthogonal to any downstream inference, and misinformation correction operates on individual beliefs, not on a population estimate. Neither is the case the three reviewers were describing. The dangerous case is a corpus used *inferentially*, where a known systematic defect — say, a citation-format error affecting every item of a given class — permits valid downstream correction or discounting precisely because it is uniform. Repair half of those items and the defect becomes conditional on an unrecorded selection process: the residual is now non-uniform, the survivors are non-random, and every downstream statistic computed over the corpus is biased in an undiagnosable direction. This is the same structure as bias amplification from partial confounder adjustment, and it is real. Crucially, C2A2's corpus is used inferentially — the whole pipeline reasons over aggregate properties of the wiki. That the reviewers reached this independently is at least weak evidence they were reasoning about the actual use case rather than the abstract one. And the CleanML benchmark (Li et al., ICDE 2021) shows empirically that cleaning does not necessarily improve downstream models and is more likely to worsen fairness than improve it when techniques are not carefully chosen.
    What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe: (a) Each proposed partial correction must be classified as monotone (removes only, introduces no new selection axis) or non-monotone; only the latter is in scope for the principle; (b) for non-monotone corrections, the specific downstream inference that depends on defect uniformity must be *named* — if none can be named, the principle does not apply and the correction should proceed; (c) every partial correction must emit a manifest of what was corrected, so the residual is characterised rather than unknown, which converts the feared silent bias into an explicit and adjustable one; (d) the principle must never be invoked as a reason to defer without a written record, or it merges with the inaction pathology in PRESUMPTION-762.
    How to test: The item notes the principle was never tested; it is testable in one run. Take a known systematic corpus defect. Construct three arms: uncorrected, randomly-half-corrected (with manifest), and fully-corrected. Run the standard downstream pipeline analyses over all three and compare results against the fully-corrected arm as ground truth. If the half-corrected arm is *further* from truth than the uncorrected arm on any downstream statistic, the principle is confirmed for that statistic and should be scoped to it. If it is closer — which is what the deduplication and correction literatures predict — the principle is refuted for that class of defect and should be retired as a general operating rule. Repeat separately for a monotone (removal-only) and a non-monotone (rewrite) correction; my prediction is that the principle fails for the former and survives for the latter, and that the correct output is a scoping rule rather than a verdict.
