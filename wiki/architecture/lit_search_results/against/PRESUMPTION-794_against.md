The disconfirming result is therefore uncomfortable rather than reassuring: it says 14b has framed a real problem in a way that points at the wrong remedy (get the human back) and would produce a false all-clear the moment the human returns. ### Sources

1. **Green, B., 2022. "The flaws of policies requiring human oversight of government algorithms." *Computer Law & Security Review* 45:105681. (Also arXiv:2109.05067.)** [verified this run — author, title, venue, year confirmed via ScienceDirect, SSRN and author's own page] — The central challenge. Green surveys 41 policies prescribing human oversight and finds two flaws: people are unable to perform the oversight functions the policies assume, and the policies consequently *legitimise* faulty systems, providing "a false sense of security" and letting adopters shirk accountability. Directly contradicts the premise that an adjudicating human is what makes output valid; on Green's account the adjudication step is more often a legitimation ritual than a validity check.
2. **Sele, D., Chugunova, M., 2024. "Putting a human in the loop: Increasing uptake, but decreasing accuracy of automated decision-making." *PLOS ONE* 19(2):e0298037.** [verified this run — authors, title, journal, DOI-level identifier and N confirmed via PLOS and PubMed listings; full text not read] — The empirical counterexample, and the strongest single source in this file. Online experiment, N=292. Allowing participants to monitor and adjust algorithmic recommendations *increased* uptake by 7 percentage points and *decreased* decision accuracy; participants followed recommendations more closely and were **least likely to intervene on the least accurate recommendations**. This is the exact inverse of the assumed mechanism: the human-in-the-loop step improved acceptance while degrading correctness, and failed selectively on the cases where adjudication was most needed.
3. **Sterz, S. et al., 2024. "On the Quest for Effectiveness in Human Oversight: Interdisciplinary Perspectives." arXiv:2404.04059 (FAccT-track work).** [title, identifier and abstract-level claims verified this run; author list not fully confirmed — treat attribution as provisional] — Synthesises the human-factors position: oversight failures are driven not by lack of information but by predictable cognitive effects — automation bias, complacency, inappropriate reliance — and there is a **lack of empirical evidence** that humans reliably integrate with automated systems to produce better decisions than either alone. Establishes that "human adjudication confers validity" is an unevidenced default, not a finding.
4. **Acceptance sampling and audit sampling methodology; PCAOB AU Section 350, *Audit Sampling*.** [AU 350 existence and topic verified this run via pcaobus.org; general acceptance-sampling account from SixSigma/Taylor Enterprises practitioner sources, verified as retrieved] — The constructive challenge. Whole professions establish the validity of a population's properties without inspecting every item, via a stated sampling plan with declared risk levels. One practitioner formulation is directly on point: a sampling plan applied *after* 100% inspection functions as an audit of the inspection process. Validity is a property of the sampling design, not of continuous presence — which means an eleven-day gap is in principle recoverable by retrospective sampling rather than being permanently uninterpretable.
5. **Calderon, N. et al., 2025. "The Alternative Annotator Test for LLM-as-a-Judge: How to Statistically Justify Replacing Human Annotators with LLMs." arXiv:2501.10970.** [title and identifier verified this run; author attribution provisional; full text not read] — Supplies a concrete statistical procedure that answers, yes/no, whether an automated judge may stand in for human annotators on a given task. This is precisely the missing instrument: it converts "is the unadjudicated output valid?" from an unanswerable framing question into a test with a decision rule.
6. **Lights-out / dark-factory practice literature** (AMD Machines; MachineTracking; industrial-automation practitioner sources) [verified this run as web sources; practitioner, not peer-reviewed] — Documents that unattended operation is a normal industrial regime, and that its validity is established by *lagging verified proof* — third-shift output consistency against plan, utilisation correlated with completed parts, per unattended window. The design pattern is: no operator present, but every unattended window carries a machine-checkable acceptance criterion. Absence of a human is a designed setting there, and it is made safe by instrumentation, not by presence.
7. **Boundary condition against my own challenge — LLM-judge reliability.** Multiple 2025 sources (ICLR 2025, "Trust or Escalate: LLM Judges with…"; "LLMs-as-Judges in Automatic Evaluation of Free-Form QA," WiNLP 2025; "A Survey on LLM-as-a-Judge," arXiv:2411.15594) [titles/venues verified this run; contents not read] — Report that automated judges show *lower and more variable* inter-rater agreement than human annotators, are over-confident about their agreement with the human majority, and degrade specifically on subjective cases. This limits how far automated adjudication can substitute, and is the honest counterweight to sources 4–5.

### Strength of challenge: Strong

### Summary

The item's residual claim rests on an unstated premise — that human adjudication is the step that confers validity on the system's output — and that premise is the least evidenced part of the whole HITL design pattern. Green's survey of 41 oversight policies found no evidence that people can perform the oversight functions such policies assume, and argued the policies function as legitimation. Sele and Chugunova's experiment is worse than that: adding the human-in-the-loop step *reduced* accuracy, and reduced it selectively on the worst recommendations, which is the failure mode that makes an oversight step actively misleading rather than merely useless. The human-factors synthesis reports no reliable evidence that human-plus-system beats either alone. If that literature holds for C2A2's review gate, then the eleven unattended days are not a period in which the central claim went untested; they are a period in which the *absence of a validity mechanism became visible*, having previously been concealed by an adjudication step that was not doing the work attributed to it. Separately and more constructively, the framing that validity requires adjudication at a cadence is not how unattended systems in other fields establish validity at all: acceptance sampling, audit sampling and lights-out manufacturing all establish population-level validity from a declared sampling or acceptance design, per unattended window, without continuous presence — and there is now a published statistical test for whether an automated judge may stand in for a human annotator on a specific task. The honest limit on this challenge is that automated adjudication is measurably weaker than human annotation on subjective tasks, so substitution is a task-by-task empirical question, not a general licence.

### Specific risks

The dominant risk is a **false all-clear on the human's return**. If the fleet accepts 14b's framing, the natural resolution is "the human comes back, the backlog is adjudicated, validity is restored." On the evidence above, that resolution is unsupported: an overloaded reviewer facing eleven days of accumulated output is the exact population in which automation bias, complacency and inappropriate reliance are strongest, and Sele and Chugunova's finding says intervention will be rarest on the worst items. The system would then hold a *documented* adjudication of an eleven-day backlog and a validity claim that is weaker than the one it had while dark — with nothing in the metrics distinguishing those two either. This is 14b's own indistinguishability problem, reproduced one level up and with a certificate attached.

The second risk is that this challenge is misread as reassurance. It is not. It says the design has been running without a validated adjudication mechanism for its entire life, not for eleven days, and that the eleven days merely removed the covering. If the review gate was in fact doing real work, the challenge does not apply — but nobody has measured whether it was, and that measurement has never been scheduled.

Third, and specific to this fleet: 14b's own observation that each condition was "reported accurately by the agent that owns it, and by no one as a whole" identifies an aggregation gap that is independent of the human question and survives any resolution of it. Whether or not human adjudication confers validity, no component currently computes the fleet-level state, and that is a design deficiency with no human in it.

### Mitigations available

(a) **Measure the review gate before restoring it.** Take a sample of items the human adjudicated while attending and re-adjudicate them independently. If concordance is high and the human's dispositions changed outcomes, the gate is load-bearing and the eleven days are a genuine validity gap. If dispositions were near-uniform approvals, the gate was a legitimation step and restoring it will not restore anything. This is one experiment and it settles the item.
(b) **Convert the adjudication requirement into a sampling plan** with a stated acceptance criterion and risk level, so that validity is a declared property of a design rather than an implicit property of attendance. This also makes the eleven-day gap *recoverable* — retrospective sampling of the unattended window is a standard and legitimate procedure.
(c) **Run the alternative-annotator test** (Calderon et al.) on any automated adjudicator the fleet proposes, per task, rather than assuming substitutability or assuming its impossibility.
(d) **Adopt the lights-out pattern explicitly**: declare the human's absence a supported operating mode with a per-window machine-checkable acceptance criterion, rather than an exception being reported around. This is the direct constructive answer to the item's core observation and it does not require the human.
(e) **Build the aggregator.** One component whose only job is to compute and report the fleet-level state — days unattended, gates dark, queues stalled, detectors silent — because eleven agents reporting accurately in isolation is precisely the configuration in which nobody reports the system.

### Recommendation: CHALLENGED

## STEELMAN

**Item:** PRESUMPTION-794

**Strongest counterargument:** The item assumes the human's adjudication is what makes the output valid, and that assumption is the single least-supported element in the entire human-in-the-loop design pattern. Green surveyed 41 policies mandating human oversight of government algorithms and concluded that people cannot perform the functions those policies assume, and that the policies therefore *legitimise* the systems rather than checking them. Sele and Chugunova ran the experiment: putting a human in the loop raised uptake by seven points and lowered accuracy, with intervention rarest on the least accurate recommendations — the human step was not merely inert, it was selectively absent exactly where it mattered. The interdisciplinary human-factors synthesis reports no reliable evidence that human-plus-system outperforms either alone, and attributes oversight failure to automation bias and complacency rather than to information deficits. On this evidence the eleven dark days are not a novel untested setting. They are the first period in which the system has been visibly without a validity mechanism, rather than invisibly without one. And the framing has a cost: it points the remedy at the human's return, which is the worst possible moment to trust the gate, because a reviewer facing eleven days of backlog is the textbook population for complacent approval. Meanwhile every other field that runs unattended — acceptance sampling in manufacturing, audit sampling under AU 350, lights-out production with per-window verified output — establishes validity from a declared acceptance design rather than from presence, and a published statistical test now exists for deciding whether an automated judge may stand in for a human annotator on a given task. The correct reading of eleven unattended days is not "eleven days of untested output." It is "the system has never had a stated acceptance criterion, and the human was standing where one should have been."

**What would need to be true for C2A2 to be safe:** The review gate must be demonstrably load-bearing — measurable as a non-trivial rate at which human adjudication *changes* dispositions, established on a sample, not assumed. And the system must carry a per-window acceptance criterion that can be evaluated without the human, so that unattended operation is a supported mode with a defined validity claim rather than a reported exception. Given both, an eleven-day gap is a bounded, recoverable sampling problem. Given neither — the current state — the item's alarm is warranted, but its diagnosis is wrong: the deficiency is a missing acceptance design, not a missing human, and restoring the human will conceal rather than repair it.

**How to test:** The decisive test is cheap and historical. Pull every item the human adjudicated during the last attended period and compute the **override rate** — the fraction where the human's disposition differed from what the system proposed or defaulted to. A rate near zero means the gate was ratification, the eleven days changed nothing epistemically, and the item's Critical grading rests on a mechanism that was never operating. A substantial rate means the gate was doing real work, the eleven days are a genuine validity gap, and the item is correct at full strength. Either result is decision-relevant and neither requires the human's participation to obtain. Second test, prospective: independently re-adjudicate a random sample of the eleven days' output and compare against whatever the fleet recorded, giving a direct estimate of what the unattended period's error rate actually is — which is the number 14b correctly observes nobody currently has. Third: apply the alternative-annotator test to any proposed automated stand-in before relying on it.
