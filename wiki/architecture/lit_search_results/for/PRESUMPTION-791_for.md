    1. DeMillo, R.A., Lipton, R.J. & Sayward, F.G. (1978). "Hints on Test Data Selection: Help for the Practicing Programmer." IEEE Computer 11(4):34-41. — The origin of the method, and it answers the naming question for the software case. Faults are DELIBERATELY SEEDED into the program by simple syntactic changes, producing mutants; the test suite's adequacy is measured by the proportion of mutants it KILLS. The two supporting hypotheses are part of the answer: the COMPETENT PROGRAMMER HYPOTHESIS (real programs are close to correct, so realistic faults are small syntactic departures) and the COUPLING EFFECT (test data that detects simple faults tends also to detect complex ones). The move at the heart of the item — "would this test have failed on the broken version?" — is the definition of killing a mutant, and it has been the field's adequacy criterion for nearly fifty years. The transfer is direct: running the check against a known-broken artefact is a negative control, its value is that it catches failure modes nobody predicted, and its status in the disciplines that use it is ROUTINE rather than optional.   Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: The corrective proposition is strongly supported, and the queue's naming question has a clean answer: the operation "would this test have failed on the broken version?" is called KILLING A MUTANT in software engineering, a NEGATIVE CONTROL in experimental biology and epidemiology, and watching the test go red in test-driven development. All three exist because the same failure recurs — a check that passes tells you nothing until you have established it was capable of not passing. Mutation testing has made this quantitative for nearly fifty years: seed a fault, see whether the suite detects it, and report the mutation score, so an unvalidated pass is a measurable gap rather than a stylistic objection. Lipsitch et al. supply the more general form and the reason it matters most here — the negative control's distinctive value is detecting UNSUSPECTED sources of spurious inference, which is precisely what a single targeted adversarial test cannot do. Tversky and Kahneman supply the reason the error is expected rather than careless: trained experts systematically over-estimate what a single small-sample result implies about replication. The decisive local fact, though, is not in the literature at all: this register already holds the rule. PREMISE-137's load-bearing condition states that an invariant counts as coverage only after the known defect has been deliberately reintroduced and the invariant confirmed to fire, and PREMISE-150 states that adequacy claims require seeded defects rather than observed pass rates. Two runs concluded a fix held from one pass on a day when the fleet's own nightly verifier refused a zero without a negative control and the fleet's own register already required mutation validation.
