  Sources:
    1. Sunstein, Cass R. 1995. "Incompletely Theorized Agreements." Harvard Law Review 108(7): 1733–1772. — Sunstein argues that participants in legal and political controversies try to produce incompletely theorized agreements on particular outcomes, agreeing on a result and on relatively narrow or low-level explanations without needing to agree on fundamental principles. This is the precise logical structure of the assumption: taking the weaker (less theoretically committed) reading of a contested claim as the shared point of agreement, allowing forward progress without requiring concession on deeper disagreements.

    2. Rawls, John. 1993. Political Liberalism. Columbia University Press. — Rawls's overlapping consensus addresses the fact of reasonable pluralism: citizens holding incompatible comprehensive doctrines can nonetheless converge on a shared political conception of justice. The overlapping consensus is reached by identifying what is held in common at a less theoretically committed level, which is structurally identical to adopting the weaker reading as the basis of agreement. Rawls demonstrates that this is not a concession of one's deeper views but a rational strategy for stable political cooperation.

    3. Gilbert, Margaret. 1989. On Social Facts. Princeton University Press. — Gilbert argues that joint commitment — a commitment of two or more persons — lies at the core of social phenomena including collective belief and acting together. A "plural subject" is constituted by a joint commitment to hold a position "as a body." When the joint commitment is formed around the weaker shared reading, this is not a concession but a genuine jointly-constituted agreement, distinct from each party privately abandoning their stronger reading. Gilbert's framework provides the social-ontological grounding for why weaker-reading agreement is a real, non-concessionary convergence.

    4. List, Christian, and Philip Pettit. 2002. "Aggregating Sets of Judgments: An Impossibility Result." Economics and Philosophy 18(1): 89–110. — List and Pettit's discursive-dilemma result shows that conclusion-level agreement can be achieved even where premise-level judgments diverge, and that aggregation procedures that operate at the conclusion level (rather than requiring premise-by-premise unanimity) can generate consistent collective judgments. Adopting the weaker reading operates at an analogous level: it enables outcome-agreement without requiring full premise alignment. This supports the claim that weaker-reading agreement is a rational non-concessionary strategy.

    5. Estlund, David. 1997. "Beyond Fairness and Deliberation: The Epistemic Dimension of Democratic Authority." In James Bohman and William Rehg (eds.), Deliberative Democracy: Essays on Reason and Politics. MIT Press. — Estlund discusses how deliberative convergence can be rational without requiring full theoretical alignment, supporting the broader claim that agreement on weaker or less specified positions is a legitimate epistemic and political outcome rather than a compromise.

  Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: Three independent theoretical traditions — Sunstein's incompletely theorized agreements, Rawls's overlapping consensus, and Gilbert's joint-commitment theory — each support the claim that adopting a weaker shared reading of a contested position constitutes a genuine, non-concessionary resolution strategy. Sunstein is the most direct: his framework was developed precisely to explain how progress on contested questions can be made by agreement at a lower level of theoretical commitment. Rawls shows this is rationally stable under pluralism. Gilbert provides the social-ontological account of why such agreement is real and not merely apparent. List and Pettit add formal support from judgment-aggregation theory.

  Caveats: The claim that taking the weaker reading "resolves the way forward" assumes that the parties genuinely hold the weaker reading as a shared commitment and are not merely deferring deeper disagreement. Sunstein and Rawls both acknowledge that incompletely theorized agreements and overlapping consensus are not final resolutions but stable and productive resting points. The C2A2 ladder context is more structured than the political philosophy contexts from which these theories were developed, but the logical structure is directly analogous.
