  Sources:
    1. Vaughan, D. (1996). *The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at
       NASA*. [established-work] — The canonical account of normalisation of deviance: an organisation
       becomes so accustomed to a deviation that it is no longer perceived as deviant. Crucially for this
       item, the Challenger case is one in which the disconfirming information was *known and
       communicated* and production pressure, resource competition and structural secrecy nonetheless
       carried the launch. This is the strongest available support for 14b's claim that discovery and
       disclosure do not by themselves produce action.
    2. NASA Safety & Mission Assurance safety message (2014-11-03), "The Cost of Silence: Normalization
       of Deviance and Groupthink," sma.nasa.gov. [agency safety document] — NASA's own restatement of the
       Vaughan finding, presented as an operative hazard rather than history. Notes that organisational
       ignorance prevents attempts to stop unfolding harm.
    3. "When Doing Wrong Feels So Right: Normalization of Deviance." PubMed 25742063. (author list not
       verified) — Applies the concept in a clinical safety context; supports transferability of the
       mechanism beyond aerospace.
    4. Stop-work authority literature (Berkeley Lab EHS Manual Pub-3000 Ch. 46, "Safety Stop Work Policy";
       ISHN, "The 'stop work authority' approach"; Construction Executive, "Stop-Work Authority Becomes
       Stop-Work Responsibility"). [institutional policy and practitioner sources] — Establish that in
       safety-critical settings the recognised control is not disclosure but *authority to halt*, vested
       at the level where the hazard is observed. Sources further state that lack of management support
       for exercising that authority "can enable a safety issue to escalate." This supports 14b's implied
       distinction: C2A2's run had disclosure (REVISE-350) but not stop authority, and the eleven-day
       silence is the documented failure mode of a disclosure-only control.
    5. Perrow, C. (1984). *Normal Accidents*. [established-work] — Theoretical grounding for why, in
       tightly coupled systems, a control that requires an external decision-maker to intervene in time
       is not a reliable control.
    6. Reflexive support from the AI literature: "The Verification Paradox: Why Agents Cannot
       Automatically Validate Themselves" (yaihq.com) [grey], and the intrinsic-self-correction findings
       in CRITIC (arXiv:2305.11738) — a verifier sharing the same priors and error surface as the system
       it evaluates "may increase confidence without increasing reliability," and self-correction without
       external feedback often fails. Supports the narrower claim that a pipeline is poorly placed to act
       on findings about itself.

  Strength of support: Moderate-to-Strong

  Summary: 14b's diagnosis is supported from two directions. The organisational-safety literature — with
  Vaughan's Challenger account as the canonical case and NASA's own safety messaging as its
  institutionalised form — establishes that disconfirming information being known, documented and
  transmitted is routinely insufficient to stop the activity it disconfirms, and that production pressure
  and structural features of the organisation carry the work forward regardless. The stop-work-authority
  literature supplies the corresponding positive control: in safety-critical settings the recognised
  remedy is authority vested at the point of observation to halt work, not a route for reporting upward;
  and lack of support at the escalation target is named as the condition that lets an issue escalate. That
  the C2A2 run completed its cohort, minted three premises, and routed the remedy to an unresponsive human
  is a textbook instance: it had disclosure, it lacked stop authority, and the escalation target was the
  bottleneck. The AI self-verification literature adds that a pipeline evaluating its own validity is
  structurally the wrong instrument for the judgement it was being asked to make.

  Caveats: Vaughan and Perrow are classic works cited from established knowledge — no page numbers or
  quotations are asserted here beyond what appeared in the sources consulted. The stop-work-authority
  material is institutional policy and practitioner commentary, not empirical study; I found no
  quantitative evidence on the effectiveness of stop-work authority versus disclosure-only controls, so
  the "authority beats disclosure" claim is supported normatively rather than empirically. All safety
  sources concern human organisations with legal and physical stakes; whether an agent pipeline should
  hold analogous halt authority is a governance question the literature does not answer and this search
  makes no recommendation on. The eleven-day non-response is a fact about this project. Search scope:
  moderate — covered normalisation of deviance, organisational silence and stop-work authority; did NOT
  cover the whistleblowing/voice literature (Morrison & Milliken on organisational silence; Edmondson on
  psychological safety), which would strengthen the disclosure-insufficiency limb considerably and
  remains unsearched.
