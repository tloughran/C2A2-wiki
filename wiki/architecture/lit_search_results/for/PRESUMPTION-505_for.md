Fail-safe design literature: "Fail-safe" (Wikipedia); Inspiro, "How to Design Fail-Safe Systems for Critical Embedded Applications"; ITU Online, "What Is Fail-Safe?" — Direct theoretical grounding for the item's central distinction. A fail-safe system "is designed to avoid situations where a fault preventing the system from performing its safety function goes undetected," with the explicit warning that "a sensor failing to indicate its failure may lead to the inability to detect an alarm condition." The field's operative classification is *detected* versus *undetected* failure, and the design metric is diagnostic coverage — the fraction of failures the monitor can see at all. PRESUMPTION-505's proposed test ("is the pass state reachable when the monitored subsystem is dead?") is the standard diagnostic-coverage question, and asking it per health check is established safety practice.
    2. Nenvitech, "Is your sensor fail-safe?"; PLCtalk sensor fail-safe mode discussion; Industrial Monitor Direct, "Understanding Fail-Safe PLC Systems." — Empirical grounding for the specific inversion mechanism. Standard sensors "can fail in either an always-ON or always-OFF state, and neither of these faults can be detected until the machine fails to operate correctly." A stuck sensor whose stuck value is the nominal value is a monitor that reports healthy precisely because it is dead — the item's "more reassuring as the condition worsens," in its simplest physical form. The industrial remedy — line monitoring, pulsed test signals, dynamic rather than static signalling — is the direct precedent for the item's implied fix: require the health check to demonstrate liveness, not merely to fail to alarm.

  Summary: The generalisation PRESUMPTION-505 performs is one that safety engineering performed decades ago and encoded in its basic vocabulary. The distinction between graceful degradation and inversion is the distinction between detected and undetected failure, and the entire fail-safe design tradition exists because the undetected case — a monitor whose failure presents as a nominal reading — is the one that defeats redundancy. The mechanism is documented in its purest form in sensor engineering, where a stuck-at-nominal sensor is indistinguishable from a healthy one until the system fails outright, and the standard countermeasures are precisely inversions of polarity: pulsed test signals, line monitoring, and watchdogs that must be actively fed on an independent timebase. Three of the item's four cases are absence-of-complaint detectors, which is the polarity this literature says is unsafe. The gray-failure work supports the generalisation step itself: because the pathology inheres in the per-component monitoring pattern rather than in any individual detector, encountering four instances across unrelated subsystems on one day is the predicted consequence of a shared design pattern, not a coincidence requiring separate explanation for each. The item's proposed audit — for each health check, ask whether its pass state is reachable when the monitored subsystem is dead, and count how many current greens survive — is a restatement of diagnostic-coverage analysis and is the correct, cheap, and standard procedure.

  Caveats: (a) The strongest sources are from physical safety-instrumented systems with hardware failure modes and quantified diagnostic coverage; C2A2's detectors fail through logic and read-set conditions, so the transfer is of the design principle, not of the quantitative apparatus. Safe-failure-fraction figures from SIL practice do not carry over. (b) The remedy has a well-known cost the item does not weigh: converting absence-of-complaint checks to affirmative liveness checks raises the false-positive rate, and the resulting alert volume is the alert-fatigue failure PRESUMPTION-495 named (44% of outages linked to suppressed alerts, per the 07-19 systemic-risk flag). Polarity inversion is not free and should be applied selectively, by criticality, which is also how SIL practice allocates diagnostic coverage. (c) The four cited instances are an internal-record claim outside search scope; I make no finding on whether all four are genuine inversions, and at least one (the vault census) may be a definitional-comparability problem rather than a detector inversion — that is ASSUMPTION-474's diagnosis of the same event, and the two readings are not obviously compatible. Separating them matters, because a census that miscounts is not the same defect as a detector that inverts, and only the latter is covered by this literature. (d) Sources 2 and 5 are practitioner material and a patent; sources 1, 3, 4 carry the weight. (e) The item's observation that this was "stated locally once, generalized by nobody" is not itself literature-testable, though it is consistent with the gray-failure literature's finding that per-component observation structurally lacks a system-level view — the absence of a generaliser is the same architectural gap at the meta level.
