  Sources:
    1. Ivers, N., Grimshaw, J.M. et al., 2025/2026. "Audit and feedback: effects on professional practice." Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, CD000259.pub5 (PMID 40130784). — 292 studies, 678 arms; synthesis of 558 dichotomous practice outcomes from 177 studies gives a MEDIAN ABSOLUTE IMPROVEMENT OF 2.7% (IQR 0.0–8.6). This is the best-quantified estimate available for a purely informational control: real, positive, and very small, with the interquartile range including zero.
    2. Farmer, A.P. et al. (Cochrane EPOC). "Printed educational materials: effects on professional practice and healthcare outcomes." — Small effects on practice from written material alone; the SUPPORT summary of this review is the standard citation for "documents distributed to practitioners produce measurable but marginal change."
    3. "Safety of surgery: quality assessment of meta-analyses on the WHO checklist." Annals of Medicine and Surgery, 2024 (PMC11060208). — The strongest FOR case: a paper artefact associated with mortality RR 0.77 (95% CI 0.67–0.89), all complications RR 0.56, infectious complications RR 0.44. Important qualification carried by the same source: included studies are predominantly observational and AMSTAR-2 overall confidence is rated CRITICALLY LOW. Also note the WHO checklist is not a passive note — it mandates a verbal, timed, multi-person pause, i.e. a procedural forcing function wearing a document's clothing.
    4. Kellogg, K.M. et al., 2017. "Our current approach to root cause analysis: is it contributing to our failure to improve patient safety?" BMJ Quality & Safety (PMID 27940638). — The most directly on-point source. The most common proposed solution types were training (20%), process change (19.6%) and policy reinforcement (15.2%); multiple event types RECURRED despite repeated RCAs over the study period. This is the published analogue of "three recorded recurrences against a written note."
    5. "Our first review: an evaluation of effectiveness of root cause analysis recommendations in Hong Kong public hospitals," 2020 (PMC7275338). — Of 760 RCA recommendations, only 18 (2%) rated strong, 116 (15%) medium, 626 (82%) WEAK; most were 'training and education' (61%), 'additional study/review' (14%) and 'review/enhancement of policy/guideline' (5%). Weaker actions were less likely to decrease event recurrence. Gives a base rate for how often organisations reach for the documented-warning remedy: ~82% of the time.
    6. Institute for Healthcare Improvement / NPSF, 2015. "RCA²: Improving Root Cause Analyses and Actions to Prevent Harm." — Codifies the action hierarchy: "actions have comparatively more strength the less they depend on human action/attention for effectiveness," with forcing functions (physically incompatible connectors) as Stronger and double-checks/policies as Weaker. The normative statement of the item's own thesis.
    7. ISMP Hierarchy of Effectiveness (ECRI/ISMP) and the general hierarchy of hazard controls (see also "The Hierarchy of Controls as an Approach to Visualize the Impact of Occupational Safety and Health Coordination," PMC8910555). — Human-factors consensus that person-focused interventions (education, new rules) are easier to implement and have higher failure tendency because they rely on individual attention and vigilance, and that layering weak with strong controls outperforms either alone.
    8. "Method for evaluating learning from incidents using the idea of 'level of learning'." Safety Science (ScienceDirect S095042301100012X); and OSHwiki, "Learning from incidents and accidents" (European Agency for Safety and Health at Work). — Organisational-learning literature: incident-report analysis and improvement implementation "are often ineffective and the full lessons are therefore seldom learned"; investigations stop at proximal events. Supports the recurrence-despite-repository finding at the organisational level.

  Strength of support: Weak

  Summary: The supporting case for documented-warning controls is real but small and conditional. Cochrane's audit-and-feedback review gives the cleanest number: a median 2.7% absolute improvement in desired practice, with an interquartile range whose lower bound is zero — a written intervention does something, on average, and frequently nothing. The one strong-looking positive is the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist (mortality RR 0.77), but it is a poor precedent for a prose memory note on two grounds: the evidence base is observational with critically low AMSTAR-2 confidence, and the intervention is a mandated verbal pause with multi-party participation rather than a document a reader must remember to consult. Against that, the RCA literature supplies both a base rate and a direct precedent for the item's own evidence: 82% of corrective-action recommendations are of the weak (training / policy / review) class, weaker actions are less likely to reduce recurrence, and event types demonstrably recurred despite repeated RCAs. Cochrane also notes audit-and-feedback works best when delivered by a supervisor or colleague, repeated, given verbally AND in writing, with explicit targets and an action plan — a specification that a passive note in a register fails on every clause. The human-factors hierarchy of controls states the item's conclusion as consensus: effectiveness rises as dependence on human attention falls.

  Caveats: (a) The evidence is overwhelmingly healthcare and occupational safety; the transfer to an agent pipeline is by structural analogy. (b) The support-side sources measure INFORMATIONAL interventions delivered to humans over time (audit and feedback, printed materials), not a note in a file read by a later instance of the same process — the closest analogue is arguably the checklist, which is the strongest positive but the most confounded. (c) Effect heterogeneity is extreme: audit and feedback works best where baseline performance is poor, which is a condition the C2A2 case plausibly meets and which slightly raises the expected effect. (d) n=3 recurrences is not itself a rate; the literature supports the direction but the item's in-hand evidence remains anecdotal. (e) The RCA² action hierarchy is an expert-consensus framework, not an empirical result; the Hong Kong review supplies the empirical backing but is single-system.

  Search scope: Comprehensive on the hierarchy of controls, RCA corrective-action strength, and Cochrane evidence for informational interventions; adequate on checklist effectiveness and lessons-learned/organisational-learning repositories. Not searched: the software-engineering literature on lint rules and CI gates versus style guides, which is the closest domain-matched analogue and would materially sharpen this item. Broader search recommended there.
