  Sources:
    1. Clark & Brennan, 1991. "Grounding in Communication." — Mutual understanding is not achieved by transmission alone; it requires evidence of *uptake/acceptance* by the participants — understanding is jointly ratified in interaction, not read off the sentences.
    2. Clark & Schaefer, 1989. "Contributing to Discourse." — Common ground is established through a presentation–acceptance cycle in which the addressee's acceptance (verbal/nonverbal) is what closes a contribution; ratification by participants is constitutive of "understood."
    3. Member checking / respondent validation (Lincoln & Guba; Birt et al., 2016, Qual Health Res). — An established qualitative validity technique in which the *original informant* confirms whether the researcher captured their meaning — a recognized methodological basis for author-ratification as a validity signal.
    4. Erdmann & Potthoff, 2023 (Int J Qual Methods). — Formalizes member-check method choice, treating participant confirmation as a legitimate (if bounded) validity operation.

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: There is real, established support for the *weaker* core of the assumption: in both psycholinguistics (grounding theory) and qualitative methodology (respondent validation), whether a claim was "understood/captured" is properly settled by the participants' own acceptance rather than by a third party's semantic mapping between sentences. Grounding theory in particular makes addressee uptake constitutive of mutual understanding, which directly supports "ratified, not mapped." Support is for participant ratification as *a* — indeed a privileged — validity criterion.

  Caveats: The literature supports participant ratification as a strong signal, but grounding is bilateral (speaker *and* addressee jointly), and member-checking sources explicitly stop short of making the informant the *sole* arbiter. The word "only/sole" in the assumption is stronger than what the supportive literature licenses.

  Recommendation: PARTIALLY-SUPPORTED
