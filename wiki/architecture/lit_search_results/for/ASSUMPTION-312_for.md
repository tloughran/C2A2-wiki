    1. MacIntyre, Alasdair. 1988. Whose Justice? Which Rationality? University of Notre Dame Press. — MacIntyre argues that there is no rationality that is not the rationality of some tradition, and that justification of large-scale viewpoints must proceed historically. He outlines how a tradition achieves rational superiority by explaining both its own success and why rival traditions failed their own internal challenges. This establishes the methodological framework invoked by the assumption: tradition-constituted rational enquiry as the mode of adjudication between conflicting traditions.

    2. MacIntyre, Alasdair. 1990. Three Rival Versions of Moral Enquiry: Encyclopaedia, Genealogy, and Tradition. University of Notre Dame Press. — MacIntyre presents the 1988 Gifford Lectures and argues that Thomism alone of the three versions (encyclopaedic, genealogical, traditional) can account for the insights and failures of the other two, emerging as rationally superior. The methodological claim — that adequate resolution requires the kind of tradition-internal, historically-constituted rational enquiry exemplified by Thomism — is the direct textual basis for the assumption as stated. The qualification in the assumption ("methodological sense," "stronger sole-source claim not asserted") maps precisely onto the distinction between MacIntyre's methodological thesis and the stronger doctrinal or theological claim.

    3. Lutz, Christopher Stephen. 2004. Tradition in the Ethics of Alasdair MacIntyre: Relativism, Thomism, and Philosophy. Lexington Books. — Lutz provides a systematic secondary treatment of MacIntyre's tradition-constituted enquiry, rebutting the charge that it collapses into relativism. Lutz's analysis supports reading MacIntyre's Thomism as a methodological rather than a merely confessional commitment, and clarifies what "rational superiority" means in the inter-traditional context — directly supporting the qualified reading expressed in the assumption.

    4. Porter, Jean. 1993. "Openness and Constraint: Moral Reflection as Tradition-Guided Enquiry in Alasdair MacIntyre's Recent Works." Journal of Religion 73(4): 514–536. — Porter examines MacIntyre's account of how traditions make rational progress and adjudicate between rivals. Her reading confirms that MacIntyre's methodological Thomism is a claim about the structure of adequate rational resolution rather than a claim that only Thomists can participate in such resolution, consistent with the assumption's careful qualification.

    5. Nicholas, Jeffrey L. 2012. Reason, Tradition, and the Good: MacIntyre's Tradition-Constituted Reason and Frankfurt-School Critical Theory. University of Notre Dame Press. — Nicholas places MacIntyre's tradition-constituted reason in comparative dialogue with other rationality frameworks, further confirming that the methodological dimension of MacIntyre's Thomism is separable from its specifically theological content and represents a general epistemological position about adequate adjudication between rival traditions.
