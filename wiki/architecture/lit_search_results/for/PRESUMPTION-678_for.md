  Sources:
    1. Institute of Internal Auditors, Global Internal Audit Standards /
       Quality Assessment programme (external assessment requirement). — Cuts
       directly against the presumption. The governing professional standard
       for the audit function requires an external quality assessment at least
       once every five years, conducted by a qualified independent assessor or
       team from outside the organisation, and states a preference that the
       assessor not be the organisation's financial-statement auditor or any
       party co-sourcing the function. The whole design premise is that the
       function which checks everything else is the one function that cannot
       check itself, and therefore needs a scheduled external channel. The
       118-day undetected interval in this item is the failure mode the
       five-year cadence exists to bound.
    2. INTOSAI GUID 1900, Peer Review Guidelines (issai.org); and World Bank
       governance note, "External Oversight of Supreme Audit Institutions: Who
       Audits the Auditor?" (documents.worldbank.org). — Same direction, at the
       apex of public-sector governance. Supreme Audit Institutions, the bodies
       with the strongest independence claim in any state, are nonetheless
       subjected to external oversight by the legislature and to structured
       peer review by other SAIs; INTOSAI has endorsed principles for external
       SAI oversight. No source located treats apex independence as grounds for
       exemption from review — independence is treated as a reason to design a
       different review channel, not to remove one.
    3. Santos, V.S. et al. [author list uncertain — confirm before reuse],
       2018. "Conflict Detection among Multiple Norms in Multi-Agent Systems."
       Applied Artificial Intelligence 32(x). And: "Detection and Resolution of
       Normative Conflicts in Multi-agent Systems," AAMAS 2018, pp. 1306-. —
       The closest formal analogue to a premise register. Norm-conflict
       checkers are defined over the norm set as a whole: conflict is the
       relation "fulfilment of one norm violates another," and checkers are
       built to detect conflict between two or more norms simultaneously
       without privileged members. Nothing in the formalism admits a norm that
       is enrolled in the system but excluded from the conflict check. A
       charter that governs an agent but is not in the register is, in this
       framework, not an exempt norm — it is an undetectable one.
    4. Normative hierarchy in legal systems (standard doctrine; see e.g.
       "Normative Hierarchy in International Law," and constitutional-supremacy
       treatments). — The presumption inverts the established ordering. In
       hierarchical norm systems, a lower-level rule that contradicts a
       higher-level rule is invalid to the extent of the contradiction. A
       charter is not thereby exempt from the premise register; it is
       subordinate to it, and the ACTIVE premises are the higher tier. The
       doctrine does supply a genuine refinement: apparent conflicts must be
       distinguished from real ones, since rules may govern different
       populations, times or objects, and priority rules or explicit exceptions
       may already resolve them. That test is available here and was not run
       for 118 days.
    5. Hart, H.L.A., 1961. The Concept of Law. [UNVERIFIED — cited from
       established knowledge; secondary summaries confirmed this session, the
       primary text was not] — The only route to partial support found. Hart
       distinguishes primary rules, which impose duties, from secondary rules,
       which confer powers and govern the making and identification of primary
       rules; the rule of recognition operates at a meta-level. This does
       supply a principled sense in which a charter is a different kind of rule
       from a premise, and so is not straightforwardly "governed by" the
       premise register in the way an object-level action is. But it does not
       yield exemption from consistency: on Hart's own account the rule of
       recognition is validated by the practice of officials, i.e. from
       outside, which is precisely how this contradiction was found.
    6. Legislative self-exemption: CalMatters, February 2023, "Rules for thee:
       How California Legislature skirts its own laws"; Congressional
       Accountability Act of 1995 (PL 104-1). — Descriptive prevalence, not
       normative support, and the trajectory runs the other way. Self-exemption
       by rule-making bodies is empirically common and legally permitted absent
       a specific constitutional bar. But every source located frames it as a
       legitimacy defect, and the notable legislative response — the
       Congressional Accountability Act — applied a dozen civil-rights, labour
       and workplace-safety statutes to the legislative branch, i.e. removed
       the exemption rather than defending it.
    7. Meta-regulation literature: Grabosky, P., "Meta-regulation" (ANU Press
       chapter, press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n2304/pdf/ch09.pdf);
       Baldwin, Cave & Lodge, Understanding Regulation, ch. on
       "Self-regulation, Meta-regulation, and Regulatory Networks" (OUP)
       [chapter confirmed to exist; page numbers not verified]. — Establishes
       that the regress the item points at is a recognised open problem, not a
       settled licence. Meta-regulation is defined as oversight of regulation
       itself; the standing criticisms concern mandate, accountability and
       procedural fairness at the oversight layer. The literature's stance is
       that the regress must be terminated by an institutional device (a
       scrutiny board, a legislature, a peer network), never by declaring the
       top layer self-validating.

  Summary: No literature was located that supports charter-level exemption from
    a system's own norm register, and the professional standards nearest to the
    case were written specifically to prevent it. The IIA's five-yearly
    external quality assessment and INTOSAI's SAI peer-review guidelines both
    encode the same reasoning: the function that checks everything else is
    structurally unable to check itself, so its review must be scheduled and
    must come from outside. Formal norm-conflict detection in multi-agent
    systems admits no privileged norm, and legal normative hierarchy places a
    charter below, not outside, the higher-tier norms. The one principled
    distinction found — Hart's primary/secondary rule split — supports the
    weaker claim that a charter is a different kind of rule, not the claim that
    it is exempt from consistency checking; Hart's own rule of recognition is
    validated externally. Legislative self-exemption is common but is
    universally characterised as a defect, and the leading corrective
    (Congressional Accountability Act 1995) removed rather than justified it.
    The item's own facts match the standards' predicted failure signature
    exactly: a self-governing rule, no internal detection channel, and
    discovery from outside after an interval far longer than any recommended
    assessment cadence.

  Caveats: The audit standards were written for organisational functions with a
    board or audit committee to report to; C2A2's structural analogue for that
    body is not identified in this item, so the transfer specifies a
    requirement without specifying who discharges it. The normative-conflict
    formalisms assume the norms are all expressed in a common representation —
    if a charter is written in prose and premises are register entries, the
    conflict may be genuinely undetectable rather than merely unchecked, which
    is a representation problem the cited work does not solve. The Hart
    citation is marked unverified as to primary text. Source 3's author list is
    uncertain and should be confirmed before this file is cited onward. Note
    also the scope escalation 14b performed: the evidence base here concerns
    single named charters, and no located source addresses whether the
    exemption generalises across a *class* of agent charters, which is what the
    item asserts.
