  Strength of support: Weak-to-Moderate

  Summary: There is a well-established human-judgment literature showing that one evaluator can argue
  opposed positions productively (consider-the-opposite, devil's advocacy, dialectical bootstrapping), and
  that assigning the opposing role is what produces the corrective effect rather than a change of person.
  Herzog and Hertwig give the strongest quantitative statement: within-person opposition recovers about
  half the benefit of a genuinely independent second judge. The LLM multi-persona and Self-Contrast results
  show the same mechanism works within one model context. This supports the weaker reading of the
  presumption (one context can write both a FOR on X and a later AGAINST on Y without the second being
  useless), and gives a rough bound on how much independence is retained.

  Caveats: (a) Herzog and Hertwig's own number cuts both ways: within-mind opposition retains roughly half,
  not all, of the independence of a second judge, so "does not condition" is stronger than the evidence.
  (b) The human sources address arguing both sides of the SAME item; none addresses the cross-item case
  (FOR on X, then AGAINST on related Y), which is what PRESUMPTION-914 actually asserts. (c) Encountered
  incidentally and NOT searched here: an LLM literature on task interference and prior-context sensitivity
  ("LLM Task Interference," arXiv:2402.18216; "Evaluating the Sensitivity of LLMs to Prior Context,"
  arXiv:2506.00069) reports substantial accuracy effects from unrelated prior context; this bears directly
  on the "carry-over is small" half of the claim and is left for 15b. (d) The presumption's second clause
  ("independence is a property of file-reading, not writer state") is a definitional stance; no source
  argues for it as such. (e) Search scope: preliminary — 8 queries; the order-effects/anchoring literature
  on sequential judgments and the Schwenk & Valacich 1994 individual-vs-group study were not read.
