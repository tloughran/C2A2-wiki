  Sources:
    1. Walters, W.H. & Wilder, E.I., 2023. "Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT." Scientific Reports, 13:14045. — 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated; among the real citations, 43% (GPT-3.5) and 24% (GPT-4) contained substantive errors. The single best base-rate source: even at the better model's rate, roughly 40% of a reference list is fabricated or materially wrong.
    2. "ChatGPT and artificial hallucinations in stem cell research: assessing the accuracy of generated references — a preliminary study," 2023 (PMC10553015). — Of 86 references, 15.12% fabricated and 9.30% erroneous. Shows the base rate is domain-dependent but never near zero.
    3. "Hallucinated Citation Analysis: Delving into Student-Submitted AI-Generated Sources at the University of Mississippi." The Serials Librarian, 85(5-6), 2024. — Library-side audit of AI-generated citations in real submitted work; documents that fabrication survives into finished artefacts when no verification gate exists.
    4. "Artificial intelligence in systematic reviews and meta-analyses: task-specific performance, residual error quantification, and human oversight." Journal of Clinical Epidemiology / ScienceDirect S1877056826002148, 2026. — Task-decomposed residual error: generative AI missed 68%–96% of relevant studies in searching, incorrect inclusion 0%–29%, incorrect exclusion 1%–83%, incorrect data extraction 4%–31%, incorrect risk-of-bias assessment 10%–56%. Concludes current evidence does not support use in evidence synthesis without human verification. This is the closest published statement to "verification is a gate, not a virtue."
    5. Jergas, H. & Baethge, C., 2015. "Quotation accuracy in medical journal articles — a systematic review and meta-analysis." PeerJ (PMC4627914). — Establishes the HUMAN baseline: quotation errors in a substantial minority of citations even without AI involvement. Supports a floor that is independent of the drafting method.
    6. "Quotation errors in general science journals." Proceedings of the Royal Society A, 476(2242):20200538, 2020. — 25% total quotation error rate in high-impact general science journals; argues explicitly that persistent error rates "more than justify the one-time cost" of adopting stricter in-text citation policies — i.e. a fixed policy cost against a recurring error cost, which is the floor argument.
    7. "Detecting and Correcting Reference Hallucinations in Commercial LLMs and Deep Research Agents." arXiv:2604.03173. — Contemporary evaluation showing reference hallucination persists in agentic/deep-research pipelines, not only in raw chat completions; relevant because the C2A2 pipeline is of that class.
    8. Statistical rule of three / ASTM E2334-09(2013), "Standard Practice for Setting an Upper Confidence Bound for a Fraction or Number of Non-Conforming Items ... When There Is a Zero Response in the Sample." — Formal basis for what a verification floor buys: with zero errors in n checks the one-sided 95% upper bound on the error rate is ~3/n. At n=0 the bound is 1.0 — a zero-verification run places no bound at all on its own citation error rate.

  Strength of support: Strong (for the need for a floor); Weak (for the specific empirical claim that imposing a floor reduces downstream error)

  Summary: The base-rate half of the claim is strongly supported and well quantified. Fabrication rates for LLM-generated citations run 15%–55% depending on model and domain, with a further 24%–43% of the non-fabricated ones carrying substantive errors; human-authored quotation error rates of 15%–25% mean the floor cannot be justified only by AI involvement. The 2026 residual-error paper is the strongest explicit endorsement of verification as a precondition rather than a discretionary activity. The floor's formal value is given by the rule of three: n checks with zero errors bound the true error rate at about 3/n, so a run at 0/44 has bounded nothing, and a run at 4/40 bounds it only very loosely. What I could NOT find is a controlled demonstration that instituting a verification-rate floor reduces downstream error — the closest is the Proc R Soc A argument that a one-time policy cost is justified by a persistent 25% error rate, which is an argument rather than a measurement. The industrial-QC literature (acceptance sampling vs 100% inspection) supports risk-tiered floors, and specifically supports 100% inspection for critical characteristics with zero tolerance — which maps onto the pipeline's own statement that "a fabrication among these three would invalidate a premise, not merely a caveat."

  Caveats: (a) Fabrication base rates vary by an order of magnitude across topic and model generation (6% for heavily-studied topics up to 60% in some psychology subfields), so a single universal floor is not derivable from this literature; the floor is defensible, its numeric value is not. (b) Several rates cited in secondary summaries (the 32.3% psychology figure, the 47%-fabricated medical figure) could not be traced to a primary source I saw directly, and are excluded from the source list. (c) The acceptance-sampling literature returned trade and consultancy material, not peer-reviewed work; the 100%-inspection-for-critical-items principle is standard but the sources I saw for it are not primary research, and I have not cited them as such. (d) The rule-of-three bound assumes the verified subset is a random sample of the citation set; if verification preferentially targets the easy-to-check, the bound is optimistic — which matters because a budget-constrained verifier plausibly checks the cheapest citations first.

  Search scope: Comprehensive on citation fabrication base rates in LLM output and on human quotation error rates; comprehensive on the statistical basis for zero-response bounds. Preliminary on "do verification floors reduce downstream error" — I found no interventional study, and a broader search into editorial-policy evaluation and reference-checking trials is recommended before treating that half as established.
