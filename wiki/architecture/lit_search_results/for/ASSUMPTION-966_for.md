  Sources:
    1. Little, J.D.C., 1961. "A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = λW." Operations Research 9(3):383-387; with standard single-server queueing results (Kleinrock, L., 1975. Queueing Systems, Vol. 1). — This is the arithmetic the assumption claims. A single review stage with arrival rate λ and service rate μ has a stable queue only if λ < μ; at λ ≥ μ the queue and the wait grow without bound. A universal validation gate makes the human the single server for the output of all 33 producers, so λ scales with the fleet and μ does not. The incompatibility is therefore genuinely arithmetic and not a matter of effort: no increase in reviewer diligence changes μ enough to track a λ that scales with agent count. [canonical; cited from domain knowledge]
    2. Goldratt, E.M., 1984. The Goal (Theory of Constraints); with the ToC/Little's Law material surfaced this session (ASQ, "Applying Little's Law and the Theory of Constraints"; Learn Lean Sigma, "Bottlenecks"). — "A bottleneck is the slowest step that caps overall throughput and inflates lead time and WIP," and system capacity equals bottleneck capacity regardless of upstream capacity. Directly implies that 33 agents behind a one-human gate is a 1-capacity system with 33 units of WIP accumulation, which is the operational form of the arithmetic claim. [Goldratt canonical; the practitioner summaries unverified — from search snippets]
    3. Graicunas, V.A., 1933. "Relationship in Organization." — The classical span-of-control result: the number of relationships a supervisor must track grows combinatorially, not linearly, with the number of subordinates. Supports a stronger version of the assumption than pure queueing does — the human's per-item cost may *rise* with fleet size rather than staying constant, because cross-agent context must be held to validate any one agent's output. If so, μ falls as λ rises and the incompatibility arrives sooner than a fixed-μ model predicts. [canonical; cited from domain knowledge, not re-verified this session]
    4. Skitka, L.J., Mosier, K.L. & Burdick, M., 1999. "Does automation bias decision-making?" International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 51(5); with Cummings, M.L., 2004. "Automation Bias in Intelligent Time Critical Decision Support Systems." AIAA. — Establishes the *mechanism by which the gate fails silently rather than visibly*, which is the practically important half. Under load, human reviewers do not slow down and hold the line; they defer to the automated output (commission and omission errors both rise). So the arithmetic does not manifest as a growing visible queue — it manifests as approvals that are not reviews, i.e. the gate reports 100% coverage while providing much less. [canonical; cited from domain knowledge]

  Strength of support: Strong for the arithmetic and for the failure mode; Weak for any specific threshold

  Summary: The assumption is well supported in its strong form. Queueing theory supplies the actual arithmetic: a universal validation gate makes one human the single server for a fleet whose arrival rate scales with agent count, and above λ = μ the queue diverges — this is a mathematical result, not a workload complaint, which vindicates 14a's insistence on "arithmetically" rather than "in tension." Theory of Constraints gives the same conclusion in operational form (system capacity equals bottleneck capacity, so 33 producers behind one reviewer is a 1-capacity system accumulating 33 units of WIP). Graicunas suggests the situation is worse than fixed-μ queueing implies, since per-item review cost plausibly rises with fleet size as cross-agent context must be held. The automation-bias literature supplies the practically decisive point: the gate does not fail by visibly falling behind, it fails by continuing to emit approvals that are no longer reviews — so a system in this state will report full validation coverage while providing little, and the arithmetic contradiction will be invisible in the metrics. The practitioner literature is unanimous and blunt, and every remedy it proposes works by abandoning universality, which is itself a concession that the two register items cannot both hold.

  Caveats: (a) No literature can supply the threshold. Queueing theory tells you the gate fails when λ ≥ μ; it cannot tell you whether 33 agents exceeds a given human's μ, because that depends on per-item review time, agent output rate, and how much output actually requires review — none of which this search establishes for C2A2. The assumption is therefore supported as a *form* of argument while its specific instantiation at n=33 remains unquantified, and I would resist letting it be recorded as "proved at 33." (b) The incompatibility is contingent on both items being read at full strength. If ASSUMPTION-017's "everything" means "every category of output at least once" rather than "every artefact," or if much agent output is intermediate rather than deliverable, λ falls sharply and the contradiction may dissolve. The arithmetic is only as strong as the literal reading of 017, and 14a should confirm that reading is the intended one. (c) Most of the multi-agent-specific material is grey literature with commercial interests in exception-based review products. (d) Publication bias in a specific direction here: the HITL-scaling literature is being written largely by people selling autonomy, so its enthusiasm for the conclusion "universal review does not scale" should be discounted somewhat — though the queueing result stands independently of it.

  Search scope: Comprehensive on queueing/ToC capacity arithmetic and on automation bias. Moderate on span-of-control and on oversight-policy critique. Comprehensive on contemporary HITL-scaling practitioner discourse. No search performed for empirical measurements of review-gate capacity in agent fleets specifically — I doubt such measurements exist publicly, and if 15d wants the threshold quantified it will have to come from C2A2's own instrumentation, not from literature.

  Recommendation: SUPPORTED (the arithmetic form of the incompatibility is well grounded, and the automation-bias literature adds that it fails silently rather than visibly) — with the explicit qualification that the n=33 threshold is not and cannot be established from literature
