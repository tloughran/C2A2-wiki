  Sources:
    1. IRB / "publicly available data" exemption practice (researchbasics, UConn; common IRB position). — Many IRBs hold that studying publicly available online content is not human-subjects research requiring consent; under this reading, notice/transparency is a proportionate response. Supports notice-as-adequate for public data.
    2. Notice-and-choice / notice-based privacy regimes. — A long-standing regulatory tradition treats clear disclosure (notice) as a primary safeguard; supports the idea that transparency is a recognized mitigation.
    3. AoIR staged-consent guidance. — Recommends reserving consent to the dissemination/quotation stage rather than blanket up-front consent; supports a graduated model in which disclosure carries part of the ethical load for public material.

  Strength of support: Weak-Moderate

  Summary: There is partial support that disclosure/notice is a recognized and sometimes proportionate safeguard for public-web material — IRB public-data exemptions, notice-based privacy regimes, and AoIR's staged model all give transparency real ethical work to do. For low-sensitivity public data, notice can be a defensible response. The support weakens sharply as identifiability and group-level harm rise.

  Caveats: All three sources frame disclosure as a PROPORTIONATE safeguard for low-sensitivity public data, not as a general discharge of consent for identifiable groups. None supports the strong claim that disclosure makes opt-in or don't-listing unnecessary; the very guidance cited (AoIR) escalates protection when content is identifiable. So the FOR case supports "notice can be part of the response," not "notice cures the consent gap" — and crucially does not address the suppressed alternatives (don't-list / opt-in), which is the core of the presumption.

  Recommendation: PARTIALLY-SUPPORTED
