  Summary: The literature supports the parts of the presumption. The SRE Workbook says, in text read directly, that too many alerts distract operators and mask more severe ones. It says a written remediation procedure is effectively pseudocode that should be automated, so a human is not needed to run it. Incident-management practice treats repeated notifications of one condition as something to deduplicate into a single, prioritised item, and clinical informatics documents habitual override under high alert volume. Read together, these predict the failure the item describes: many uncoordinated reports of one known fault, each waiting on one absent human, with the fix already named. No source found names "distributed diagnosis, centralised human remediation" as an anti-pattern in those words, and none sets a volume threshold. About 8 notes a day to one person is low by SRE paging standards. The harm here comes more from duplication and the absence of a delegated path than from volume as such.

  Caveats:
    - The same SRE chapter warns that automation with admin-level powers can cause outages. It recommends defensive safeguards and starting with human-backed interfaces before self-service. That supports a staged or guarded remediation path, not unrestricted agent action. C2A2's task-file prohibition on schedule changes may be a deliberate safety boundary of the kind this literature endorses.
    - The Google Cloud toil post notes that rarely run tasks are hard to automate safely. A one-time schedule fix (OPEN-274) may fall in that category, which weakens the case for automation and strengthens the case for consolidation.
    - Alert-fatigue evidence is from on-call engineering and clinical settings with far higher volumes. Transfer to a single-owner agent pipeline is analogous only.
    - Most incident-management sources are vendors with a commercial interest in dedup tooling.
    - Bainbridge (1983) and the levels-of-automation literature were not reached. They likely bear on reserving the final decision for the human.
