    1. Norton, J.D. (2003). "A Material Theory of Induction." Philosophy of Science 70(4):647-670. (Expanded as The Material Theory of Induction, BSPS Open / University of Calgary Press, 2021.) — Argues that there are no universal inductive inference schemas: every inductive inference is licensed by contingent material facts holding in a particular domain, so all induction is local. The direct implication for the presumption is its central point stated as a positive doctrine: "generalise from the instances seen so far" is not itself a warrant, because the schema carries no licence of its own. What would license the fifth abstraction as general is a background fact about the domain — an enumeration of the kinds of page, or a structural fact entailing that every page has controls — and the pipeline's own record notes that no such enumeration was given. Norton also supplies the constructive test the presumption implies: assess the inductive risk by investigating the warrant for the underwriting fact, not by counting confirming instances.
    2. Lakatos, I. (1976). Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery. Cambridge University Press (eds. Worrall & Zahar). — The dialogue traces the successive repair of Euler's polyhedron formula and of the definition of "polyhedron" itself under a stream of counterexamples (the picture-frame, the cylinder, the hedgehog), each repair adequate to every instance then known and each superseded by the next monster. The book's stated thesis is that definitions are not carved in stone but are patched in the light of later insight, and it names "monster-barring" — redefining the term so that the counterexample ceases to be an instance — as the standard way a superseded generalisation is made to look as though it had been right all along. This is the closest available structural analogue to the presumption's target: a fifth definition of "the general case", written in the past perfect, over an unenumerated domain of future instances.
    3. Laudan, L. (1981). "A Confutation of Convergent Realism." Philosophy of Science 48(1):19-49. — The canonical statement of the pessimistic meta-induction: where a long succession of empirically successful theories was subsequently found false, the same track record undercuts confidence that the current successful theory is approximately true, because it was arrived at by the same methods. The argument form is exactly the presumption's: a base rate of four superseded general cases, all produced by the procedure that produced the fifth, is evidence about the fifth. The presumption's specific asymmetry — the moral applied to the superseded abstraction but not to its replacement — is what Laudan's argument is designed to block.
    4. Hájek, A. (2007). "The reference class problem is your problem too." Synthese 156(3):563-585 (DOI 10.1007/s11229-006-9138-5). — Shows that the reference class problem is not confined to frequentism: any assignment of a probability (or, by extension, of a coverage claim) to a case depends on how the case is classified, and every major interpretation of probability inherits its own version of the difficulty. Applied here, "the general case" and "its worst-covered instance" have no determinate content until the class over which generality is claimed is specified. Three page architectures out of an unenumerated set of future tabs is not a reference class, so the claim that the fifth abstraction is general is not yet in a form that can be true or false — which is a stronger version of the presumption's complaint than the presumption itself makes.
    5. Card, A.J. (2017). "The problem with '5 whys'." BMJ Quality & Safety 26(8):671-677. — A published critique of the most widely taught root-cause stopping rule, in a peer-reviewed safety journal. Card's objections are that 5-whys forces a single analytical pathway, insists on a single root cause as the target for solutions, and assumes without evidence that the most distal link on the causal pathway — the fifth "why" — is inherently the most effective place to intervene; he notes the technique's popularity is not grounded in effectiveness evidence. This is direct external support for the presumption's core claim that a fifth-iteration diagnosis is not thereby terminal, and it supplies the corresponding remedy (branching tools that hold several candidate causes open rather than converging on one).

The philosophy-of-induction literature supports the presumption's negative claim directly: on Norton's material theory there is no schema-level warrant for "generalise from the instances seen so far", so a fifth abstraction has exactly the standing its underwriting domain fact gives it, and no more — counting instances does not confer terminality. Lakatos supplies the structural precedent in the case most favourable to terminal generality, mathematics: a sequence of definitions each general over every instance then known, each superseded, with monster-barring as the recognised device by which the past-perfect reading ("was always") gets installed retrospectively. Laudan makes the self-application explicit — a track record of superseded generalisations produced by a method is evidence about the next output of that method — which is precisely the asymmetry 14b identified, the moral applied to the superseded abstraction but not to its replacement. Hájek sharpens the diagnosis: without a stated reference class, "the general case" and "its worst-covered instance" are underspecified rather than merely unproven, so the in-house test is to enumerate the page kinds first. Card 2017 shows the same stopping-rule problem is already documented in applied safety practice, where the assumption that the fifth iteration is the right terminus is criticised as unevidenced.

  Caveats: (a) Norton's theory cuts both ways and this is the most important limitation of the support. On a material theory, a well-chosen background fact can warrant a very strong induction from very few instances — so if C2A2 can state the fact that underwrites "every page has controls" (e.g. a framework-level invariant of the page architecture), n=3 would be ample and the fifth abstraction could be terminal in the only sense available. The literature supports "the induction is unwarranted as stated", not "the conclusion is wrong". (b) Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction is itself contested in a substantial reply literature (its base rate, its treatment of theory parts, and whether it commits a fallacy of ignoring changes in method are all live objections); a critic could reasonably argue the fifth diagnosis was reached by a *better* procedure than the first four, in which case the base rate does not transfer. The presumption's own wording — "the same procedure" — is the premise that would have to be defended. (c) Lakatos and Hájek are philosophical rather than empirical; the transfer to a codebase abstraction is by analogy, though the analogy is close because the object in both cases is a definition being revised under counterexample. (d) Card 2017 addresses healthcare incident analysis, and its "single root cause" objection is about causal analysis rather than about abstraction design; the shared element is only the unjustified stopping rule. (e) None of these sources addresses the specific claim that terminality *can* be established. Two adjacent literatures were reached for and did not yield a usable positive criterion — eliminative induction in the Baconian/Millian tradition requires an antecedently enumerated space of hypotheses, which is the thing missing here, and statistical learning theory's sample-complexity results require a stated hypothesis class and distribution, likewise missing. That absence is itself informative: the literature's consistent answer is that terminality is purchased by enumerating the domain, not by accumulating instances.
