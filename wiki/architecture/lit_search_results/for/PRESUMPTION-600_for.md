  Supporting evidence found: Yes

  Sources:
    1. Berkson, J. (1946). "Limitations of the Application of Fourfold Table Analysis to Hospital Data." Biometrics Bulletin 2(3):47-53. — The canonical result and the closest structural match. Berkson showed that conditioning on admission — where admission depends on more than one characteristic — induces association between characteristics that are independent in the source population, and that rates computed within the admitted group therefore do not estimate rates in the population. The pre-check is an admission filter whose criterion (already governed by an ACTIVE premise) is plausibly correlated with the very property that predicts INCORPORATE (being a well-formed, register-adjacent claim). Under Berkson the residual cohort's INCORPORATE rate is not an unbiased estimate of anything, and comparing it across filtered and unfiltered cohorts without accounting for the filter is exactly the error 14b names.
    2. Heckman, J.J. (1979). "Sample Selection Bias as a Specification Error." Econometrica 47(1):153-161. — Supplies the general statement and the correction. Heckman's result is that non-random selection into an observed sample is formally an omitted-variable problem, so any statistic computed on the selected sample carries a bias term determined by the selection rule; and — the part that supports 14b's remedy — the bias is estimable if the selection rule is modelled. This is direct support for the claim that the 07-30 figure cannot be "explained entirely downstream": the selection term is present whether or not it is written down.
    3. Altman, D.G. & Bland, J.M. (1994). "Diagnostic tests 2: Predictive values." BMJ 309(6947):102. — Support for the specific mechanism of yield depression. Positive predictive value is a function of prevalence, not only of the test; removing from the pool the items most likely to be positive lowers prevalence in the residual, which lowers the yield of any downstream stage of fixed sensitivity and specificity. If already-governed items are disproportionately the ones that would have converted, the residual yield falls for reasons entirely upstream of the downstream explanation offered.
    4. Sequential-filtering recall literature in e-discovery and technology-assisted review (Grossman & Cormack tradition; see also "The eDiscovery Medicine Show", arXiv:2109.13908). — Direct support for the compounding claim: when retrieval or screening methods are applied in sequence, end-to-end recall is the product of the stages' individual recalls, so overall recall is strictly worse than the weakest stage. Adding a pre-check to an existing pipeline therefore cannot leave end-to-end yield unchanged unless the pre-check has recall 1.0 on the target class — an assumption the pre-check's own stated limit (it reads premise statements, partly by term match) explicitly denies.
    5. Systematic-review screening error-rate literature: Wang, Z. et al. (2020). "Error rates of human reviewers during abstract screening in systematic reviews." PLOS ONE / PMC6959565. — Empirical support that screening stages have measurable and non-trivial error rates (reported total error rate 10.76% at abstract screening, combining false inclusion and false exclusion). Applied to the pre-check, a false-exclusion component means governed-candidate labelling removes some items that were not in fact governed, and the residual cohort's yield is depressed by however many of those would have converted.
    6. Admission-screening yield literature: systematic screening on admission for SARS-CoV-2 (PubMed 33640031). — An applied precedent for 14b's methodological demand rather than for the direction of the effect: the study's contribution is that it *measured* the yield of a screening step (0.1%) and used the measurement to question the cost-benefit ratio, rather than asserting the step's value from its rationale. This is precisely 14b's point — the pre-check's redundancy saving is currently unpriced, and the comparison it requires is a measurement, not an argument.
    7. Precision/recall trade-off in triage and pre-screening (model-evaluation and clinical-trial pre-screening literature, e.g. Improving Patient Pre-screening for Clinical Trials, arXiv:2304.07396). — Corroborates the trade-off form: a conservative high-precision filter yields links very likely correct but with recall low enough to limit standalone utility. Any filter tuned for confident exclusion buys its confidence in recall, and the recall loss lands on the residual cohort.

  Strength of support: Moderate-Strong

  Summary: Supported, and EXTERNALLY ANCHORED (named external referents: Berkson 1946 in Biometrics Bulletin; Heckman 1979 in Econometrica; Altman & Bland 1994 in BMJ; Wang et al. 2020 in PLOS ONE; sequential-filtering recall results in the e-discovery/TAR literature). Four independent literatures — epidemiological selection bias, econometric sample selection, diagnostic-test predictive value, and sequential screening in information retrieval — agree on the structural claim 14b makes: a non-random admission filter changes the composition of the residual population, so a yield statistic computed on the residual is not comparable to one computed on an unfiltered cohort, and any explanation of the difference that mentions only downstream causes is incomplete by construction. Berkson supplies the mechanism, Heckman the general form and the correction, Altman & Bland the specific reason yield falls (prevalence drives predictive value), and the sequential-filtering result the reason it cannot be assumed neutral (composed recall is a product, and the pre-check's own stated limit denies it recall 1.0). The applied screening literature supplies the methodological standard 14b is invoking: yield of a screening step is measured, not argued.

  Caveats: (a) The support is for the DIRECTION of a bias term, not its SIZE. Nothing found bounds how much a governed-item filter depresses INCORPORATE yield, and the effect could be negligible. 14b's own cohorts are 8 items and 4 items; at those sizes the observed 1-of-8 versus 0-of-4 difference is entirely within sampling noise — one INCORPORATE either way flips the comparison. The literature supports the claim that the comparison is confounded; it does not support any claim that the confound is doing work here, and this file must not be read as evidence that the pre-check is harming yield. (b) There is a genuine disanalogy that weakens the transfer and should be stated: in systematic review, deduplication — removing records already represented — is standard practice and is not held to depress yield, because a true duplicate carries no new information. If the pre-check were removing true duplicates of governed premises, the selection-bias literature would not apply. The whole question is whether "governed by an ACTIVE premise" is duplication or is a correlated-but-distinct property, and the pre-check's own tagging convention (GOVERNED-CANDIDATE, not GOVERNED) concedes it is the latter. The support is contingent on that concession. (c) Domain transfer: Berkson and Heckman concern estimation of a population parameter from a selected sample. C2A2's INCORPORATE rate is not obviously an estimate of a population parameter — it may be better read as a throughput statistic, in which case "bias" is the wrong frame and the right question is opportunity cost. This reframing does not defeat 14b's point but changes what would settle it. (d) Search scope: three targeted searches across screening, selection bias and triage. Two literatures reached for and not exhausted: work-in-progress limits and admission control in queueing/manufacturing (which would speak to the throughput framing directly), and the empirical literature on redundancy filtering in systematic review measuring what is lost to it. Moderate search scope; the queueing-theoretic framing is the more promising gap.
