  Sources:
    1. Gartlehner, G., et al., 2020. "Single-reviewer abstract screening missed 13 percent of relevant studies: a crowd-based, randomized controlled trial." Journal of Clinical Epidemiology 121. — Establishes abstract-level screening as a validated, quantified triage instrument: single-reviewer sensitivity 86.6% (95% CI 80.6–91.2%), specificity high. Supports abstract/snippet screening as a legitimate first-pass method with known error bounds.
    2. Meta-research on one vs. two reviewers for title/abstract screening (J. Clinical Epidemiology, 2026, "Evaluating the accuracy of screening titles and abstracts... part 1 of a meta-research study of rapid review methodological trade-offs"). — Reports single-reviewer title/abstract sensitivity of 48.8–66.3% but specificity 88.0–93.3%; explicitly frames abstract-only screening as an accepted rapid-review tradeoff whose error profile is characterized.
    3. LLM-assisted screening studies (e.g., "Enhancing title and abstract screening for systematic reviews with GPT-3.5 turbo," BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine/PMC10850650, 2024; medRxiv 2025 dual-model ensemble reporting near-perfect sensitivity). — Recent evidence that abstract-level (full-text-free) relevance judgments can reach high sensitivity, supporting the feasibility of snippet-scale screening pipelines.
    4. Rapid review methodology literature (Tricco et al. lineage; WHO rapid review guidance). — Codifies abbreviated screening (title/abstract only, single reviewer) as an accepted methodology when time or access constraints bind, provided limitations are declared.

  Strength of support: Weak

  Summary: The systematic-review literature gives genuine but bounded support. Screening on titles/abstracts — i.e., snippet-level evidence — is a standard, validated stage of evidence synthesis, and its error rates are well characterized; rapid-review methodology explicitly sanctions it under access constraints, exactly the situation described (primary source unfetchable). High specificity findings support one direction of inference: when a snippet shows a source IS relevant/prior, that positive identification is fairly reliable. But the presumption uses snippets for the opposite inference — declaring novelty, i.e., the absence of prior treatment — which corresponds to the weak side of the instrument: single-pass abstract screening misses 13–51% of relevant material, so "nothing found at snippet level" only weakly establishes novelty. The method is supported as triage; it is not supported as a novelty-establishing endpoint.

  Caveats: Support weakens when (a) the novelty claim carries downstream weight (misses of 13%+ are expected); (b) snippets are search-engine extracts rather than author-written abstracts (no validation literature covers engine snippets, which are shorter and query-biased); (c) only one screener/pass is used; (d) the unfetchable source is the single best-matching document — precisely the case where full-text confirmation matters most.

  Recommendation: PARTIALLY-SUPPORTED
