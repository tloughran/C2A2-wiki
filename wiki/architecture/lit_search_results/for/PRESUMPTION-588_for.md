  Sources:
    1. Molenberghs, G., Beunckens, C., Sotto, C. & Kenward, M.G. (2008). "Every missingness not at random model has a missingness at random counterpart with equal fit." Journal of the Royal Statistical Society: Series B (Statistical Methodology) 70(2):371-388. — The decisive theoretical result for the narrowed question. The authors prove that for any MNAR model fitted to incomplete data, altering the conditional distribution of the *unobserved* outcomes to comply with MAR yields a new model with exactly the same fit to the observed data — from which it follows that MAR versus MNAR cannot be definitively tested from the observed data. Transposed: the day's output cannot, in principle, distinguish "the missing Chat context would have changed nothing" from "it would have changed a great deal", because both hypotheses fit the observed output equally well. This is the strongest possible support for 14b's "the cost is entered as zero because nothing measures it" — the observed record is not merely silent on the cost, it is provably incapable of settling it. It also licenses 14b's chosen remedy by elimination: if the observed data cannot identify the effect, an external comparison or an explicit assumption is the only route.
    2. Groves, R.M. & Peytcheva, E. (2008). "The Impact of Nonresponse Rates on Nonresponse Bias: A Meta-Analysis." Public Opinion Quarterly 72(2):167-189. — A meta-analysis of 59 methodological studies designed to estimate the magnitude of nonresponse bias, drawing on rich sampling frames, matched administrative records, screening-interview data on nonrespondents, and nonrespondent follow-up. The headline finding is that the response rate is *not* generally predictive of nonresponse bias — there is little correlation between how much is missing and how much the estimate is distorted. This supports the presumption in a way that cuts against a naive reading of it: seven consecutive dark syncs is a *rate*, and by Groves & Peytcheva the rate carries little information about the bias. The methodological lesson is exactly 14b's: the magnitude of the missingness cannot substitute for a measurement of its effect, and the study designs that *do* estimate the effect all work by obtaining auxiliary information about the missing units — which is the matched comparison 14b specifies.
    3. Johnson, G.A., Lewis, R.A. & Nubbemeyer, E.I. (2017). "Ghost Ads: Improving the Economics of Measuring Online Ad Effectiveness." Journal of Marketing Research 54(6):867-884. (2017 Paul E. Green Award.) — The applied methodological template for 14b's settling quantity. The problem the paper solves is how to measure the incremental effect of an input (an ad) on units that would have received it, when the counterfactual is unobservable and the naive comparison is confounded by who gets exposed. Ghost ads construct the control counterparts of exposed units by logging the auction outcomes that *would* have served an ad without serving one, delivering a matched comparison at substantially reduced cost and improved precision relative to public-service-announcement and intent-to-treat designs. This is direct support for 14b's proposed method — run one comparable workload with Chat context and one without — and it supplies the design refinement that matters: the comparison must be constructed so that the two arms are matched on what *would* have been attended to, not merely on nominal workload.
    4. Howard, R.A. (1966). "Information Value Theory." IEEE Transactions on Systems Science and Cybernetics 2(1):22-26. — The canonical framework for pricing an input in the absence of the input. Howard's contribution is to value information jointly on its probabilistic and *economic* consequences, showing that a numerical value can be assigned to the elimination or reduction of any uncertainty, and introducing the expected value of clairvoyance (now expected value of perfect information) as the upper bound one would rationally pay for it. Applied here, this is the one method found that can produce a *bound* on the cost of the missing Chat context without waiting for the channel to be restored: an upper bound on the loss is the value of the decisions the context could have redirected. It supports the presumption's structural claim — that "the file is the deliverable" prices only the outbound leg — by supplying the missing accounting concept, and it does so in a way that is computable from stated assumptions rather than requiring the matched run.
    5. VanderWeele, T.J. & Ding, P. (2017). "Sensitivity Analysis in Observational Research: Introducing the E-Value." Annals of Internal Medicine 167(4):268-274 (DOI 10.7326/M16-2607). — Supplies the second-best method when the matched comparison is unavailable: bound the unmeasured factor by asking how strong it would have to be to matter. The E-value is the minimum strength of association an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both exposure and outcome to fully explain away an observed association, conditional on measured covariates. The transposed question is well formed and answerable in-house: how much would the missing Chat context have had to shift the agents' attention allocation to change any of the day's dispositions? This is direct support for 14b's implicit claim that the honest entry is *not* zero — a sensitivity bound is available, is standard practice in a field where unmeasured inputs are the central methodological problem, and is cheaper than the matched run.
    6. Sculley, D., Holt, G., Golovin, D., Davydov, E., Phillips, T., Ebner, D., Chaudhary, V., Young, M., Crespo, J.-F. & Dennison, D. (2015). "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems." Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 28 (NIPS 2015):2503-2511. — The engineering-side support: the paper's whole thesis is that ML systems accrue debt in forms that do not surface as errors, and its enumerated risk factors include data dependencies, undeclared consumers, changes in the external world, and system-level anti-patterns. Its treatment of underutilised and unstable data dependencies makes precisely 14b's point in production terms — an input that silently stops arriving degrades behaviour without raising a failure, because nothing in the pipeline asserts that the input is present or that it mattered. The paper's recommended countermeasures (explicit dependency declaration and automated data-dependency analysis) are the design analogue of 14b's demand: the pipeline should assert the presence of Chat context as a declared dependency so that its absence is an event rather than a silence.

SSC; VanderWeele & Ding 2017 in Annals of Internal Medicine; Sculley et al. 2015 in NIPS 28). The methodological literature converges on 14b's position and adds three things the presumption does not contain. First, the impossibility result: Molenberghs et al. prove that the observed record cannot in principle distinguish a costly missing input from a costless one, so recording the cost as zero is not a conservative default but an unidentified assumption presented as a finding. Second, the rate is not the bias: Groves & Peytcheva's meta-analysis of 59 studies finds response rate largely unpredictive of nonresponse bias, which means "seven consecutive dark syncs" is not itself evidence of magnitude in either direction — this disciplines the presumption as much as it supports it. Third, three concrete methods exist, ordered by cost. The matched comparison 14b names is the gold standard and has a refined published template in ghost ads, which shows the two arms must be matched on what *would* have been attended to. Failing that, Howard 1966 gives an upper bound via expected value of clairvoyance, and VanderWeele & Ding 2017 gives a sensitivity bound answerable from the existing record. Sculley et al. 2015 supplies the preventive measure: declare the Chat context as an explicit data dependency so that its absence registers as an event rather than a silence.

  Caveats: (a) The support is for the *method*, per the narrowing, and says nothing about the magnitude. Every source is consistent with the true cost of seven dark syncs being negligible. Groves & Peytcheva in particular should be read as a warning against inferring a large cost from a long outage; the presumption's force is entirely that the entry is unjustified, not that it is wrong. PREMISE-124's UNCALIBRATED remains the correct entry and this search does not displace it. (b) Domain transfer is the main limitation and it is uneven across sources. Molenberghs et al. and Groves & Peytcheva concern statistical estimands with a defined target quantity and sampling frame; "items attended to by an agent on a day's workload" has neither, so the identification result transfers as a conceptual argument rather than as a theorem about this case. Howard's EVPI requires a specified decision problem with stated priors and payoffs, which the pipeline does not have in that form; producing one would itself be a modelling exercise with contestable inputs. The E-value transfers only if a baseline association can be stated, which here it cannot without first defining the outcome. Ghost ads and Sculley et al. transfer most cleanly because both concern engineered pipelines with logged units. (c) The matched comparison 14b specifies has a confound the literature flags and the presumption does not: the two arms cannot be run simultaneously, so day-to-day variation in workload difficulty is fully confounded with the treatment at n=1 per arm. The ghost-ads design exists precisely because the naive two-arm comparison is untrustworthy. A single with/without pair would be weak evidence; several matched pairs, or a within-day randomisation over agents, would be needed for the comparison to bear the weight 14b assigns it. This is a strengthening of the presumption's remedy, but it also raises its cost above what the presumption implies. (d) Publication bias: Sculley et al. is a position paper from an industrial lab describing its own practice, not an empirical study, and its claims about silent degradation are asserted from experience rather than measured. It is cited for the concept and the countermeasure, not for evidence of magnitude. The other five are peer-reviewed methodological work where the direction of publication incentive is toward reporting identifiable effects, which is the harder direction for a null-identification result like Molenberghs et al. — that paper's finding is a negative result published against incentive, which strengthens it. (e) Scope limit imposed by the narrowing: the epistemic half — whether an agent can bound its own omissions from inside — was not searched, per 14b's intake instruction assigning it to PREMISE-109. Readers should not treat this file as evidence about that question in either direction. (f) Search scope: six targeted verification searches, one per source. Two literatures were reached for and not exhausted: silent data corruption and silent failure detection in large-scale production systems (which would strengthen source 6 with measured rates rather than asserted ones), and ablation/permutation-importance methodology, which is the standard machine-learning method for pricing an input by removing it and is the closest formal analogue of the matched comparison. Either would add applied corroboration rather than change the direction. Moderate search scope.
