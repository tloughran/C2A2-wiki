    1. NEW-THIS-CYCLE — Klein, J. T. (2017). "Typologies of Interdisciplinarity: The Boundary Work of Definition," in Frodeman (ed.), The Oxford Handbook of Interdisciplinarity, 2nd edn, Oxford University Press. — The canonical survey of typologies of interdisciplinary integration. Establishes that the field repeatedly produces bounded typologies (multidisciplinary juxtaposition / interdisciplinary integration / transdisciplinary synthesis and their subdivisions), which is the closest existing analogue to a "finite typology of connecting memes." Klein's own framing, however, is that typologies of interdisciplinarity are *boundary work* — contested and purpose-relative — not a discovered finite set.

    2. NEW-THIS-CYCLE — Zhang et al. / Fernandes et al. (2022). "Boundary objects, knowledge integration, and innovation management: A systematic review of the literature," Technovation (ScienceDirect S0166497222001961). — A systematic review that does inductively classify boundary-object types used to bridge distinct knowledge communities. This is the strongest genuine precedent for a bounded, empirically derived classification of cross-community connectors. It does not claim exhaustiveness.

    3. NEW-THIS-CYCLE — Frontiers in Education (2026). "Low-threshold interdisciplinary encounters across boundary objects: early insights from an exploratory study," 10.3389/feduc.2026.1873958. — 2026 empirical work treating boundary objects as bridging artifacts with interpretive flexibility.   Summary: There is a substantial and active literature on classifying cross-disciplinary connectors — Klein's typologies of interdisciplinarity, the boundary-object systematic review, and conceptual-blending theory's four integration-network types are all bounded classification schemes over roughly the target phenomenon. That is real support for the weaker claim that *a* bounded typology is constructible. What remains absent, after 19 queries across two cycles, is any empirical demonstration that such a typology *stabilizes* — no convergence or saturation test has been run on cross-paradigm connecting mechanisms specifically. The qualitative-methods literature now supplies a ready-made convergence criterion (code saturation), so the change-condition is newly operationalizable, but unoperationalized. Klein's own position — that these typologies are contested boundary work rather than a discovered finite set — is a direct caution against reading the classification literature as establishing finiteness.

  Caveats: All supporting classification schemes are purpose-relative; none claims exhaustiveness, and Klein argues explicitly that exhaustiveness is the wrong ambition. Domain transfer is a live concern: boundary objects (organizational artifacts) and conceptual blends (cognitive structures) are not obviously the same kind of thing as C2A2's displacement phrasings (linguistic constructions in a wiki). Publication bias favours papers that successfully produce a typology over papers reporting that categories failed to stabilize.
