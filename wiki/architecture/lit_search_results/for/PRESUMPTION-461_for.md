    1. Waffenschmidt et al., 2019. "Single screening versus conventional double screening for study selection in systematic reviews: a methodological systematic review." BMC Medical Research Methodology. — The strongest available support: experienced single screeners missed a median of only 3% of relevant studies (vs. 13% for inexperienced), and in the experienced-reviewer screenings the misses had negligible impact on conclusions. Single-pass expert screening can be acceptable when the rater is genuinely expert.
    2. Gartlehner et al., 2020. "Single-reviewer abstract screening missed 13 percent of relevant studies: a crowd-based, randomized controlled trial." Journal of Clinical Epidemiology. — Quantifies the single-vs-dual gap (sensitivity 86.6% vs 97.5%); supports single screening only as an explicit sensitivity trade-off, which is a form of qualified support.
    3. Shea et al. (algorithmic single-review incorporation), 2021. "Successful incorporation of single reviewer assessments during systematic review screening." Systematic Reviews / PMC8020619. — Demonstrates validated hybrid designs where single-reviewer decisions are trusted for a calibrated subset (clear excludes) — precedent that single-pass labeling can be made reliable when scoped and validated.

  Strength of support: Weak

  Summary: There is qualified support: the methodological literature shows experienced single reviewers can screen with ~97% sensitivity and negligible conclusion impact, and validated single-reviewer designs are accepted in practice for calibrated decision subsets. This supports "a single specialist can do intake labeling acceptably" as a cost-conscious design. However, the support inverts for the specific label at issue: 'paradigm-shift candidate' is a rare, high-stakes, judgment-heavy category, and the same literature (a) shows single-reviewer miss rates concentrate exactly on ambiguous/nonstandard items, (b) notes chance-corrected agreement (kappa) is hardest to achieve on rare categories, and (c) Cochrane/JBI guidance mandates dual screening for consequential inclusion decisions. Rare-novelty detection is the worst case for one-pass single-rater reliability, since a missed candidate is silent and unrecoverable downstream.

  Caveats: Support weakens as (a) base rate of true candidates falls (misses dominate and are invisible); (b) the label triggers costly or irreversible downstream routing; (c) the rater lacks calibration data. Cheap literature-consistent mitigations: second opinion only on borderline/positive labels, or periodic audit sampling of negatives — both preserve most of the single-pass cost saving.

  Search scope confidence: Comprehensive for screening-reliability literature; no study found on 'paradigm-shift' or novelty-significance labeling per se.
