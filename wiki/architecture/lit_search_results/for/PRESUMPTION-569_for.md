  Sources:
    1. Macmillan, N.A. & Creelman, C.D. (2005). Detection Theory: A User's Guide, 2nd ed. Lawrence Erlbaum Associates (3rd ed. 2021, Hautus, Macmillan & Creelman, Routledge). — Signal detection theory decomposes observed performance into two independent indices: sensitivity (d', the ability to discriminate signal from noise) and response bias (the criterion, the willingness to say "yes"). The decisive point for this presumption: a rise in the hit rate alone is diagnostically ambiguous — a more liberal criterion raises hits *and* false alarms with d' unchanged, and the two cases are separated only by observing the false-alarm rate. A fivefold rise in INCORPORATE decisions is a rise in the "yes" rate. Without the corresponding rate of adoptions that should not have been made, it is formally indistinguishable from a criterion shift and cannot on its own be evidence that discrimination improved. This is the single strongest and most precisely fitting external support for the presumption.
    2. Campbell, D.T. (1979). "Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change." Evaluation and Program Planning 2(1):67-90; and Strathern, M. (1997). "'Improving Ratings': Audit in the British University System." European Review 5(3):305-321. — Campbell's law: "the more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor." Strathern's restatement of Goodhart: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Both are external, named, canonical support for the presumption's core inference: once a criterion is imposed and its satisfaction is itself reported as evidence of success, the observed rate becomes data about behaviour under the criterion rather than an unbiased measure of what the criterion was meant to track. The mechanism does not require bad faith; both authors describe it as a structural consequence of using the indicator for judgement.
    3. McCambridge, J., Witton, J. & Elbourne, D.R. (2014). "Systematic Review of the Hawthorne Effect: New Concepts Are Needed to Study Research Participation Effects." Journal of Clinical Epidemiology 67(3):267-277. — A systematic review of 19 purposively designed studies (8 RCTs, 5 quasi-experimental, 6 observational) of the effects of knowing one is being studied. It supports the presumption's "reactivity" premise in the honest form: research participation effects are real and consequential enough to warrant new concepts and further study, but effects varied so widely that no summary estimate could be calculated. This licenses the presumption's claim that awareness of being under judgement is a live alternative explanation that must be excluded; it does not license any claim about the size of the distortion.
    4. Moore, D.A., Tetlock, P.E., Tanlu, L. & Bazerman, M.H. (2006). "Conflicts of Interest and the Case of Auditor Independence: Moral Seduction and Strategic Issue Cycling." Academy of Management Review 31(1):10-29. — Moral seduction theory holds that professionals are typically *unaware* of how far conflicts of interest have compromised their judgement; bias operates without intent and survives good faith and professional norms. This addresses the presumption's most pointed feature — that the rate anomaly arose on the run in which the pipeline was itself under judgement for whether it can adopt premises about its own reliability. Where the assessor's interest is engaged by the outcome, the literature says the assessment is not rescued by the assessor's sincerity, and that self-review is the structurally suspect configuration. Directly supports the presumption's "self-serving application of admission standards" search target.
    5. King, M. & Bearman, P. (2009). "Diagnostic Change and the Increased Prevalence of Autism." International Journal of Epidemiology 38(5):1224-1234. — Retrospective case-record study of 7,003 California DDS patients: 26.4% of the increase in caseload 1992-2005 was uniquely attributable to diagnostic change (substitution and accretion), and the odds of acquiring the diagnosis were significantly elevated in years coinciding with changes to DSM criteria and state guidelines. This is the best-documented external instance of the presumption's specific pattern — a step change in a rate that is temporally aligned with the introduction or revision of a criterion, and a portion of which is decomposable into "how the criterion is applied" rather than "what the criterion detects." Crucially, the study also shows the decomposition can be *done*, and that the criterion-driven share was substantial but not total.

  Summary: Supported, and EXTERNALLY ANCHORED (Macmillan & Creelman 2005 on detection theory; Campbell 1979 in Evaluation and Program Planning; Strathern 1997 in European Review; McCambridge et al. 2014 in J Clin Epidemiol; Moore, Tetlock, Tanlu & Bazerman 2006 in AMR; King & Bearman 2009 in Int J Epidemiol). Signal detection theory gives the presumption a formal statement: a fivefold rise in the "yes" rate is jointly consistent with improved discrimination and with a relaxed criterion, and the two are separated only by the false-alarm rate — so the adoption count alone cannot be evidence that the bar works. Campbell's law and Strathern's Goodhart formulation establish that once a criterion is imposed and its satisfaction is reported back as evidence of its own success, the observed rate is contaminated by design rather than by misconduct. The Hawthorne review supports reactivity to being evaluated as a real and non-negligible phenomenon while explicitly declining to quantify it — which is the appropriately cautious form of the presumption's premise. Moore et al. supply the sharpest point: unawareness is the expected condition of a party assessing a matter in which its own standing is at stake, so the pipeline's sincerity about its explanation is not evidence about the explanation. King & Bearman then show the pattern empirically and, importantly, show it is decomposable — the fix the presumption implies (report the rejection rate and the reasons alongside the adoption rate) is a real and executed methodology, not an unattainable ideal.

  Caveats: (a) None of these sources shows the external-referent bar does *not* work; they show a single post-imposition rate cannot demonstrate that it does. The presumption is about the sufficiency of the offered evidence, and support should not be over-read as a finding against the bar. (b) The detection-theory argument (source 1) is only as good as its analogy: it requires that INCORPORATE decisions be treatable as yes/no responses against a ground truth about which premises *should* have been adopted. If no such ground truth is definable even in principle, d' and criterion are not separable and the framework supplies a vocabulary rather than a test. (c) n = 1 vs n = 5 is a very small sample; a fivefold ratio on these counts is not distinguishable from noise on any conventional test, which independently — and perhaps more simply than any mechanism in this file — undercuts reading it as evidence of anything. This arithmetic point should be checked in-house before the reactivity literature is invoked. (d) Campbell and Strathern describe indicators used for reward or sanction; whether an internal pipeline note reporting its own adoption count meets that condition is arguable, and the transfer weakens if nothing turns on the number. (e) McCambridge et al. found effect sizes too heterogeneous to summarise, so it supports existence and not magnitude. (f) King & Bearman is epidemiological and institutional; the mechanism transfers, the 26.4% figure carries no implication for this case. (g) Moderate search scope: six targeted searches plus two citation-verification searches. No search was run on LLM- or agent-specific self-evaluation reactivity, where the base rates may differ materially from the human literature; a follow-up search there is recommended.
