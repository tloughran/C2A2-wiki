  Original statement: Four identifiers were cited in support of claims that their own bodies argue against, and all four passed an existence check and a label check — i.e. "every such anchor would have looked correct at the Label." The implied assumption is that existence + label verification cannot detect citation polarity failure, which is only visible in the body text.

  Sources:
    1. Teufel, S., Siddharthan, A., & Tidhar, D., 2006. "Automatic Classification of Citation Function." Proceedings of EMNLP 2006 (companion scheme paper: "An Annotation Scheme for Citation Function," SIGdial 2006). — Establishes citation function as a distinct annotation/classification task with explicit contrast and weakness categories (a citation whose function is to dispute the cited work). The scheme is defined over the citing sentence and its surrounding argument, not over the reference entry, which is direct support for the claim that a correct-looking label carries no functional information.
    2. Athar, A., 2011/2014. "Sentiment Analysis of Scientific Citations." University of Cambridge Computer Laboratory Technical Report UCAM-CL-TR-856; with Athar & Teufel, 2012, "Context-Enhanced Citation Sentiment Detection," NAACL-HLT. — Built an ~8,700-sentence annotated citation corpus and found that negative/polarised citations are rare, are frequently hedged or implicit, and are systematically missed unless the citation context is widened beyond the citing sentence. This is the strongest available support for the specific failure mode in 1131: polarity is under-marked at the surface and only recoverable from body text.
    3. Cohan, A., Ammar, W., van Zuylen, M., & Cady, F., 2019. "Structural Scaffolds for Citation Intent Classification in Scientific Publications." NAACL-HLT 2019 (SciCite dataset). — Treats citation intent as a supervised problem requiring the citation context plus document structure; the model needs section-title and citation-worthiness scaffolds to reach state of the art. Confirms that intent/polarity is not derivable from the reference itself and is hard even with the body available.
    4. Wadden, D., Lin, S., Lo, K., Wang, L. L., van Zuylen, M., Cohan, A., & Hajishirzi, H., 2020. "Fact or Fiction: Verifying Scientific Claims." EMNLP 2020 (SciFact). — Formalises the SUPPORTS / REFUTES / NOINFO decision as requiring abstract-level rationale selection, not document identification. The task design itself encodes the assumption in 1131: identifying the right document is a separate and insufficient step relative to determining whether it supports or refutes the claim.
    5. Liu, N. F., Zhang, T., & Liang, P., 2023. "Evaluating Verifiability in Generative Search Engines." Findings of EMNLP 2023. — Measured four generative search engines and found only 51.5% of generated sentences fully supported by their citations and only 74.5% of citations actually supporting the sentence they were attached to. Quantifies the exact failure: the citation resolves to a real, on-topic source that does not support the sentence.
    6. Mogull, S. A., 2017. "Accuracy of cited 'facts' in medical research articles: A review of study methodology and recalculation of quotation error rate." PLOS ONE 12(9): e0184727. — Recalculated quotation error rate of 14.5% (95% CI 10.5–18.6%), of which 64.8% are major content errors, defined as cases where the referenced source "fails to substantiate, is unrelated to, or contradicts the assertion." A published, quantified base rate for polarity-inverted citation in human-authored literature.
    7. Jergas, H., & Baethge, C., 2015. "Quotation accuracy in medical journal articles — a systematic review and meta-analysis." PeerJ 3:e1364. — Independent systematic review and meta-analysis of quotation accuracy confirming that a substantial minority of citations misrepresent their source, and that detection requires manual comparison of the assertion against the source body.
    8. Magesh, V., Surani, F., Dahl, M., Suzgun, M., Manning, C. D., & Ho, D. E., 2025. "Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools." Journal of Empirical Legal Studies (arXiv:2405.20362, 2024). — Introduces the correctness/groundedness distinction and the "misgrounded" category: a citation to a real, existent, correctly-labelled authority that does not support (or has been overruled relative to) the proposition it is attached to. Explicitly argues this class "may be even more pernicious than the outright invention" of sources — i.e. existence checks are the wrong guard.

  Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: The assumption is very well supported and is essentially the founding premise of two established research literatures. Citation function/intent classification (Teufel et al. 2006; Cohan et al. 2019) and citation sentiment analysis (Athar 2011; Athar & Teufel 2012) both exist because the reference label is polarity-free and the function must be recovered from the citing context; Athar & Teufel further show negative polarity is the hardest case because it is hedged and often expressed outside the citing sentence itself. Independently, the quotation-error literature gives a measured base rate for the exact failure: Mogull (2017) finds 64.8% of content errors are major errors in which the source fails to substantiate, is unrelated to, or contradicts the assertion. Liu, Zhang & Liang (2023) show that 25.5% of citations emitted by generative search engines fail to support their own sentence, and Magesh et al. (2025) name the class directly ("misgrounded") and argue it is more dangerous than fabrication precisely because everything at the label level checks out. Four same-day failures passing existence and label checks is entirely consistent with these published rates.

  Caveats: (a) None of these sources is about identifier schemas of the type in use here; they are about scholarly citation and about generative-system citation, so the transfer is by analogy of structure rather than by direct replication. (b) The quotation-error base rates (Mogull; Jergas & Baethge) are for human-authored medical literature and may not transfer numerically. (c) Teufel's citation function is explicitly *not* sentiment — the survey literature notes a supporting citation can carry negative sentiment and vice versa — so "polarity" needs to be defined as argumentative function, not tone, before these results are applied. (d) Two additional promising hits ("Reading Between the Citations: A Typed Claim Network for Scientific Literature", arXiv:2605.30966; "Large Language Models for Citation Function Classification", arXiv:2607.17738) were returned by search but their author lists and venues were not confirmed and they are therefore excluded from the source list [unverified — title/venue not confirmed].
