  Sources:
    1. Knight, J.C., & Leveson, N.G. (1986). "An Experimental Evaluation of the Assumption of Independence in Multiversion Programming." IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering SE-12(1):96-109. — The landmark refutation of exactly this inference. N-version programming credits redundancy on the assumption that independently developed programs fail independently; Knight and Leveson had 27 versions written independently from a common specification at two universities and ran one million tests. The individual versions were highly reliable, but the number of tests in which more than one version failed was substantially greater than the independence assumption predicts. The separation in their experiment — different people, different institutions, different code — is strictly stronger than the separation between two instances of one model family, and it still failed to deliver error independence. This is the presumption's claim demonstrated experimentally: procedural separation of the checkers is not evidence of decorrelated errors, because correlation is induced by what the checkers share (here, the specification and the difficulty structure of the problem), not by what they were run as.
    2. Mosleh, A., Rasmuson, D.M., & Marshall, F.M. (1998). NUREG/CR-5485, "Guidelines on Modeling Common-Cause Failures in Probabilistic Risk Assessment." U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission, Washington, DC (November 1998). — A standards-body treatment of the same problem in safety-critical risk assessment. Redundancy may not be credited on an independence assumption; common-cause failures must be modelled explicitly, with components grouped into common-cause component groups on the basis of *coupling factors* — shared design, shared manufacturer, shared environment, shared maintenance procedure — and with parameters quantifying the residual correlated-failure probability. The relevant transfer is the classification rule: two items from the same design lineage are by definition in one common-cause component group regardless of how independently they are operated. On that rule a second instance of the same model family is a redundant unit within a group, not an independent channel, and the correlated term is the load-bearing quantity rather than a residual.
    3. Panickssery, A., Bowman, S.R., & Feng, S. (2024). "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations." Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 37 (NeurIPS 2024); arXiv:2404.13076. — Shows that LLM evaluators exhibit self-preference — scoring their own outputs higher than others' that human annotators rate as equal in quality — and links this to non-trivial self-recognition accuracy: GPT-4 and Llama 2 can distinguish their own generations from those of other models and of humans. The finding is at the level of the model, not the session, and it identifies a mechanism by which a same-model checker's judgement is systematically shifted towards the thing it is checking. This is direct evidence that model identity, not instance identity, is the variable carrying the bias.
    4. Xu, W., Zhu, G., Zhao, X., Pan, L., Li, L., & Wang, W. (2024). "Pride and Prejudice: LLM Amplifies Self-Bias in Self-Refinement." Proceedings of the 62nd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL 2024), 2024.acl-long.826; arXiv:2402.11436. — Measures self-bias across six models (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Gemini, LLaMA2, Mixtral, DeepSeek) on translation, constrained generation and mathematical reasoning, finding self-bias prevalent across all of them and across languages, and — the load-bearing result here — finding that the self-refine loop *amplifies* it while improving surface fluency, whereas external feedback with accurate assessment significantly reduces it. See also Huang, J., Chen, X., Mishra, S., Zheng, H.S., Yu, A., Song, X., & Zhou, D. (2024), "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet," ICLR 2024, which finds that intrinsic self-correction — revision based solely on the model's own capabilities, without external feedback — fails to improve reasoning. Together these say the operative distinction is internal versus external feedback, not one instance versus two, which is precisely the substitution the presumption identifies.
    5. Kuncheva, L.I., & Whitaker, C.J. (2003). "Measures of Diversity in Classifier Ensembles and Their Relationship with the Ensemble Accuracy." Machine Learning 51(2):181-207. — Surveys ten diversity statistics (Q, correlation, disagreement, double-fault, entropy, difficulty index, Kohavi-Wolpert variance, interrater agreement, generalized diversity, coincident failure diversity), notes that there is no generally accepted formal definition of ensemble diversity, and reports that the relationship between measured diversity and ensemble accuracy is weak enough to raise doubts about using diversity measures to build ensembles. The bearing on the presumption is on what "decorrelated" must mean to be load-bearing: it is a measurable property of the joint error distribution of the checkers, estimable only from paired outcomes on common items, and it does not follow from any structural fact about how the checkers were instantiated. See also Kleinberg, J., & Raghavan, M. (2021), "Algorithmic monoculture and social welfare," PNAS 118(22):e2018340118, which shows that convergence of many decision-makers on one algorithm produces correlated failure exposure, and that decision quality can fall even under normal operation without any shock.

  Summary: Strongly supported and EXTERNALLY ANCHORED (named external referents: Knight & Leveson 1986 in IEEE TSE; NUREG/CR-5485, Mosleh, Rasmuson & Marshall 1998, U.S. NRC; Panickssery, Bowman & Feng 2024 at NeurIPS; Xu et al. 2024 at ACL and Huang et al. 2024 at ICLR; Kuncheva & Whitaker 2003 in Machine Learning, with Kleinberg & Raghavan 2021 in PNAS). The convergent finding across four independent literatures is that separation of the checking *process* does not deliver independence of the checking *errors*, and that the correlation is carried by what the checkers share. Knight and Leveson demonstrated this experimentally under a separation regime far stronger than instance separation — 27 versions, independently written by different people at different institutions — and still found coincident failures well above the independence prediction; the shared specification was the coupling. Safety-critical practice has since codified the point: NUREG/CR-5485 requires that redundant units sharing a design lineage be placed in one common-cause component group and that the correlated term be quantified rather than assumed away, which reverses the presumption's ordering directly — under that rule, model-level correlation is the operative variable and instance-level separation is not a credit at all. The LLM-specific evidence closes the transfer: self-preference and self-recognition are properties of the model rather than the session (Panickssery et al.), self-bias is prevalent across model families and is amplified rather than corrected by internally-fed revision loops (Xu et al.), and intrinsic self-correction without external feedback does not improve reasoning (Huang et al.) — so the distinction that does work is internal versus external referent, which is what PREMISE-132 was minted to insist on. Finally, the ensemble literature establishes what would be needed to make "decorrelated" load-bearing: a measured joint error distribution, not a structural claim about instantiation. On this evidence the two unreconciled statements in the file resolve in favour of the batch header, not the verification section: MONITOR-486 standing is the accurate description, and the verification pass has not discharged what it claims to have applied.

  Caveats: (a) The strongest sources are analogical. Knight & Leveson concerns software versions and NUREG/CR-5485 concerns hardware channels; neither studies LLM instances, and the coupling mechanisms differ (shared specification and shared component design versus shared weights and training data). The mechanism transfers cleanly, the quantitative failure rates do not. (b) The presumption's target is the *warrant* for the decorrelation claim, and that is what the literature supports; none of these sources establishes that the five INCORPORATEs in this batch are in fact wrong, or that the citations the pass checked are in fact inaccurate. Correlated checkers still catch uncorrelated errors — the check has non-zero value, it simply cannot certify the shared failure modes it was built to catch. (c) Some decorrelation is plausibly bought at instance level: sampling temperature, context differences, and the absence of the first instance's reasoning trace do reduce error correlation relative to a same-context re-read, so the true independence is neither zero nor one. The literature's position is that the residual must be measured, not that it is total. (d) Xu et al.'s mitigation finding — external feedback with accurate assessment substantially reduces self-bias — points to the constructive remedy and would count in favour of the verification pass *if* the pass consulted external sources (retrieval of the cited works) rather than judging from the model's own knowledge; the distinguishing question for C2A2 is therefore not how many instances were used but whether each citation was resolved against an external record, which is what PREMISE-132's own wording ("citing is not verifying") demands. (e) Panickssery et al.'s self-recognition result is demonstrated on a small set of models and its magnitude varies; treating it as a general law of model families is an extrapolation. (f) Kuncheva & Whitaker's negative result about diversity measures also weakens the constructive prescription: measuring decorrelation is harder than it sounds and the measures may not predict the property wanted. (g) Good search scope: five targeted verification searches plus two corroboration searches; not exhausted on the LLM-as-judge bias literature, which is large and growing and would very likely add further same-direction evidence.
