    1. Hicks, D., Wouters, P., Waltman, L., de Rijcke, S., Rafols, I. (2015). "Bibliometrics: The Leiden Manifesto for research metrics." Nature 520, 429-431. — The governing statement for the item's situation. Ten principles for the use of research metrics, of which the first and most emphasised is that quantitative indicators must *support* and not replace qualitative expert assessment. The manifesto's premise is that a research enterprise steered by counts of its own outputs will optimise the counts; C2A2's INCORPORATE count is structurally identical to a publication count.
    2. San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA, 2012; sfdora.org). — Empirical precedent at scale. An entire scientific community, having observed the consequences, formally committed to abandoning a volume/impact proxy for research quality. That thousands of institutions found this necessary is evidence that the substitution 509 identifies is both common and consequential rather than a theoretical worry.
    3. Goodhart's law as formalised for evaluation regimes (FORRT glossary, "Goodhart's Law"; Wikipedia, "Goodhart's law"; Strathern's canonical rendering "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"). — Theoretical grounding. Applied to academic incentives, Goodhart's law predicts that metrics of scientific evaluation "will likely be abused and exploited," and the retrieved sources note that publication counts, citation counts and impact factor "have become targets and follow Goodhart's Law." This is the general law of which the item's claim is an instance.
    4. Responsible-metrics practice guidance (Virginia Tech, "Responsible Research Assessment"; University of Derby, "Responsible Use — Research Metrics"). — Supplies the remedy class: combine quantitative indicators with qualitative assessment, recognise disciplinary diversity, and never use a single indicator as a summary of quality. Directly applicable to replacing an INCORPORATE count with a composite.
    5. "What indicators matter? The Analysis of Perception toward Research Assessment Indicators and Leiden Manifesto." arXiv:1809.02953. — Empirical work on how researchers perceive assessment indicators, corroborating that volume indicators are widely recognised as poor quality proxies even by those measured on them — which parallels 509's sharpest observation, that the pipeline validated the very principle it then failed to apply to itself.

  Strength of support: Strong
  Summary: The item's claim is an instance of one of the best-documented pathologies in the evaluation of knowledge work, and the supporting literature is unusually mature: not merely a body of findings but a set of formal declarations — DORA and the Leiden Manifesto — adopted because the substitution of output volume for output value had measurable consequences at scale. Goodhart's law supplies the mechanism, and the research-assessment literature supplies the specific finding that publication counts, the closest available analogue to an INCORPORATE count, have demonstrably become targets and ceased to be good measures. The Leiden Manifesto's first principle is precisely the rule 509 says was violated: quantitative indicators must support rather than replace qualitative judgement. The item's most distinctive contribution — the reflexivity failure, in which the pipeline validated the Goodhart vulnerability of artifact-volume counts on the same day it used one as its own health metric — is not itself a literature-testable proposition, but it is consistent with the pattern in source 5, where researchers who recognise volume indicators as poor proxies nonetheless operate under them. The item's proposed test is also better than the literature's usual offering: defining in advance what a bad pipeline day looks like and classifying the last thirty days is a discrimination check, and a metric that never fires is not measuring health. That is a sharper falsification criterion than DORA or Leiden propose.
  Caveats: (a) The research-assessment literature concerns metrics attached to consequential incentives — funding, hiring, promotion. Goodhart's mechanism requires an optimising agent facing those incentives, and whether C2A2's pipeline has anything analogous is exactly what is unestablished; without an incentive gradient the count could be merely uninformative rather than actively corrupting. That distinction matters for the remedy and the item does not draw it. (b) A count can be a bad *summary* metric while remaining a legitimate *operational* one; the literature argues against single-indicator quality summaries, not against counting. (c) The reflexivity observation, though rhetorically strong, is an internal record claim outside search scope, and same-day co-occurrence is the weakest form of evidence for a systemic gap. (d) DORA and Leiden are advocacy documents with normative aims, not empirical studies; their evidential weight is as expert consensus and as evidence that the problem was large enough to require collective action. (e) The proposed remedy inherits a known risk: any replacement composite is itself Goodhart-vulnerable, and the responsible-metrics literature's answer — pair with qualitative judgement — routes straight back into the reviewer channel that PRESUMPTION-510 and PRESUMPTION-512 identify as saturated. The remedies for 509 and 512 are in direct tension and should be resolved together.
