  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: Both limbs of the claim find support. On the *breadth* limb, a mature metaresearch
    literature in evidence synthesis has repeatedly tested exactly this question and repeatedly
    found the marginal channel to be low-yield: Halladay et al. (2015) found a median of one
    unique record per review from EMBASE beyond PubMed and only modest effect on meta-analytic
    results; Bramer et al. (2017) found acceptable recall from four databases in 93% of reviews;
    the 2022 metaresearch study found >95% coverage from two or more; and the field's own summary
    is that literature searching obeys a law of diminishing returns. The rapid-review tradition
    institutionalises the trade. On the *unconsumed* limb, the data-valuation literature makes the
    conditional explicit — realised (post) value is defined by impact on a downstream task, and
    valuation methods assign zero to unused data — while the data-minimisation and dark-data
    literatures find that unconsumed collection is not cost-neutral but positively costly, taxing
    storage, pipeline, monitoring, engineering attention and legal exposure. Taken together these
    support a stronger statement than the claim makes: where items are not being consumed,
    narrowing collection is not merely low-cost but is the recommended default. The recoverability
    limb is weakly supported by the norm that documented searches are re-runnable and are in fact
    routinely re-run before write-up.

  Caveats: (1) The evidence-synthesis findings are about *conclusion stability in aggregate*, not
    about any particular item; every one of these studies also reports that some unique records
    exist only in the dropped channels, so the low cost is a low *expected* cost across many
    reviews rather than a guarantee for one. (2) Bramer et al. and the 2022 metaresearch study
    both condition the result on the retained channels being *well chosen* for the topic —
    topic-matched specialist sources are named as necessary — so the support covers principled
    narrowing, not arbitrary narrowing. (3) Domain transfer: these results concern bibliographic
    databases with heavy inter-database overlap. Where channels are largely disjoint, or where the
    reason for dropping a channel is correlated with what that channel uniquely contains, the
    overlap-driven redundancy that makes narrowing cheap does not obtain. (4) The recoverability
    limb is the weakest: the same reproducibility literature I cite for it reports that most
    published search strategies are not in practice reproducible, so "we can always backfill
    later" is an assumption about documentation quality rather than an established property.
    (5) The data-minimisation literature is a privacy/governance argument about personal data;
    its cost accounting transfers well but its normative force does not automatically.

  Recommendation: SUPPORTED

    literature evaluates breadth against a *concurrently conducted* synthesis; it does not evaluate
    breadth in a corpus whose consumption is deferred indefinitely, which is the case at issue here.
