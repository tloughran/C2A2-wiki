  Sources:
    1. Wang, P., Brown, C., Jennings, J.A., Stolee, K.T. (2020). "An Empirical Study on Regular Expression Bugs." MSR '20 (17th International Conference on Mining Software Repositories). — Analysed 356 regex-related bugs from merged pull requests across Apache, Mozilla, Facebook and Google repositories. Found that *incorrect regular expression semantics* is the dominant root cause (165/356, 46.3%) — i.e. the regex compiles, runs, and returns confidently wrong results. Also found regex bugs take more time and more lines of code to fix than general PRs. This is precisely "wrong by construction, silently."

    2. "Demystifying regular expression bugs: A comprehensive study on regular expression bug causes, fixes, and testing." Empirical Software Engineering 27 (2021/2022); preprint arXiv:2104.09693. [author list is the same NCSU group as source 1 but not individually re-verified] — Extends source 1 with ten recurring regex bug-fix patterns; reinforces that the dominant failure mode is a regex that matches the wrong *scope* rather than one that errors.

    3. Parasuraman, R., Manzey, D.H. (2010). "Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation: An Attentional Integration." Human Factors 52(3): 381–410. — Canonical synthesis of automation bias. Defines *commission errors*: incorrectly following an automated directive without verifying it against other available information, even when contra-indicating evidence exists. Directly models the hazard of a retirement pass acting on a detector's "absent" verdict.

    4. Mosier, K.L., Dunbar, M., McDonnell, L., Skitka, L.J., Burdick, M., Rosenblatt, B. (1998). "Automation Bias and Errors: Are Teams Better than Individuals?" Proceedings of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society Annual Meeting 42(3). — Empirical demonstration that automation bias occurs in both naive and expert operators and is not reliably trained away, supporting the argument that the defence must be architectural (reversibility) rather than procedural (be more careful).

    5. Krakovna, V., et al. (2020). "Avoiding Side Effects By Considering Future Tasks." NeurIPS 2020. — Part of the DeepMind safety line of work formalising impact penalties over *reachability*: an agent is penalised for reducing its ability to reach previously reachable states. Establishes irreversibility, not magnitude of change, as the primary safety-relevant axis for agent actions. Related: Krakovna et al. (2018), "Penalizing side effects using stepwise relative reachability," and Leike et al. (2017), "AI Safety Gridworlds" (arXiv:1711.09883), which includes irreversibility as a distinct safety problem class.

    6. Saltzer, J.H., Schroeder, M.D. (1975). "The Protection of Information in Computer Systems." Proceedings of the IEEE 63(9): 1278–1308. — The *fail-safe defaults* principle: base access decisions on permission rather than exclusion, so that the default outcome of a detection failure is to do nothing rather than to act. A detector that reads a present id as absent and then deletes is the textbook violation.

    7. Industry practice literature on malware quarantine (multiple vendor/practitioner sources, e.g. Huntress "What Is a False Positive Virus?"; Blumira glossary on quarantine; SecurView "Malware Quarantine"). — Documents that quarantine, not deletion, is the standard default response for EDR/AV precisely because a false positive on a legitimate file is otherwise unrecoverable: "the file is preserved in a locked-down state so security teams can ... recover it if it turns out to be a false positive." A direct, mature analogue of "retire-to-quarantine, don't delete."

    8. Automated program repair / patch-overfitting literature, e.g. "The Patch Overfitting Problem in Automated Program Repair: Practical Magnitude and a Baseline for Realistic Benchmarking," FSE '24 Companion (UCL). — Establishes that automated remediation routinely produces *plausible but incorrect* actions that pass the very oracle that authorised them. The retirement pass and its regex are exactly such a closed oracle loop.

    9. Practitioner literature on auto-remediation design (e.g. self-healing/auto-remediation guidance surveyed in SRE and Kubernetes operations writing). — Converges on the rule that remediation actions must be idempotent, reversible and auditable, that destructive operations (node/resource deletion) require human approval, and that dry-run or recommendation mode should precede enforcement. Non-peer-reviewed but broadly consistent across independent sources.

  Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: Every component of ASSUMPTION-1129 is independently well-supported. First, regex instruments failing by *scope* rather than by error is not an edge case but the single largest empirically measured category of regex defect (46.3% in Wang et al.'s 356-bug corpus), and such bugs are demonstrably harder and slower to fix than average — so "wrong by construction and silent" is the expected failure mode of a regex-scoped instrument, not an unusual one. Second, the human-factors literature establishes that operators and downstream automation follow a detector's verdict without independent verification (commission errors), that this occurs in experts as well as novices, and that training does not eliminate it — which is why the defence has to be built into the action rather than the reviewer. Third, the design requirement the assumption implies — reversible-by-default repair — is already the settled convention in two mature domains: security operations quarantine rather than delete on detection precisely to survive false positives, and AI-agent safety research treats irreversibility (measured as loss of reachability) as the primary quantity to penalise. The automated-program-repair literature completes the picture by showing that automated remediation reliably produces plausible-but-wrong actions when its only oracle is the same instrument that proposed them. The asymmetry the assumption asserts — that a false absence which triggers deletion "doesn't come back" — is exactly the asymmetry these literatures were built around.

  Caveats: (a) The AI-safety reachability work is theoretical/RL-environment work, not an empirical study of production deletion pipelines; the analogy is conceptual. Krakovna's own framing notes a limitation: a plain reversibility penalty is insensitive to *magnitude* and does not discriminate between necessary and unnecessary irreversible acts, so "penalise irreversibility" is not by itself a complete policy. (b) The quarantine-over-deletion evidence is practitioner literature, not peer-reviewed measurement; it establishes convention and rationale, not effect size. (c) Source 2's author list was not individually verified. (d) No source addresses the specific claim that *two* instruments were found wrong on the same day by the agents operating them; the co-occurrence rate of by-construction instrument errors is not something this search located, and the systemic-flakiness literature (co-occurring failures sharing root causes) is only suggestive here. (e) The patch-overfitting literature includes a 2024 result arguing the problem may be less severe than previously assumed — noted here as a scope limit on how hard to lean on source 8.
