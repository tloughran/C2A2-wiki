  THE ARITHMETIC, so far as the item's own numbers permit it — AND IT IS NOT A TEST:
    THE CENSUS MARGINALS. 35 overstate + 28 understate = 63 discrepant files of 307, i.e. 20.5% of the
    corpus disagrees, and among the disagreements the split is 55.6% / 44.4%. **That is close to even.**
    The census's finding is therefore not "the field errs in both directions somewhat" but "the
    discrepancy has no dominant direction," which is a materially stronger statement than the item makes.
    THE SAMPLES HIT THE MINORITY SIDE. The quoted sample instances are understatements (Day 262 at +27%,

    is 28/63 = 0.444 of the discrepant population.
    THE ILLUSTRATIVE FIGURE. If the cohorts were a random draw from the discrepant files, the chance that
    four consecutive draws all land on the minority side is 0.444^4 ≈ 3.9%; for five, ≈ 1.7%.
    **THIS IS NOT A VALID TEST AND MUST NOT BE REPORTED AS ONE.** Three reasons, each sufficient. (a) The
    cohorts were not drawn at random from the discrepant set — they are consecutive-day work packages, a
    BLOCK SAMPLE, which PREMISE-168's Geddes/ISA 530 citation already says is not projectable. (b) The
    direction was noticed after the first instances and subsequent ones were counted BECAUSE they agreed

    undeclared (PREMISE-136): "every instance so far" is over an unstated number of files, some of which
    may not be discrepant at all, so 0.444 may be the wrong marginal.
    WHAT THE FIGURE IS GOOD FOR, WHICH IS ONE THING. Had the cohorts been nominated in advance as a
    random sample, 3.9% would be the surprise the run should have felt — and feeling it would have
    prompted the right question. **Read forward rather than backward, the low figure is weak evidence
    that the six-pair cohorts are NOT exchangeable with the corpus**: consecutive days share a generation
    vintage, a template version and possibly a parser version, so a block of days may genuinely err in
    one direction while the corpus does not. That is a real and testable hypothesis, it explains all five
    reports and the census simultaneously with nobody being wrong, and it is a better reading of the day

  Strength of support: **Strong** on clauses (i) and (ii): the over-reading of small same-direction
  samples and the inflation produced by unstopped sequential confirmation are among the best-established
  results in the judgement literature, and the Klayman-Ha framing additionally explains why the runs'
  behaviour was locally rational. **Moderate-Strong** on clauses (iii) and (iv): the sample/census
  epistemics are well developed in official statistics but the located material establishes the OPPOSITE

  Summary: The corrective proposition is supported, and the useful finding is that the literature exonerates
  the four runs while condemning the word they used. Tversky and Kahneman established that people treat a
  small sample as representative of its population in all essential characteristics, direction included,
  and over-expect replication; Klayman and Ha established that testing the cases you expect to confirm is
  a general, usually efficient heuristic that produces systematic error only when applied to an
  inappropriate task — and a task with a cheap exhaustive census available is exactly that; Simmons,
  Nelson and Simonsohn established that accumulating observations and reporting the running tally without
  a pre-declared stopping rule inflates apparent support, with "decide the termination rule before
  collection begins" as the first of their six requirements. The register already holds most of this:
  PREMISE-120 forbids the phrase "a fifth independent confirmation" for checks sharing corpus, code path
  and execution context; PREMISE-140 bars streak framings on channel-derived metrics; PREMISE-136 requires
  every settling quantity to declare its scope; PREMISE-168, minted the same day as this item, already
  cites the audit-sampling doctrine that block samples do not project. What is genuinely new is
  structural: the four cohorts are NESTED INSIDE the census, so their instances are already among its 63
  discrepancies, and a nested same-method subsample of a completed census is not weak evidence about the
  population but no evidence at all — which is why "confirmation" is a category mistake here rather than
  an overstatement. And the arithmetic points somewhere the item does not look. The census's split is
  35/28, near-even; four to five draws all landing on the 44% side would happen about 4% or 2% of the time
  under random sampling, which — read forward rather than backward — is weak evidence that consecutive-day
  cohorts are NOT exchangeable with the corpus, and that a block of days may genuinely err one way while
  the corpus does not. That reading makes every run right, which is what the item says and does not
  quite show.
