  Sources:
    1. Bainbridge, L., 1983. "Ironies of Automation." Automatica 19(6): 775-779. — [FULL TEXT read] Mostly a warning, but Section 2 sets out conditional mitigations that bear directly on C2A2. Because people cannot watch for rare events for more than about 30 minutes, detection "has to be done by an automatic alarm system". "Automatic systems should fail obviously", and automatics should also flag unusual variable movement so that failures are not camouflaged. "The straightforward solution when shut-down is simple and low-cost is to shut down automatically." Processes that "can be left for several minutes" while the operator works out what is happening tolerate manual (delayed) recovery. Skills and working knowledge decay without use, so hands-on practice or simulator practice is needed. Relevance: a slow, cheap-to-halt batch pipeline is the benign case in Bainbridge's own scheme. She does not claim absence is safe; she claims it can be engineered around under these conditions.
    2. Endsley, M. R. & Kiris, E. O., 1995. "The Out-of-the-Loop Performance Problem and Level of Control in Automation." Human Factors 37(2): 381-394. DOI 10.1518/001872095779064555. — [ABSTRACT via TRID record and secondary citations] Out-of-the-loop decrements (slower decisions after automation failure) follow lost situation awareness, caused mainly by the shift from active to passive processing. The level of operator control moderates this loss. Theoretical support: intermediate levels of automation that keep the human doing some active processing reduce the damage. The study also shows that the problem is real.
    3. Parasuraman, R., Mouloua, M. & Molloy, R., 1996. "Effects of Adaptive Task Allocation on Monitoring of Automated Systems." Human Factors 38(4): 665-679. DOI 10.1518/001872096778827279. — [ABSTRACT via SWOV, TRID and UCF STARS records] Experimental. Detection of automation failures was low for every group during the first 40 minutes. Handing monitoring back to the human for 10 minutes mid-session significantly improved later failure detection under automation, whether the handback was model-based or performance-triggered. Direct empirical support that a periodic return of control, an engineered human-in-the-loop interval, reduces complacency damage.
    4. Patterson, E. S., Roth, E. M., Woods, D. D., Chow, R. & Gomes, J. O., 2004. "Handoff strategies in settings with high consequences for failure: lessons for health care operations." Int J Quality in Health Care 16(2): 125-132. DOI 10.1093/intqhc/mzh026. — [FULL TEXT read] Field study of NASA mission control, Canadian nuclear plants, US railroad dispatch and Toronto ambulance dispatch. It catalogues 21 handoff strategies; 19 were observed in at least one setting. Directly transferable to C2A2: outgoing writes a summary before the handoff (#8); incoming assesses current status at a glance (#9); incoming scans the historical log (#11) and reviews automatically captured changes (#12); "on call" staff keep intermittent monitoring with daily 15-minute updates (#13); responsibility is transferred unambiguously (#17); transfer is delayed while the process is unstable (#21). Limitation stated by the authors: they did not evaluate how effective any strategy is. They also advise that automated logs supplement, not replace, a human-to-human update.
    5. Parasuraman, R. & Riley, V., 1997. "Humans and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, Abuse." Human Factors 39(2): 230-253. — [ABSTRACT/secondary summaries] A theoretical framework. Monitoring failures under misuse depend on automation reliability and consistency and on how salient the status indicators are, so design levers exist. Alarm thresholds that ignore base rates drive disuse, which matters for how C2A2 monitors set their escalation thresholds.
    6. Klein, G., Woods, D. D., Bradshaw, J. M., Hoffman, R. R. & Feltovich, P. J., 2004. "Ten challenges for making automation a 'team player' in joint human-agent activity." IEEE Intelligent Systems 19(6): 91-95. — [SNIPPET/secondary summaries only; full text not read] Requirements for agents that coordinate with humans: mutual predictability, directability, common ground, and observability, the last as characterized in a later MITRE report. This is theoretical grounding for designing agents to be observable and redirectable, so that periodic review is informative enough for effective intervention.
    7. (Analogous, contextual) Eriksson, A. & Stanton, N. A., 2017. "Takeover Time in Highly Automated Vehicles: Noncritical Transitions to and From Manual Control." Human Factors 59(4): 689-705. DOI 10.1177/0018720816685832. — [ABSTRACT/press summaries] Noncritical takeover times ranged from 1.9 to 25.7 seconds. The authors advise designing for the full range rather than the mean. This is weak support: generous, non-urgent lead times make handovers workable, which C2A2's asynchronous review naturally provides.
    Also seen but not used as evidence: Wilkinson & Lardner 2013, "Shift Handover After Buncefield" (Chem Eng Trans), and the HSE safety-critical communications guidance, both seen only as snippets. They confirm that handover is a recognised accident contributor and say the gap is in applying known good practice, not in the research.

  Strength of support: Moderate (for "handover/escalation practices can contain the damage"); Weak (for "unattended operation with periodic review is safe enough" as such)

  Summary: The core supervisory-control literature does not support supervisor absence as safe by default. Bainbridge and Endsley & Kiris are primarily accounts of how absence and passivity degrade detection, situation awareness and takeover skill. They and later work do support the narrower claim that specific practices contain the damage: automatic alarms and automatic safe shutdown when halting is cheap; failures that show obviously; keeping the human actively involved through intermediate levels of control or periodic handback (Parasuraman et al. 1996, experimental); and structured handoffs built on written summaries, log and status review, on-call update routines, unambiguous transfer, and delaying transfer when the process is unstable (Patterson et al. 2004). By Bainbridge's own criteria, C2A2's pipelines are the benign case: slow dynamics, low cost to halt, and recovery can wait. That is analogous support for the presumption, conditional on those properties holding.

  Caveats:
    - Domain transfer: the sources concern humans co-present with real-time physical processes (process plants, cockpits, mission control, cars). C2A2's supervisor is absent for hours or days, not passively monitoring. Support transfers only through Bainbridge's "slow, cheaply halted process" argument.
    - Conditional support: support holds only if C2A2 monitors fail loudly, halt or quarantine automatically on anomaly, and leave legible logs. Bainbridge's question "who notices when the alarm system is not working properly" remains open for monitors run by the same unattended scheduler.
    - Patterson et al. describe practice and do not measure effectiveness. Parasuraman et al. 1996 is a laboratory study with short sessions (tens of minutes), not days.
    - Evidence levels: only Bainbridge and Patterson were read in full. Endsley & Kiris, Parasuraman et al. 1996, Parasuraman & Riley, and Eriksson & Stanton were seen at abstract level. Klein et al. 2004 was seen only through secondary summaries.
    - Publication bias: mitigation studies that report positive effects are likelier to be published.
