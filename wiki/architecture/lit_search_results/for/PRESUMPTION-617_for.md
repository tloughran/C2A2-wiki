  Sources:
    1. Lakens, D., 2024. "When and How to Deviate From a Preregistration." Collabra: Psychology, 10(1), 117094. — The clearest normative statement of the supported position: deviations arising from unforeseen events, errors in the preregistration, missing information, violated untested assumptions or falsified auxiliary hypotheses are legitimate, and the operative requirement is transparent presentation plus evaluation of the deviation's consequences — not withholding the result.
    2. Lakens, D. "Preregistration and Transparency," Ch. 13 in *Improving Your Statistical Inferences* (lakens.github.io/statistical_inferences). — "If you transparently list all deviations from your analysis plan, and provide strong justifications for these deviations, the readers can draw their own conclusions about how severely a hypothesis has been tested." This is the disclosure-discharges position stated explicitly, with the discharge located in the reader rather than in the result.
    3. Willroth, E.C. & Atherton, O.E., 2024. "Best Laid Plans: A Guide to Reporting Preregistration Deviations." Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 7(1). — Provides an accepted reporting apparatus for deviations, presupposing that a properly reported deviation yields a usable result. Supports the practice; note it prescribes a specific structured disclosure format, not a free-text aside.
    4. Claesen, A., Gomes, S., Tuerlinckx, F. & Vanpaemel, W., 2021. "Comparing dream to reality: an assessment of adherence of the first generation of preregistered studies." Royal Society Open Science, 8:211037. — 89% of the 27 Psychological Science preregistered-badge studies contained undisclosed deviations; the authors state adherence "may not always be feasible and is not necessarily desirable." Supports deviation-tolerance while locating the harm specifically in NON-disclosure.
    5. Sweetman, E.A. & Doig, G.S., 2011. "Failure to report protocol violations in clinical trials: a threat to internal validity?" Trials (PMC3192675). — Frames the threat to internal validity as failure to REPORT, which is the strongest available support for disclosure being the operative act. Also documents that unacknowledged protocol/report discrepancies undermine validity.
    6. Torka, A.-K. et al., 2025. "Going off script: Exploring the reporting of preregistration deviations in Industrial and Organizational Psychology and their relationship with questionable research practices." Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology. — Empirical study linking deviation-reporting practice to QRP prevalence; establishes reported deviation as a distinct and studied category rather than an automatic disqualifier.
    7. Lakens, D. "Preregistration does not improve the transparent evaluation of severity in Popper's philosophy of science or when deviations are allowed." arXiv:2408.12347. — Included as the boundary case: argues that once deviations are permitted, preregistration no longer secures a severity claim. Read as support, it says the deviation does not lower status because the status was never secured by the protocol; read as caveat, it says disclosure does not restore what the deviation removed.

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: There is a real, mainstream, explicitly argued position in the open-science methods literature that a disclosed and justified deviation yields a result that may be used, and that the epistemic work is done by transparency rather than by protocol adherence. Lakens (2024) and Willroth & Atherton (2024) are direct, citable support. However, every source that supports it attaches two conditions the C2A2 case does not satisfy: the disclosure must be structured and co-located with the result (not filed in a different artefact from the one that is load-bearing downstream), and it must include an explicit evaluation of what the deviation costs. Claesen et al. and Sweetman & Doig both locate the harm in disclosure failure — but "disclosed in the run footer, absent from the register entry" is nearer to non-disclosure at the point of use than to disclosure. No source supports a disclosed-deviation result carrying identical status with no marking on the artefact itself; the clinical-trial literature is explicit that major deviations are documented and summarised IN the study report, i.e. the deviation travels with the result.

  Caveats: (a) The supportive literature is normative/methodological commentary, not empirical outcome data — I found no study measuring whether downstream citation or reuse of disclosed-deviation results produces more error than protocol-conformant results, which is the empirical question PRESUMPTION-617 actually poses. (b) The trial literature distinguishes major from minor deviations, and "no independent verification occurred" is squarely a major deviation by the standard definition (potential to substantially affect scientific integrity or validity), a class for which the literature prescribes prompt reporting and explicit accounting, not silent parity. (c) The specific deviation here — loss of verification INDEPENDENCE, i.e. self-verification — is treated much more harshly in the audit and evaluation literature than in the preregistration literature; searching that literature (self-attestation vs external audit) yields materially less support. (d) Scope: preregistration norms govern analysis-plan deviations, not verification-independence lapses; the transfer is not exact.

  Search scope: Comprehensive within the preregistration-deviation and clinical-trial protocol-violation literatures; preliminary on the "status of results from non-independent verification" strand, which returned mostly practitioner and compliance material rather than primary research. Broader search into audit/assurance and metascience-on-replication-independence recommended.
