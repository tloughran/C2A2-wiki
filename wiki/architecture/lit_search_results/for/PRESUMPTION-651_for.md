  Sources:
    1. Brennan, R.L. Generalizability Theory (NCME instructional module);
       Shavelson, R.J., Webb, N.M. & Rowley, G.L., Generalizability Theory. —
       The framework's central claim runs against the presumption: observed
       score variance decomposes into a component for the object of
       measurement and separate components for each facet (rater, occasion,
       instrument) plus their interactions. The ideal case, in which nearly
       all variance is attributable to the object, is an empirical result to
       be demonstrated, not an assumption to be made.
    2. Kravitz, R.L. et al., 2010. "Editorial Peer Reviewers' Recommendations
       at a General Medical Journal: Are They Reliable and Do Editors Care?"
       PLoS ONE. — Chance-corrected agreement on rejection among reviewers
       was kappa 0.11; reviewer-level ICC 0.23 exceeded manuscript-level ICC
       0.17. The judge accounted for more of the disposition than the thing
       judged.
    3. Peer-review reliability meta-analytic literature (mean inter-reviewer
       correlation ≈0.34 across 45 studies; a further study finding ~20% of
       rating variance attributable to the reviewer). — Establishes that
       disposition mixes from single-pass expert review are substantially
       instrument-loaded as a general matter, not as a pathological case.
    4. NIST, Metrological Traceability policy; ISO/IEC 17025 traceability
       requirements. — Traceability is a property of a measurement result,
       not of an instrument; where the calibration chain is broken the result
       is not defensible. An instrument that has declared its own arrangement
       insufficient has, in these terms, an unclosed chain.
    5. Duke Global Health RDAC, Core Guide: Pilot & Feasibility Studies. —
       Names the specific error at issue: the commonest failure with pilot
       work is interpreting its results as definitive evidence rather than as
       feasibility signal.

  Strength of support: None

  Summary: No source was found supporting the attribution of a disposition mix
    to the items rather than to the instrument, and several bear directly
    against it. Generalizability theory treats object-attributable variance as
    something a G-study must estimate before any such attribution is licensed.
    The empirical peer-review literature, which is the closest analogue to a
    small panel issuing REVISE/ACCEPT-type dispositions, repeatedly finds
    judge-attributable variance at or above item-attributable variance. The
    metrology literature makes the self-declared insufficiency decisive: a
    result produced through an unclosed calibration chain is not defensible
    regardless of how confidently it is issued. The pilot-study literature
    names the exact inferential move as the field's most common mistake.
    Issuing at full authority is the aggravating factor, not the mitigating
    one — the located practice literature would have required the findings be
    marked provisional.

  Caveats: The peer-review evidence concerns human reviewers and multi-rater
    designs; a single deterministic instrument has a different variance
    structure, and a 6-of-7 skew could in principle reflect a genuinely poor
    item set. That possibility is not excluded by the literature — it is
    simply not established by the disposition mix alone, which is the point.
    A G-study or a re-run under the revised arrangement would settle it
    empirically; nothing short of that will.
