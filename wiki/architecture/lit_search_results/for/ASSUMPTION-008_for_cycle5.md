    6. May, K. O. (1952), majority-rule characterisation theorem, via Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Social Choice Theory" (plato.stanford.edu/entries/social-choice) — Majority rule is the *unique* aggregation rule satisfying universal domain, anonymity, neutrality and positive responsiveness. Since 2-of-3 is simple majority at n=3, this gives 2/3 a genuine axiomatic warrant that 3/3 (unanimity) and other supermajorities do not have.
    7. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Social Choice Theory," on supermajority rules; and Wikipedia, "Supermajority" — Both symmetric and asymmetric supermajority rules violate positive responsiveness.     8.   Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: "Meaningful" is supported; "optimal" and "sufficient" are not. Two independent lines converge. First, axiomatically: at n=3, a 2-of-3 rule *is* simple majority, and May's theorem makes majority rule the unique aggregation rule satisfying universal domain, anonymity, neutrality and positive responsiveness — so 2/3 is not an arbitrary pick but the only rule at this ensemble size with that warrant, and 3/3 would forfeit positive responsiveness. Second, empirically: small LLM panels with majority aggregation demonstrably beat single judges (PoLL), self-consistency majority voting yields double-digit accuracy gains, majority-rule ensembles win on categorisation, and at least one 2026 benchmark reports a 3-judge majority vote raising human agreement from chance-level to moderate (kappa 0.049 → 0.432) while damping single-model strictness bias. However, the disposition's actual decisive questions remain unanswered: no source located compares 1/2 vs 2/3 vs 3/3 *for this task class*, none characterises C2A2's error-cost structure, and — critically — the 2026 literature has moved sharply toward documenting the *failure* conditions of majority voting rather than confirming it.

  Caveats:
    - Correlated errors are the governing threat, and the strongest 2026 work is on that side. "Nine Judges, Two Effective Votes: Correlated Errors Undermine LLM Evaluation Panels" (arXiv:2605.29800) reports a 9-judge, 7-family panel carrying roughly *two independent votes* of information, with an 8–22 percentage-point accuracy shortfall versus independent prediction. If nine judges from seven model families are worth two votes, a 15a/15b/15c trio drawn from one model family is plausibly worth close to one. This is the direct, quantified answer to the disposition's groupthink question, and it does not favour the assumption.
    - The Condorcet guarantee that underwrites majority voting is conditional on error independence. Multiple 2026 sources ("Minority Sentinel," arXiv:2606.29270; "The Consensus Trap," arXiv:2604.17139) state that shared pretraining corpora make LLM errors strongly correlated, that positive inter-voter correlation weakens the theorem (Ladha), and that under high correlation majority voting can systematically lock in incorrect answers — termed "Tyranny of the Majority" (Estornell & Liu). C2A2's trio is *more* correlated than a cross-family panel, not less.
    - Aggregation-rule alternatives now beat majority vote: "Auditing Multi-Agent LLM Reasoning Trees Outperforms Majority Vote and LLM-as-Judge" (arXiv:2602.09341) and "When Agents Disagree: The Selection Bottleneck in Multi-Agent LLM Pipelines" (arXiv:2603.20324). The assumption's weakest point is no longer whether 2/3 is meaningful but whether voting is the right aggregation primitive at all.
    - Agreeableness/sycophancy bias in judge ensembles is documented ("Beyond Consensus: Mitigating the Agreeableness Bias in LLM Judge Evaluations," arXiv:2510.11822), which inflates apparent consensus independently of correctness.
    - Structural note specific to C2A2: 15a and 15b are *assigned opposite directions* (FOR / AGAINST). A directionally-assigned trio is not a jury of independent voters, and none of the located voting literature covers adversarially-assigned ensembles. The Condorcet framing may simply not apply to this architecture.
    - Threshold optimality is error-cost dependent, and C2A2's error-cost structure is still unspecified — the same gap flagged in April, unclosed.
