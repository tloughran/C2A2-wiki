  Sources:
    1. Schlichting, R.D., & Schneider, F.B. (1983). "Fail-stop processors: an approach to designing fault-tolerant computing systems." ACM Transactions on Computer Systems 1(3):222-238. — The foundational statement of the fail-stop fault class: a processor that "automatically halts in response to any internal failure and does so before the effects of that failure become visible," offered precisely because this behaviour must be *engineered* and cannot be assumed of an arbitrary component. The paper's whole point is that failure MODE is a design property, and that programs must be written against a declared mode. This is direct external support for the presumption's core distinction: producing wrong records (a wrong-pids defect, which is fail-silent-AND-wrong in PREMISE-128's terms) and producing nothing at all are different failure modes with different containment properties, and the second does not follow from the first. Adjacency in time does not merge two fault classes.
    2. Beyer, B., Jones, C., Petoff, J., & Murphy, N.R. (eds.) (2016). Site Reliability Engineering: How Google Runs Production Systems. O'Reilly Media — chapters "Monitoring Distributed Systems" and "Practical Alerting from Time-Series Data"; together with the Prometheus project documentation, "Query functions" (prometheus.io), which specifies `absent()` and `absent_over_time()` as the functions that "alert on when no time series exist for a given metric name and label combination," and Grafana Labs, "Handle missing data in Grafana Alerting." — Detection of an expected-but-missing signal is treated in this literature as a *named, first-class, separately-implemented* control, not as a by-product of error monitoring. The SRE material records the Gmail case in which alerts fired when individual tasks were de-scheduled by the workqueue — an absence, not an error. The Prometheus documentation supplies the exact capability the presumption says is missing: an assertion over the existence of an expected output that fails loudly when the output does not appear. This is strong external support for the presumption's positive claim (the missing capability is real, standard, and cheap), and for its structural observation that this is the same class of absence as a missing reconciliation assertion — both are checks that must be able to fail.
    3. IEEE 1044-2009, IEEE Standard Classification for Software Anomalies (IEEE Standards Association; adopted as ISO/IEC/IEEE 1044:2010; published 2010-01-07, inactivated 2020-03-05). — A standards-body treatment that separates the classification of a *failure* (the observed anomalous behaviour) from that of a *defect* ("an imperfection or deficiency in a work product where that work product does not meet its requirements"), and structures anomaly handling as recognition -> investigation -> action -> disposition, with the investigation step being what establishes the defect behind a failure. Under this standard, an observed non-generation is a failure awaiting investigation; the wrong-pids item is a defect already investigated. Filing the first as a consequence of the second collapses two stages of the standard's own workflow, and does so without the step (investigation) that would license the link.
    4. ITIL / ITSM problem-management practice, as documented across the practitioner literature (IT Process Wiki, "Problem Management"; Atlassian, "Problem Management in ITIL"; IBM, "Incident management vs. problem management"). — The framework's definitional structure is directly on point: a *problem* is the underlying cause of one or more incidents whose root cause has NOT yet been determined; it becomes a *known error* only once investigation and diagnosis have documented the root cause. The known-error record is defined as the artefact carrying the diagnosis. The framework therefore makes the diagnosis step the explicit gate between "an incident occurred" and "this incident is attributable to that cause" — which is exactly the gate the presumption says was passed without being opened. It also separates incident triage (restore service, impact-driven) from problem management (eliminate cause), supporting the presumption's queue-placement objection: two items at different investigation stages are not naturally the same queue item.
    5. Croskerry, P. (2003). "The Importance of Cognitive Errors in Diagnosis and Strategies to Minimize Them." Academic Medicine 78(8):775-780; with the associated literature on premature diagnostic closure. — Names and characterises *search satisficing*, the tendency to call off the search for further possibilities once a satisfactory explanation has been found, and *anchoring*, the tendency to fix on an early salient feature; both are identified as leading contributors to diagnostic error, and both are described as operating without the reasoner's awareness. Attaching an unexplained absence to the loud, already-diagnosed fault sitting next to it is a textbook instance of the pattern this literature documents, and it supplies external support for 14b's stated mechanism ("a silent null is easy to attach to the loud fault next to it") rather than merely for its conclusion.

  Summary: Supported, and EXTERNALLY ANCHORED (named external referents: Schlichting & Schneider 1983 in ACM TOCS; the Google SRE book, Beyer et al. 2016, O'Reilly; the Prometheus project's `absent_over_time` documentation; IEEE 1044-2009 / ISO-IEC-IEEE 1044:2010; and Croskerry 2003 in Academic Medicine). The presumption's strongest element — that a fails-to-run event and a produces-wrong-output event are distinct fault classes and that neither implies the other — is the founding distinction of the fail-stop literature, where failure mode is treated as a designed and declared property rather than something inferable from a neighbouring fault. Its second element, that an existence check on scheduled outputs is a missing capability, is supported unusually concretely: absence detection is a named, standard, separately-implemented control in production monitoring, with a specific canonical implementation (`absent()` / `absent_over_time()`) whose documented purpose is to alert when an expected series never appears. On the causal-link objection, both a standards-body classification (IEEE 1044's recognition/investigation/action sequence) and the ITIL problem/known-error distinction make diagnosis the explicit gate that converts an observed failure into an attributable cause; the summary attributed without passing that gate. Finally, the diagnostic-error literature supplies external grounding for the specific cognitive mechanism 14b proposed, naming search satisficing and premature closure as documented, unconscious failure modes of exactly this shape. The one element the literature supports least directly is the queue-and-priority claim, which is treated below.

  Caveats: (a) The queue/priority element is the weakest. No source found states that an undiagnosed fault must be prioritised ABOVE a known defect; standard triage practice prioritises by impact and business risk, and a defensible triage could rationally rank a known, decided-upon defect higher than a one-off missing artefact. What the literature supports is the narrower and different claim that the two are at different *stages* (uninvestigated failure vs. diagnosed defect) and so are not naturally the same queue item — and, via PREMISE-128's own logic, that a fault of unknown scope has an unbounded rather than a low blast radius, which is a reason to bound it, not automatically a reason to raise its priority. The presumption is on firm ground about classification and about the missing check; it is on softer ground about priority. (b) The ITIL material (source 4) is practitioner documentation rather than a primary standard or peer-reviewed research; the definitional structure it reports is stable and uncontested across independent sources, but it carries the epistemic weight of codified practice, not of evidence. (c) IEEE 1044-2009 was inactivated in 2020, so it is a lapsed rather than current standard; its classification scheme remains widely referenced but should not be cited as an in-force requirement. (d) Croskerry's domain is emergency-department clinical diagnosis. The cognitive mechanism transfers plausibly to fault triage — both are diagnosis under time pressure with salient competing cues — but the transfer is by analogy, and per PREMISE-127/134 that analogy is itself an unverified cross-domain mapping; it is offered here as mechanism support, not as evidence about software pipelines. (e) The monitoring sources (2) establish that the capability exists and is standard; they do not establish that its absence *caused* anything here, and none of the sources establishes what actually happened at 04:38. Every source supports the claim that the attribution was unwarranted; none supports the claim that the attribution was wrong. This is a warrant finding, not a fault finding. (f) A substantial portion of the initial hits for the absence-detection query were vendor and blog material (heartbeat-monitoring products, cron-monitoring services); these were excluded in favour of the book, the project documentation and the standard, but their volume is itself a mild publication-bias signal in the supportive direction — the pattern is commercially promoted.
