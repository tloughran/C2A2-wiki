  Sources:
    1. List, Christian, and Philip Pettit. 2002. "Aggregating Sets of Judgments: An Impossibility Result." Economics and Philosophy 18(1): 89–110. — List and Pettit's discursive-dilemma result demonstrates that conclusion-level agreement can mask deep premise-level disagreement: a group may unanimously accept a conclusion while each member's supporting premises diverge or even contradict. This is the theoretical core of the assumption: if only conclusions are logged, the record cannot distinguish genuine convergence (same reasons, same conclusion) from spurious agreement (different reasons, coincidentally identical conclusion). Separate logging of reasons is the natural corrective, providing a record that can distinguish these cases.

    2. Stasser, Garold, and William Titus. 1985. "Pooling of Unshared Information in Group Decision Making: Biased Information Sampling During Discussion." Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 48(6): 1467–1478. — Stasser and Titus found that groups systematically over-discuss shared information and under-discuss unshared information, producing apparent consensus that reflects only the common informational base rather than the full collective knowledge. In the C2A2 context, if reasons are not separately logged, the agreement record may similarly reflect only the shared surface of reasons, obscuring divergent underlying rationales. Separate reason logging is a structural corrective to this bias.

    3. Sunstein, Cass R. 1995. "Incompletely Theorized Agreements." Harvard Law Review 108(7): 1733–1772. — Sunstein explicitly notes that "spurious unanimity" arises when a shared conclusion results from opposing background reasons. Distinguishing genuine from spurious agreement requires access to the underlying reasons, not merely the conclusion. This directly supports the dual-reasons rule: separately logged reasons preserve the evidence necessary for this distinction.

    4. List, Christian. 2004. "A Model of Path-Dependence in Decisions over Multiple Propositions." American Political Science Review 98(3): 495–513. — List demonstrates that the order in which propositions are decided can affect collective outcomes, and that premise-level and conclusion-level majority voting can diverge. A log of separately recorded reasons provides the evidential trail needed to diagnose whether conclusion-level agreement reflects genuine or path-dependent convergence. This supports the evidentiary rationale for the dual-reasons rule.

    5. Cohen, Joshua. 1986. "An Epistemic Conception of Democracy." Ethics 97(1): 26–38. — Cohen argues that legitimate deliberative outcomes require that the reasons giving rise to decisions be themselves subject to public assessment. Logging reasons separately makes reasons assessable independently of their conclusion, preserving the epistemic quality of the agreement record. This supports the claim that separate reason logging preserves evidence of genuine (as opposed to procedurally-generated) agreement.

  Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: The discursive-dilemma literature (List and Pettit) establishes formally that conclusion-level agreement does not imply reason-level agreement, and that shared conclusions can arise from divergent or even opposing premises. The hidden-profile literature (Stasser and Titus) demonstrates empirically that group discussion systematically under-surfaces divergent informational bases, making apparent consensus unreliable. Sunstein explicitly identifies spurious unanimity as a failure mode. The dual-reasons rule — separately logging the reasons for each party's rung agreement — is the natural and well-grounded corrective. It preserves the evidential record needed to distinguish genuine agreement (same conclusion, convergent reasons) from convergence-by-adjudication (same conclusion, divergent or arbitrarily reconciled reasons). This evidentiary function is supported by all sources reviewed.

  Caveats: The literature directly addresses group decision-making and collective judgment, which is structurally analogous to but not identical with the C2A2 human-AI dyadic ladder context. The dual-reasons rule is a sound procedural corrective in both contexts, but the specific design and implementation of the logging protocol is a C2A2-internal engineering decision that the literature does not address.
