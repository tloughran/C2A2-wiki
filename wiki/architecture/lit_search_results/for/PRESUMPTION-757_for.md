  Original statement: Whether a source's self-revision binds a construct derived from it. Tested here as the operative proposition: that when an upstream source revises its own theory or definition, a construct derived from that source is thereby obliged to change (or to be revalidated) rather than continuing unchanged under its original operationalisation.

  Supporting evidence found: Partial

  Sources:
    1. Cronbach, L. J., & Meehl, P. E., 1955. "Construct Validity in Psychological Tests." Psychological Bulletin 52(4), 281–302. — The foundational statement. A construct is *implicitly defined* by its position in a nomological network deduced from theory; the network's laws relate observables to each other, constructs to observables, and constructs to one another. On this account, to know what a construct is *is* to know its position in the network. It follows directly that revising the network revises the construct's meaning — this is the strongest available support for the "binds" claim, though it is a conceptual, not empirical, warrant. Construct validation and theory testing are explicitly intertwined in the original formulation.
    2. "Repositioning Construct Validity Theory: From Nomological Networks to Pragmatic Theories and Their Evaluation by Explanatory Means," PMC11881521 [author list not independently confirmed]. — Contemporary re-examination arguing that nomological networks do more than validate measures: they establish what those measures mean in the first place, through implicit definition. Reinforces that construct meaning is not stable independently of its source theory.
    3. "Uncovering Jingle and Jangle Fallacies: A Systematic Review Mapping Future Directions," Zeitschrift für Psychologie 233(4), doi:10.1027/2151-2604/a000602 [author list not independently confirmed]. — Systematic review documenting that jingle fallacies (same name, different phenomena) arise specifically from "the vague connection between psychological theory and its inadequate operationalization in empirical studies." Where a source revises its theory but the derived measure keeps the old name and old items, a jingle fallacy is the predicted outcome — support for the claim that the derived construct is materially affected. The review's recommended remedies are revision of theory, improved construct validity, and new instruments, i.e. the binding is treated as normative in the field.
    4. "Extending Lawson and Robins' (2021) guideline for the evaluation of jingle and jangle fallacies," Behavior Research Methods, 2025, PMC12089215 [author list not independently confirmed; builds on Lawson & Robins, 2021, on sibling constructs]. — Provides formal criteria for deciding whether two similarly-named constructs are in fact the same, which is the operational test a derived construct would need to pass after its source has been revised.
    5. Borsboom, D., Mellenbergh, G. J., & van Heerden, J., 2004. "The Concept of Validity." Psychological Review 111(4), 1061–1071. [Cited from standing knowledge of the validity literature; not retrieved in this session's searches — treat as a pointer for follow-up rather than as evidence gathered here.] — Argues validity is a property of the causal relation between attribute and measurement outcome, which would *weaken* the binding claim; noted here for completeness because it bears directly on the item and is more likely to be developed by sibling agent 15b.

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: The strongest support is definitional rather than empirical. Cronbach and Meehl's original 1955 account makes a construct's scientific meaning constitutive of its place in a nomological network deduced from theory — so a source's self-revision does not merely bear on a derived construct, it alters what that construct means, and validation and theory-testing were never separable in the original formulation. Contemporary re-readings of construct validity reinforce this "implicit definition" reading. The empirical literature on jingle and jangle fallacies supplies the practical consequence: when theory moves and operationalisation does not, the field ends up with identically-named measures capturing different phenomena, and the systematic review of that literature identifies inadequate operationalisation relative to theory as the mechanism, recommending theory revision, new instruments and revalidation as the remedy. This is enough to support the presumption as a methodological norm — a derived construct inherits an obligation to be re-examined when its source revises — but it is not enough to support the stronger reading that the derived construct is automatically invalidated. The literature's own recommendation is revalidation, i.e. a test, not an automatic verdict.

  Caveats: (a) The support is largely conceptual/normative; no study was located that empirically measures how often a source theory's revision actually invalidates a previously-derived measure, so the "binds" claim is well-motivated rather than demonstrated. (b) There is a significant counter-tradition — the Borsboom et al. (2004) line treats validity as a causal property of the attribute–measurement relation, on which a measure could remain valid while its parent theory is revised. That reading is not developed here because it runs against the FOR direction; it should be expected from sibling agent 15b and materially bounds the present result. (c) Everything located is from psychometrics; transfer to an identifier/claim schema is by structural analogy. (d) Author lists for sources 2, 3 and 4 could not be independently verified before the session's web-search budget was exhausted and are therefore given by title, venue and PMID/DOI only. Source 5 was not retrieved in this session at all and is flagged as a follow-up pointer, not as gathered evidence.
