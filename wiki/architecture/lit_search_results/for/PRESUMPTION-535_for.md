  Sources:
    1. Bainbridge, L. (1983). "Ironies of automation." Automatica 19(6):775-779. — Canonical: automating a human-in-the-loop process does not remove the human requirement; it relocates and degrades it. The designer's errors are "frozen in code," and the operator, now out of the loop, is least able to intervene when needed.
    2. Parasuraman, R. & Riley, V. (1997). "Humans and automation: use, misuse, disuse, abuse." Human Factors 39(2):230-253. — Documents automation complacency and over-reliance: monitoring performance decays precisely when automation is highly reliable, so an unattended stretch is a risk-accumulation state, not a stable equilibrium.
    3. Out-of-the-loop performance problem (Endsley & Kiris, 1995; review literature). — Loss of situation awareness and manual skill under prolonged automation makes takeover slower and more error-prone; "stable" unattended running masks a growing takeover deficit.

  Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: The presumption that unattended operation is a benign steady state is challenged by a mature human-factors literature. Bainbridge's ironies, automation complacency (Parasuraman & Riley), and the out-of-the-loop problem all say the same thing: removing the human from an originally human-in-the-loop design does not reach equilibrium — it silently accumulates skill/awareness/takeover debt while surface behavior looks normal. 18 attended-free days is better read as latent degradation than as steady state.

  Caveats: The literature is from safety-critical supervisory control (aviation, process plants). C2A2 is lower-stakes and its "automation" is agentic text processing; the mechanism (out-of-loop debt) transfers, the catastrophic-failure framing does not necessarily.
