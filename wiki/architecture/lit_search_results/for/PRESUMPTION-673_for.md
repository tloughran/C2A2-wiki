  Sources:
    1. Tucker, A.L. & Edmondson, A.C., 2003. "Why Hospitals Don't Learn from
       Failures: Organizational and Psychological Dynamics That Inhibit System
       Change." California Management Review 45(2): 55-72. — The central
       quantitative result runs against the presumption: observed process
       failures elicited a workaround or quick fix 93% of the time and a report
       to someone able to change the system 7% of the time. The cost is
       therefore paid repeatedly by construction, because the act that would
       make it a one-time cost is the rare one.
    2. Tucker, A.L., Edmondson, A.C. & Spear, S., 2002. "When Problem Solving
       Prevents Organizational Learning." Journal of Organizational Change
       Management 15(2): 122-137. — Names the mechanism: first-order problem
       solving (FOPSB) restores flow without removing the cause, and its very
       success suppresses the signal that would trigger second-order problem
       solving (SOPSB). The workaround feels like a resolution to the actor who
       performs it, which is precisely the presumption under test.
    3. Alter, S., 2014. "Theory of Workarounds." Communications of the
       Association for Information Systems 34, Article 55: 1041-1066. — The one
       genuinely supportive source. Alter's process theory is explicit that
       workarounds can and sometimes do feed into larger planned changes, i.e.
       that the cost can be paid once. But the theory makes this contingent on
       the interaction of factors that determine whether a workaround is
       considered, executed and then escalated — including the actor's
       authorisation and the visibility of the workaround to those who can
       change the system.
    4. Woods, D.D., Allspaw, J., Cook, R.I. et al., 2017. The STELLA Report:
       Report from the SNAFUcatchers Workshop on Coping With Complexity.
       SNAFUcatchers consortium. — Introduces dark debt: debt that lives in the
       interactions between components rather than in any component, is not
       detectable by inspecting the pieces, and surfaces only through anomalies.
       Repeated undocumented workarounds are a canonical accumulation site,
       because the knowledge that would make them visible never leaves the
       run that performed them.
    5. Walsh, J.P. & Ungson, G.R., 1991. "Organizational Memory." Academy of
       Management Review 16(1): 57-91. — The theoretical grounding for the
       supportive direction: knowledge acquired in one episode is available to a
       later one only if it is deposited in a retention facility outside the
       individual actor. Where no retention bin exists, the acquisition cost is
       re-incurred each episode. An agent run that ends without writing the
       workaround down has no retention bin.
    6. "Reducing Maintenance Effort through Software Operation Knowledge: An
       Eclectic Empirical Evaluation." CSMR/software-maintenance literature,
       2011. [AUTHORS UNVERIFIED — retrieved by title only.] — States the
       software-specific form: engineering knowledge that is not documented is
       volatile, and organisations repeatedly pay professionals to rediscover
       knowledge previously acquired and lost. Cited for the framing, not as
       load-bearing evidence.

  Strength of support: Weak

  Summary: No literature supports the unconditional claim that a workaround's
    cost is paid once. The best-established empirical finding points the other
    way: workarounds are overwhelmingly first-order, and their success is what
    prevents the second-order work that would make the cost one-time. Support
    exists only in conditional form. Alter's theory of workarounds and the
    second-order problem-solving literature both describe a real route by which
    a workaround converts into a durable fix — but that route requires the
    workaround to be reported to, and acted on by, someone authorised to change
    the system. The item as reported states that both agents named the durable
    fix and neither was authorised to make it, which places the case on the
    unsupported side of exactly the condition the supportive literature
    identifies. The organizational-memory and dark-debt literatures explain the
    persistence: with no retention facility outside the run, each run re-incurs
    the acquisition cost, and the resulting debt is invisible to component-level
    inspection.

  Caveats: The strongest evidence (Tucker & Edmondson) is from hospital nursing,
    where the actor is a human under time pressure and reporting is socially
    costly; the transfer to an agent run is by analogy, and the agent case
    differs in one relevant respect — both agents did in fact disclose the
    workaround and name the durable fix, which is second-order behaviour and
    is more than the 7% baseline predicts. This makes the failure mode here
    narrower than the literature's: not a failure to report, but a failure of
    the report to reach an authorised actor. That narrower failure is well
    described by Alter but is not, on this search, separately quantified.
    Publication bias note: the knowledge-management literature is systematically
    optimistic about capture mechanisms and reports success cases; treat the
    "capture solves it" claim as under-tested rather than established.
