  Sources:
    1. W3C, 2013. PROV-Overview and PROV-DM (W3C Working Group Note / Recommendation family). — The standard itself supports the corrective reading, carefully. PROV defines provenance as "information about entities, activities, and people involved in producing a piece of data... which can be used to form assessments about its quality, reliability or trustworthiness" — i.e. provenance is an input to a trust judgement, not a warrant. PROV-DM models provenance as *assertions attributed to agents*; the standard provides no integrity mechanism and does not claim that a provenance record is true. Reading a document's own provenance claim as evidence about the document is a misuse the specification's own framing does not license. ACM TISSEC 2(2). — The classical result that an audit trail is only evidence to the extent it is cryptographically bound and tamper-evident; an unprotected log can be rewritten by whoever holds the pen, including retroactively. Theoretical grounding for the distinction 14b's item turns on: asserted metadata versus unforgeable metadata is a categorical difference, not a difference of degree. The profession that has thought longest about trusting documents ranks self-generated provenance near the bottom.   Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: The corrective proposition is strongly supported and, in the audit profession, is codified. Three independent traditions converge: provenance standards (PROV models provenance as agent assertions and explicitly frames it as an input to assessment rather than a warrant of quality), security engineering (an audit trail is evidence only insofar as it is tamper-evident, per Schneier and Kelsey), and audit doctrine (external evidence outranks internally-generated evidence, and self-attestation is treated differently from third-party attestation by regulators and courts). Nisbett and Wilson supply the deepest version of the point: a producer's narrative about how it produced something is a post-hoc account, not an observation. The agent-specific literature adds the base-rate concern most relevant to C2A2 — in systems where a component can write its own provenance, fabricated provenance becomes both an accident mode and an exploit surface, and at least three 2026 preprints appear to treat it as a measured phenomenon.

  Caveats: (a) The literature supports discounting self-reported provenance; it does not tell you what to do when no independent record exists. In C2A2 the independent record would be the tool-call trail, which per PRESUMPTION-777 is unavailable — so the remedy this literature prescribes (verify against external evidence) may not be executable, and the honest conclusion may be that affected provenance claims are simply not evidence rather than that they should be checked. That coupling matters and I flag it for 15c. (b) Three false claims in one day is a rate but not a base rate: without a denominator (how many provenance claims were examined) the severity cannot be calibrated, and nothing found here supplies a comparable denominator from another system. (c) The self-report bias literature transfers imperfectly — its mechanisms are motivational and mnemonic, neither of which straightforwardly describes a generated document. (d) PROV's own position is subtler than a simple endorsement: the standard is agnostic, not sceptical, and citing it as *support* for distrust is a reading of its framing rather than a finding it reports.
