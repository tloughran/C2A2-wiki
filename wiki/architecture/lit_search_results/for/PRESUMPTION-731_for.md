[foundational; widely re-cited, including in Columbia Magazine's 2021 retrospective and a 2022 ScienceDirect systematic review] — "Normalization of deviance": repeated disclosure/acceptance of a known deviation without catastrophic consequence causes the deviation to become the accepted baseline rather than triggering correction — precisely the "thirteen consecutive days ... with no change" pattern.

"A qualitative systematic review on the application of the normalization of deviance phenomenon within high-risk industries." — Confirms the mechanism generalizes across industries: repeated tolerance of a known-nonconforming state (here: a breached budget/ceiling) erodes the organizational response to it over time.
    3. Compliance Week, "Institutional resilience: Why remediation fails without independent governance challenge." — Directly on point for "compliance-by-disclosure": states that governance/remediation that does not change how decisions are made allows "the same mistakes to happen over and over," and that a governance challenge "if it does not influence outcomes over time... is not independent, it's just a formality" — this is a direct analogue to disclosure without remediation discharging an obligation only nominally.
    4. NYU Law Review, "Asymmetrical Regulation: Risk, Preemption, and the Floor/Ceiling Distinction," Buzbee, W. — Establishes the floor/ceiling framing in governance: a floor sets a minimum that cannot be dropped below, a ceiling sets a maximum that cannot be exceeded; when a mandatory floor and an imposed ceiling are both binding and inconsistent, the requirement set is infeasible by construction, which is the structural claim embedded in "one ceiling unsatisfiable... makes the other near-unmeetable."
    5. arXiv:2401.05673, "Analyzing and Debugging Normative Requirements via Satisfiability Checking." — Formal treatment of exactly this failure mode: normative requirement sets can be "subject to boundary conditions rendering the requirements infeasible given their inconsistent prescription" — i.e., a system can be in a state where no compliant action exists, and disclosure of that state is not itself a resolution.
