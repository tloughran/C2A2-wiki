  Sources:
    1. The McNamara fallacy (Wikipedia, "McNamara fallacy"; Logically Fallacious; FallacyCheck; Yankelovich's original four-step formulation as reproduced in these sources). — Direct theoretical grounding, and the closest named analogue to the item's claim in reverse. The canonical four steps are: measure what can easily be measured; disregard what cannot; presume what cannot be measured easily is not important; conclude that what cannot be measured does not exist. The item asserts the same terminal state reached by a different route — that an objective which survives only in unmeasurable form ceases to function as an objective. The documented historical instance is exact: McNamara struck the resolve of the rural Vietnamese population from his analysis on the explicit ground that it was "unmeasurable," and the model then behaved as though it were zero.
    2. Bächtiger, A. et al. "Measuring Deliberation" (Ash Center, Harvard) and Steenbergen/Bächtiger/Spörndli/Steiner, "Measuring Political Deliberation: A Discourse Quality Index." — The key finding for the item's operative clause. Deliberation research distinguishes measuring the *conditions* enabling deliberation from measuring its *outcomes* (opinion shift, enhanced mutual understanding, decision quality), and the Discourse Quality Index operationalises "mutual understanding" via the presence and level of justification in statements. This establishes that a separable alternative to convergence is constructible — mutual registration is measurable — which is precisely the precondition the item says must exist before convergence is removed.
    3. Goddard, A. & Gillespie, A. (2023). "Textual Indicators of Deliberative Dialogue: A Systematic Review of Methods for Studying the Quality of Online Dialogues." Social Science Computer Review, 37(1)/doi:10.1177/08944393231156629. — Systematic review supplying an inventory of validated text-based dialogue-quality indicators measurable within a turn, between turns, or across a whole dialogue. Empirical precedent that the replacement measure the item demands is available off the shelf rather than needing invention.
    4. "CIG: Measuring Conversational Information Gain in Deliberative Dialogues with Semantic Memory Dynamics." arXiv:2604.15647. — Recent work explicitly motivated by the observation that existing deliberation metrics "struggle to capture true informational progress," and proposing a computable alternative. Directly relevant: it is an instance of the discipline doing what 485 says C2A2 should do — build the separable measure first, then displace the old one.
    5. Goodhart's law as formalised for evaluation regimes (FORRT glossary, "Goodhart's Law"; Wikipedia, "Goodhart's law"). — Supplies the counterweight the item is implicitly answering: convergence may indeed be a corrupted proxy. Retrieved so that the item's support is not stated as though the objection did not exist; Goodhart argues for replacement, not for deletion, which is 485's position.

  Strength of support: Moderate-to-Strong
  Summary: The item's general principle has a named fallacy attached to it and a canonical historical case. The McNamara fallacy describes precisely the failure mode in which a construct that resists measurement is first excluded from the analysis and then, in effect, from existence; 485 asserts the same collapse when a possibly-mis-specified objective is retired in favour of one that cannot be scored. The item's specific operational recommendation — do not remove convergence from Rung-2 scoring until a separable alternative exists — is additionally supported by the deliberation-measurement literature, which shows that the alternative is neither hypothetical nor expensive: mutual understanding is operationalised in the Discourse Quality Index through justification level, a systematic review catalogues validated text-based indicators, and recent computational work provides an information-gain measure designed for exactly this gap. That combination is the strong form of the item's case: the sequencing rule is justified not because no better measure is possible but because better measures demonstrably exist and can be instrumented in parallel before the switch. The in-house test named in the item — rank-correlating convergence, mutual registration, and an ITT-style score over existing dialogues with a mandatory pre-dialogue baseline — is the standard construct-validity procedure for exactly this substitution and is well matched to the literature.
  Caveats: (a) The McNamara fallacy is a rhetorical/logical construct with a historical illustration, not an empirically tested proposition; it grounds the item's reasoning but does not evidence it. (b) The deliberation-measurement sources concern human political deliberation; transfer to agent dialogues is by analogy, and the DQI's justification-level coding assumes participants with independent standing, which agents drawn from a common model may not have — the correlated-error problem in ASSUMPTION-483 applies here and could make mutual registration a less separable alternative than it appears. (c) Goodhart cuts both ways: if convergence is already a gamed target, retaining it during the transition has a real cost that the item does not price. (d) The item's claim is a sequencing rule, and sequencing rules are structurally biased toward inertia; the literature supports "instrument the replacement first," not "retain the incumbent indefinitely," and the difference matters if no replacement is actually built. A deadline on the parallel-instrumentation period would make the rule falsifiable. (e) arXiv:2604.15647 is a preprint of unverified peer-review status.
