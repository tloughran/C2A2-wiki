    1. van der Bles, A.M., van der Linden, S., Freeman, A.L.J., Spiegelhalter, D.J. (2020). "The effects of communicating uncertainty on public trust in facts and numbers." PNAS 117(14). doi:10.1073/pnas.1913678117. PMID 32205438. — The named source, retrieved and confirmed. Five experiments including a preregistered national replication and a BBC News field experiment (total n=5,780). Communicating epistemic uncertainty produced only a small decrease in trust in the number and in the source's trustworthiness, and that decrease was concentrated in *verbal* uncertainty expressions. Numeric uncertainty reduced confidence in the figure — as it should — without meaningfully costing trust in the communicator. This is direct support for the item's operative recommendation: a stated numeric or directional bound is close to free, so the cost that would justify abstention is not there.
    2. van der Bles et al. and follow-on work on verbal vs numeric formats (Royal Society Open Science 2023, doi:10.1098/rsos.230604, named in the search targets — I located the topic area and the PNAS parent finding but did not independently retrieve the RSOS paper's text; treat that identifier as unverified by me). — The consistent direction across this programme is that numeric expression of uncertainty outperforms verbal hedging on both accuracy of belief and preservation of trust.
    3. Anchoring-and-adjustment literature: Jacowitz, K.E. & Kahneman, D. (1995). "Measures of Anchoring in Estimation Tasks." Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 21(11). doi:10.1177/01461672952111004; Epley & Gilovich, "The Anchoring-and-Adjustment Heuristic: Why the Adjustments Are Insufficient." — Theoretical grounding for the mechanism the item asserts. Once a figure has entered the record it exerts disproportionate pull on subsequent judgments and adjustment away from it is systematically insufficient. Withdrawing the caveat while leaving the anchor available is therefore predicted to leave the anchor operative — which is the substance of "republishes the figure with the caveat quietly removed."
    4. Practitioner literature on ranges vs point estimates (Search Engine Journal, "Reporting Uncertainty Without Losing Credibility"; cost-estimation anchoring guidance). — Supports the specific remedy: a range invites stakeholders to ask what actions are robust across outcomes, whereas a single figure "implies a level of exactness that often doesn't exist." Weak evidentially, but directly on the item's recommendation.

  Strength of support: Moderate (on the recommendation); Weak (on the abstention mechanism)
  Summary: The item makes two claims and they are supported unevenly. The recommendation — report a directional bound rather than abstain — is well supported by the strongest source available: van der Bles et al.'s five-experiment programme found that numerically communicated uncertainty costs almost nothing in source trust while correctly lowering confidence in the figure, which removes the usual justification for withholding. Anchoring theory supplies a coherent mechanism for the first claim: a figure already in circulation continues to anchor downstream judgment, and insufficient adjustment means that removing the qualifier without removing the number leaves most of the number's influence intact. What I did not find is any published study of the specific phenomenon the item names — abstention that *presents as* withholding while functionally re-endorsing the prior figure. The pieces are all present in the literature; the composition appears not to be.
  Caveats: (a) van der Bles's population is the general public receiving news statistics, not an internal technical record with a small expert readership; the trust dynamics may not transfer, and in an internal audit record the reputational cost of a wrong bound is borne differently. (b) The anchoring support is theoretical rather than a test of this situation; no retrieved study manipulated caveat-removal specifically. (c) I could not independently verify the RSOS 2023 identifier given in the search targets and have marked it accordingly rather than reproducing it as confirmed. (d) The item's framing ("quietly removed while appearing to withhold") imputes an effect on readers that has not been measured here; the in-house test named — inspecting the next artifact citing a connector count for reappearance of the uncaveated figure — is the correct and cheap way to settle it and is not substitutable by literature. (e) A directional bound can itself become an anchor, and the anchoring literature applies symmetrically; the remedy is not cost-free.

  NOVELTY-FLAG-ASSUMPTION-484:
    Claim element: That an abstention from restating a disputed figure functions as republication-minus-caveat — i.e. that withholding a correction is not neutral but strictly worse than restating the figure with its uncertainty attached, because the anchor persists while the qualifier does not.
    Search performed: numeric vs verbal uncertainty and source credibility; belief-consistent processing; anchoring and insufficient adjustment; correction and retraction effects.
    Result: The component findings are established (anchor persistence, insufficient adjustment, low trust cost of numeric uncertainty) but I found no study testing the composite — the asymmetry between silence and qualified restatement in a standing record. The nearest adjacent literature is on the continued-influence effect of retracted information, which concerns explicit correction rather than abstention.
    Assessment: Plausibly original as a stated principle for record-keeping systems, and cheap to test in-house. Recommend it be treated as a hypothesis with a named test rather than as a settled finding.
