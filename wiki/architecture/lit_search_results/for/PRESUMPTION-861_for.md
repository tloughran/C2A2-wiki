  Original statement: That filing a flag discharges the duty the flag names. Five same-day instances,
    including a ninth consecutive flag on an unfixed metric and a one-line fix withheld at a measured

  Reading used for this search: the FOR direction is read as support for 14b's diagnosis — that reporting
  is routinely mistaken for remedy, that this substitution is a named and studied organisational
  pathology with identified generative mechanisms, and that mature reporting regimes therefore refuse to
  treat a filed item as closed until an effectiveness condition is met. Per the dispatch, Vaughan (1996)

  Sources:
    1. Tucker, A. L. & Edmondson, A. C. (2003). "Why Hospitals Don't Learn from Failures: Organizational
       and Psychological Dynamics That Inhibit System Change." *California Management Review* 45(2),
       55–72. — The most directly on-point source, and the one that supplies the vocabulary this item
       needs. Tucker and Edmondson's distinction between *first-order* problem solving (a short-term
       patch applied on the fly, which "merely transfers the original problem to another time, person, or
       place") and *second-order* problem solving (investigating and removing the cause) is precisely the
       distinction between filing a flag and applying the one-line fix. Their central finding is a
       dynamic model in which the system reaches an "illusory equilibrium": small process failures are
       continuously absorbed, the absorption looks like competence, and the underlying defect is never
       removed. Nine consecutive flags on an unchanged metric is that equilibrium in its purest form.
    2. Rae, A. J., Provan, D. J., Weber, D. E. & Dekker, S. W. A. (2018). "Safety clutter: the
       accumulation and persistence of 'safety' work that does not contribute to operational safety."
       *Policy and Practice in Health and Safety* 16(2), 194–211. — Names the exact pathology at the
       system level: activity performed *in the name of* safety that does not make operations safer. The
       three generative mechanisms they identify — duplication, generalisation, over-specification — are
       driven by an asymmetry the item exhibits directly: it is easy and rewarded to add a safety
       activity and difficult and unrewarded to remove one. They also name the costs: opportunity cost of
       ineffective activity, cynicism and "surface compliance," and obstruction of the work itself. The
       97 item-days is an opportunity cost in exactly their sense.
    3. Meyer, J. W. & Rowan, B. (1977). "Institutionalized Organizations: Formal Structure as Myth and
       Ceremony." *American Journal of Sociology* 83(2), 340–363. — The deep theoretical grounding, and
       the reason the presumption is stable rather than merely careless. Meyer and Rowan's *decoupling*:
       formal structures are adopted because they confer legitimacy, and are then systematically
       disconnected from the technical activity they nominally govern. A flag-filing apparatus that
       confers the appearance of vigilance while remaining decoupled from the fix is the predicted, not
       the anomalous, outcome of institutionalising a reporting requirement.
    4. Banja, J. (2010). "The normalization of deviance in healthcare delivery." *Business Horizons*
       53(2), 139–148. — The requested forward step from Vaughan. Banja's contribution is the
       demonstration that egregious, *known and documented* violations of practice standards persist for
       years once normalised, in a domain with mandatory reporting and a strong safety culture. That is
       the load-bearing point for this item: normalisation survives documentation. Filing does not
       interrupt it.
    5. Corrective and preventive action (CAPA) doctrine in ISO 9001 and FDA-regulated quality systems:
       the requirement that a nonconformance may not be closed until *effectiveness verification* has
       been performed, that verification criteria be defined at the time the CAPA is opened, that
       verifying implementation is explicitly distinguished from verifying that recurrence has stopped,
       and that a failed effectiveness check reopens the CAPA rather than closing it with a note.
       (ISO 9001:2015 §10.2; FDA 21 CFR 820.100; practitioner syntheses at compliancequest.com,
       qmslearning.com, ifactoryapp.com.) [standards and regulatory practice] — This is the positive
       control the item lacks, and it is unusually explicit. The regulated world *institutionalises a
       terminating condition*: a filed item is a live obligation with a defined closure test, not a
       discharged duty. It also institutionalises the recurrence signal — a repeat is not another filing,
       it is a reopening.
    6. Alarm/alert fatigue literature: ECRI's ranking of clinical alarm hazards as the top health
       technology hazard across multiple years; Joint Commission Sentinel Event Alert 50 (2013) on
       medical device alarm safety; Cvach, M. (2012), "Monitor Alarm Fatigue: An Integrative Review,"
       *Biomedical Instrumentation & Technology* 46(4), 268–277; AHRQ, *Making Healthcare Safer III*, ch.
       on alarm fatigue. — Supplies the mechanism by which repeat filing actively destroys the value of
       the original filing. Non-actionable alarms have been measured at up to 85% of clinical alarms and
       invalid rates from 85% to 99.4%; the documented consequences are desensitisation, delayed
       response, and staff disabling the alarm. A ninth consecutive unactioned flag is not a neutral
       record — on this literature it is a contribution to the condition that makes the tenth invisible.
    7. On the terminating-condition question, negatively: I found no organisational literature that
       treats an escalation channel *without* a closure test as acceptable design. The CAPA regime,
       the aviation hazard-reporting regime, and the safety-clutter critique all impose one, from
       different directions.

  Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: 14b's diagnosis is well supported and, unusually, supported by literatures that were built
  specifically to prevent it. Tucker and Edmondson give the mechanism at the individual level — the
  first-order fix that resolves the immediate friction and leaves the cause untouched, producing an
  illusory equilibrium in which the organisation's competence at absorbing failures is what prevents it
  from removing them. Meyer and Rowan give the mechanism at the institutional level: formal reporting
  structures are adopted for legitimacy and then decouple from technical activity as a matter of course,
  which means the substitution of filing for fixing is the expected steady state of any reporting
  requirement that lacks a closure test. Rae and colleagues name the resulting accumulation directly —
  safety work that does not contribute to safety — and identify the asymmetry that drives it, which is
  the asymmetry the item exhibits: filing is cheap and adding is rewarded, while removing and fixing are
  neither. Banja, running forward from Vaughan into a domain with mandatory reporting, establishes the
  point that matters most here: normalised deviance persists through documentation, so the existence of a
  written record is not evidence that the record is doing work. Against all of this the CAPA doctrine
  stands as the designed remedy — a filed nonconformance is a live obligation, closure requires
  effectiveness verification defined in advance, verifying that a fix was implemented is explicitly not
  the same as verifying that recurrence stopped, and a failed check reopens rather than closes. On the
  question of whether repeat filing is ever correct: the aviation hazard-reporting literature (ASRS,
  which has accumulated over two million reports since 1976) shows that repetition is genuinely
  informative *when* the system is designed to aggregate — repeated low-severity reports are how weak
  signals reach statistical visibility. The distinguishing feature is not the repetition but whether
  anything downstream consumes the count. Where nothing does, the alarm-fatigue evidence indicates the
  repetition is not merely inert but corrosive of the channel.

  Caveats: The strongest sources concern human organisations with physical stakes and legal exposure;
  whether a pipeline of agents should be held to CAPA-style closure discipline is a governance question
  the literature does not answer, and this search makes no recommendation. Source 5 is standards and
  regulatory practice synthesised from practitioner documentation rather than empirical study — it
  establishes that a terminating condition is mandated, not that mandating one improves outcomes; I found
  no controlled evidence that effectiveness-verification requirements reduce recurrence, and the
  compliance literature's own complaint is that the step is "the most often skipped or superficially
  completed." That complaint cuts both ways for this item: it is further evidence that filing-as-discharge
  is the default even where a closure test formally exists. The alarm-fatigue percentages are from
  clinical monitoring and should not be read as a rate for a text pipeline; they are cited for the
  mechanism, not the magnitude. Per instruction, Vaughan (1996) was not re-searched; source 4 is the
  forward step and inherits her framework rather than independently corroborating it, so 3 and 4 are less
  than fully independent of the estate's existing return. The 97 item-days and the nine consecutive
  filings are facts about this project and are not attested by any source here. Search scope:
  good — covered organisational learning, neo-institutional decoupling, safety science, quality-system
  corrective action, alarm fatigue, and voluntary hazard reporting. Did NOT cover Morrison & Milliken on
  organisational silence, Dutton & Ashford on issue selling, or the whistleblowing literature, all of
  which bear on why a filer keeps filing rather than escalating differently, and all of which remain
  unsearched.

  Recommendation: SUPPORTED
