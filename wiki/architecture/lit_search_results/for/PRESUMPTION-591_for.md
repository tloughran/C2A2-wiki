  Sources:
    1. Manski, C.F. (1990). "Nonparametric Bounds on Treatment Effects." American Economic Review Papers & Proceedings 80(2):319-323; and Manski, C.F. (2003). *Partial Identification of Probability Distributions*. Springer. — The decisive support. Manski's programme establishes that when a quantity is not point-identified, the data alone still imply a non-empty interval computed by assigning worst-case values to the unobserved units. This is exactly the third state the amendment proposes: not "we know the value" and not "we know nothing", but "the data bound it, assumption-free". Crucially the bound is *computed*, not asserted, so the state has an operational entry criterion — which is what distinguishes resolving from renaming.
    2. Manski, C.F. (2003/2011), the "Law of Decreasing Credibility": the credibility of inference decreases with the strength of the assumptions maintained. — Supports the presumption's implicit worry in the constructive direction: a two-state discipline (valued / UNCALIBRATED) forces every item toward one of two ends of the credibility-informativeness trade-off, whereas the intermediate state is precisely where the trade-off is best sited. The literature treats this interior position as the methodologically preferred one, not as a hedge.
    3. Tamer, E. (2010). "Partial Identification in Econometrics." Annual Review of Economics 2:167-195. — A field-level review confirming that partial identification is now a mature, applied methodology with worked estimators and inference procedures, not a philosophical stance. Support for the claim that a third state can be given enough machinery to be checkable: there exist standard tests for whether an identified set is informative (i.e. strictly narrower than the logical range).
    4. Ciucci, D., Dubois, D. & Lawry, J. (2014). "Borderline vs. unknown: comparing three-valued representations of imperfect information." International Journal of Approximate Reasoning 55(9):1866-1889. — Direct support on the logic side: the paper distinguishes a genuinely third truth-value from an epistemic "unknown" over a two-valued underlying reality, and argues the two must not be conflated. This is the discriminating question for the amendment — whether the proposed third state is an epistemic marker over a still-binary quantity (in which case it is a rename) or a distinct evidential state with its own inference rules (in which case it resolves). The literature supplies the test.
    5. Der Kiureghian, A. & Ditlevsen, O. (2009). "Aleatory or epistemic? Does it matter?" Structural Safety 31(2):105-112. — Supports the distinction being action-relevant rather than cosmetic: the authors argue the aleatory/epistemic split matters exactly when it changes what one would do (epistemic uncertainty is reducible by effort; aleatory is not). Transposed: a third state earns its place if items assigned to it get a different subsequent treatment (compute the bound) from items assigned to UNCALIBRATED (do nothing until data arrive). That is a testable consequence of the amendment, and it is the one the frequency grep does not test.
    6. Graydon, P.J. & Holloway, C.M. (2017). "Reasoning About Confidence and Uncertainty in Assurance Cases: A Survey." Springer LNCS (Software Engineering for Resilient Systems / assurance-case literature). — Applied precedent in the nearest neighbouring practice: assurance and safety cases have repeatedly adopted graded confidence states over binary satisfied/not-satisfied verdicts, and the survey documents both the motivation and the recurring failure mode (labels applied without a stated rule for assignment). Support for feasibility, with the failure mode named.
    7. Embracing Uncertainty: The Value of Partial Identification in Public Health and Clinical Research (2024). PMC10799552. — An applied demonstration outside econometrics that partial-identification bounds are actionable in practice, i.e. that the intermediate state is used to make decisions rather than to defer them.

  Strength of support: Moderate-Strong

  Summary: Supported, and EXTERNALLY ANCHORED (named external referents: Manski 1990 in AER P&P and Manski 2003, Springer; Tamer 2010 in Annual Review of Economics; Ciucci, Dubois & Lawry 2014 in IJAR; Der Kiureghian & Ditlevsen 2009 in Structural Safety; Graydon & Holloway 2017 assurance-case survey). Two independent literatures — partial identification in econometrics and three-valued/epistemic representation in logic and risk analysis — both hold that an intermediate state between "valued" and "unknown" is legitimate and does non-vacuous work, PROVIDED it carries a computed entry criterion rather than a label. Partial identification supplies exactly such a criterion: an item belongs in the third state iff a worst-case bound can be computed and is strictly narrower than the logical range. That is 14b's settling quantity restated in the literature's own terms, which is the strongest form of support available for a proposed convention. Der Kiureghian & Ditlevsen supply the second half: the state earns its place only if assignment to it changes subsequent action. Together these convert 14b's rhetorical question into a two-part test the amendment can be held to.

  Caveats: (a) The support is for the *form* of the amendment, not for its yield. Cochran (1953) used worst-case bounding in survey missing-data work and abandoned it because the bounds were too conservative to be useful; the modern literature repeats the warning that no-assumptions bounds are frequently wide enough to cover zero and so to be practically uninformative. If the C2A2 partition fraction turns out near zero — i.e. almost no UNCALIBRATED item admits a non-trivial bound — the amendment is a rename after all, and this search says nothing against that outcome. The literature supports the *availability* of the third state, not its *population*. (b) Domain transfer: Manski bounds presuppose a well-defined estimand with a bounded outcome support. Several C2A2 UNCALIBRATED entries (e.g. "cost of a missing input to a day's agent attention") have no stated outcome scale, so no bound is computable until the scale is defined — and defining it is a modelling act that reintroduces assumptions. The transfer is conceptual, not mechanical. (c) The assurance-case literature is the closest applied analogue and it also documents the failure mode most relevant here: graded states adopted without an assignment rule become a third bin for whatever is inconvenient. Graydon & Holloway is cited for the pattern, not as evidence the pattern is avoided. (d) Search scope: four targeted searches across econometrics, many-valued logic, structural-safety uncertainty and assurance cases. Two literatures reached for and not exhausted: Dempster-Shafer / imprecise-probability treatments of the same interval idea, and the empirical literature on whether adding categories to a rating scale improves or degrades inter-rater reliability — the latter is the sharpest available test of 14b's "weakens or sharpens in practice" clause and was not found in a usable form this run. Moderate search scope; broader search recommended on the rating-scale-granularity question.
