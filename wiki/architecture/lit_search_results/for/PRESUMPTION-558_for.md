  Sources:
    1. NIOSH / CDC, "Hierarchy of Controls" (current guidance; the hierarchy dates to mid-20th-century industrial hygiene practice and is codified by NIOSH and OSHA). — Ranks five control levels from most to least effective: elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, PPE. Signs and warning labels are explicitly classified as *administrative* controls, and the stated rationale for the ranking is that elimination, substitution and engineering controls "control exposures without significant human interaction" whereas administrative controls leave the hazard present and only change how people work. This is a direct, named, external ranking placing a written warning at the second-least-effective tier — exactly the claim the presumption makes.
    2. Wogalter, M.S., McKenna, N.A., & Allison, S.T. (1988). "Warning Compliance: Behavioral Effects of Cost and Consensus." Proceedings of the Human Factors Society Annual Meeting. — Experimentally, behavioural compliance with warnings dropped as the cost of complying rose, and shifted up or down with the observed (non-)compliance of others. Warning efficacy is therefore contingent on cost and social context, not a property of the warning text; a bolded prohibition does not deliver a fixed compliance rate.
    3. Wogalter, M.S., Conzola, V.C., & Smith-Jackson, T.L. (2002). "Research-Based Guidelines for Warning Design and Evaluation." Applied Ergonomics 33(3):219-230; and the C-HIP (Communication-Human Information Processing) model. — C-HIP decomposes warning effectiveness into a chain: source -> channel -> delivery -> attention switch -> attention maintenance -> comprehension -> attitudes/beliefs -> motivation -> behaviour. A break at *any* stage yields zero effect. A warning on a delivery channel that has failed five consecutive times breaks the chain at the channel stage, meaning the warning has no efficacy at all rather than reduced efficacy. The same body of work finds compliance with a posted sign is significantly *lower* than the identical warning embedded in task instructions, because readers judge posted warnings as not directed at them.
    4. Norman, D.A. (1988/2013). The Design of Everyday Things, Basic Books — forcing functions: interlock, lock-in, lock-out; and Shingo's poka-yoke taxonomy (control poka-yoke vs warning poka-yoke). — The literature draws exactly the presumption's distinction: *control* poka-yokes / forcing functions make the error mechanically or electronically impossible, while *warning* poka-yokes only signal the error condition. Control devices are the recommended form wherever the error is consequential; warning devices are the fallback where control is infeasible. Removing submit capability from the defective page, or making the page unreachable, is the control-poka-yoke equivalent and is what the design literature prescribes.
    5. Leveson, N.G. (2004). "A New Accident Model for Engineering Safer Systems." Safety Science 42(4):237-270 (STAMP). — Safety is treated as a control problem: a hazard is mitigated only when a safety constraint is *enforced* by the control structure. An instruction to a human operator is a constraint whose enforcement depends on that operator receiving and acting on it; where the enforcement path is unreliable the constraint is, in STAMP terms, inadequately enforced regardless of how clearly it was written.

  Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: Strongly supported and EXTERNALLY ANCHORED (named external referents: the NIOSH/CDC Hierarchy of Controls, Wogalter's warnings-and-human-factors literature, Norman's forcing functions with Shingo's poka-yoke taxonomy, and Leveson's STAMP). The classification the presumption makes is not a rhetorical framing but the standard taxonomy: written warnings and signs *are* administrative controls, and the established ranking places them below engineering controls precisely because they leave the hazard live and depend on human interaction. The human-factors evidence adds two independently damaging findings: compliance is contingent on cost and social consensus rather than on warning clarity, and warning effect requires an unbroken delivery-to-behaviour chain, so a warning carried on a channel with five consecutive delivery failures has no effect rather than a diminished one. The design literature prescribes the specific alternative — a control poka-yoke / forcing function (make the page unreachable or non-submit-capable) — for exactly the case of a consequential, silent, irreversible error, which makes the in-house adjunct ("is the defective page still reachable and still submit-capable?") the decisive test.

  Caveats: (a) The hierarchy of controls is normative safety-engineering doctrine developed for physical/occupational hazards; its transfer to software-tool hazards is by analogy, although the analogy is now standard in software safety and the STAMP source is explicitly about software-intensive systems. (b) Warning-efficacy research measures population compliance rates; where the audience is a single, highly-motivated, technically-informed operator who authored the warning system, compliance may be far above the laboratory base rate — the human-factors evidence is weakest exactly in the single-expert-operator case. (c) The hierarchy does not say administrative controls are worthless, only that they are less effective and should not be the *sole* mitigation where a higher-tier control is feasible; the presumption's force rests on feasibility of the interlock, which the literature does not establish for this specific tool. (d) None of the sources quantifies the residual risk here. Moderate-to-good search scope: four targeted searches; the warnings literature is deep and a fuller sweep would add quantitative compliance-rate estimates.
