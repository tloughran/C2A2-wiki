    1. Zylos Research (2026-02-20), "Graceful Degradation Patterns in AI Agent Systems." — Direct support for the item's decision to decline the claim. States the governing principle: "always communicate degraded state to downstream consumers so they can calibrate their trust accordingly," and that "in AI systems, silent failures erode trust faster than explicit ones — users are willing to accept limitations but not unpredictability." Also names the specific instrumentation gap: "the fallback firing is itself an event worth tracking," which is exactly what four undetected weeks of fallback classification means was not happening.
    2. SRE School, "Comprehensive Tutorial on Graceful Degradation in Site Reliability Engineering"; Web-Alert, "Graceful Degradation: Designing Systems That Fail Well." — Establishes the distinction the item turns on. Legitimate graceful degradation "switches to a compatible fallback if the contract can stay the same, and reduces capability on purpose if the backup is weaker" — with the reduction *declared*. Silent substitution, where a weaker path serves the same interface without announcing it, is named as the anti-pattern: "always be explicit about degraded state, which is preferable to silent degradation where users may not realize they are receiving inferior outputs."
    3. "When Errors Become Narratives: A Longitudinal Taxonomy of Silent Failures in a Production LLM Agent Runtime" (arXiv:2606.14589). — The strongest empirical precedent, and close to identical in structure: a scheduled job whose primary call failed silently fell back to a degraded mechanical path that "emitted leftover container headings as if they were review content," producing output "plausible enough to pass casual inspection for weeks." Four consecutive weeks of undetected fallback classification is the same duration and the same mechanism. The paper's formulation — "a fallback path that manufactures plausible-shaped output is a hallucination implemented in shell" — supports the item's stronger claim that the fallback did not merely lose information but produced positively misleading classifications.
    4. "Ontological uncertainty and epistemic humility in an age of automation" (arXiv:2605.23614). — Supports the retraction and the declined claim as correct behaviour rather than as failure to deliver. Frames epistemic humility as "knowing what precision was earned over and what was assumed, propagating that awareness through the chain of inference rather than laundering it." An agent that declines an installed-count claim because the count is unearned is doing exactly this; the prior week's "too charitable" explanation was laundering.
    5. "Epistemological Fault Lines: Human vs. AI" (2512.19466) and the Metacognitive Integrity Index framing (via arXiv:2605.23614). — Supplies a measurement frame: abstention behaviour, calibration curves and selective accuracy are proposed as first-class evaluation targets, with governance standards naming "evidence provenance, uncertainty disclosures, abstention thresholds, audit trails." Supports treating the declined claim as a positive signal to be recorded rather than a gap.
    6. CDTS / graceful trust degradation (arXiv:2606.29484). — Four-part prescription that maps onto the item: as reliability degrades the signal should "know it (remain calibrated), show it (offer explanations), warn about it (detect degradation), and route around it." The connector-health agent did the first three; the fourth — routing around, i.e. repairing enumeration — is the item's open action.

The agent's response — declining to make an installed-count claim and retracting the prior week's over-charitable explanation — is precisely what the epistemic-humility and trust-calibration literature prescribes, and recent work proposes making abstention behaviour a measured quantity rather than treating a declined claim as a missing deliverable.
