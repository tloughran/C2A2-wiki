  Sources:
    1. Little, J.D.C. (1961). "A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = lambda*W." Operations Research 9(3):383-387; and the standard statement of Little's Law (e.g. Wikipedia, "Little's law", and CS/OR course treatments). — The law relates the mean number in system L, arrival rate lambda and mean time in system W, and holds *provided a steady state exists*. The received teaching is explicit that stability (long-run arrival rate equal to long-run departure rate) is a precondition; where the arrival rate permanently exceeds the departure rate the number in system grows without bound and the average W does not converge, so the law yields no finite W to interpret. This supports the presumption's structural point directly: the formalism presupposes a departure process. Applied to a register with departure rate identically zero, it does not report "instability" as a finding about the register — it reports that its own precondition is absent, and the substantive question (is a departure process *required*?) is smuggled in as an assumption rather than derived.
    2. Kleppmann, M. (2017). Designing Data-Intensive Applications, O'Reilly, Ch. 11 ("Stream Processing"), on the distinction between traditional message brokers (JMS/AMQP-style) and log-based brokers. — In queue semantics a message is deleted once acknowledged by a consumer, so residual depth *is* backlog and a consumer is structurally obligatory. In log semantics the store is append-only, the same records are readable by many consumers or by none, offsets rather than deletions track consumption, and records are removed by *retention policy* rather than by being consumed. Growth of a log is therefore not by itself a defect and the governing knob is retention. This is the precise queue-vs-log distinction the presumption invokes, drawn in a standard reference, and it establishes that "which semantics does this register have" is a real, consequential, and separately-specifiable design question.
    3. ISO 31000:2018, Risk management — Guidelines (and the risk-register practice literature built on it). — A risk register is a monitored record whose expected lifecycle is periodic *review*, with items legitimately remaining open across many cycles; the register's terminal states are decision-driven (treated / accepted / closed on re-assessment), not consumption-driven. The recognised failure modes of a register are absent owners and absent review cadence — i.e. staleness — rather than depth. This is an established, named instance of exactly the register type the presumption proposes as the alternative reading, in which non-zero depth is a design property and the remedy for pathology is retention/review policy.
    4. Thibodeau, P.H., & Boroditsky, L. (2011). "Metaphors We Think With: The Role of Metaphor in Reasoning." PLoS ONE 6(2):e16782. — Across ~1,500 participants, describing crime as a "beast" versus a "virus" shifted policy preferences substantially (about 75% versus 56% favouring enforcement) with identical statistics; and only ~3% of participants identified the metaphor as an influence on their reasoning, attributing their choices to the numbers instead. Direct empirical support for the presumption's meta-claim that the *name* drives the analytic frame and the metric selected, and that this influence is typically invisible to the person reasoning — calling the register a "queue" recruits queueing analysis and its stability norm before any argument that a queue is what it is.

  Summary: Supported in its conditional form, and EXTERNALLY ANCHORED (named external referents: Little 1961 in Operations Research, Kleppmann's Designing Data-Intensive Applications on queue-versus-log semantics, ISO 31000:2018 on risk-register lifecycles, and Thibodeau & Boroditsky 2011 in PLoS ONE). Little's Law is stated in the literature with an explicit steady-state precondition, so applying it to a register with zero departures is applying a formalism outside its stated scope; the resulting "instability" verdict is derived from the presupposition that a departure process is required, not from evidence that one is. The queue-versus-log distinction is a standard, well-drawn design distinction with materially different implications — in queue semantics depth is backlog and a consumer is obligatory, while in log semantics depth is expected and the governing control is retention policy — which establishes that the register's semantics is a separately-specifiable design fact rather than something the metric can determine. ISO 31000's risk register is a named external instance of the alternative reading, in which items legitimately remain open and the recognised failure mode is stale review rather than depth. Thibodeau & Boroditsky supply experimental support for the meta-claim that naming shapes which analysis is reached for and does so largely invisibly. The in-house adjunct is decisive: whether 15d's charter names a consumer and a terminal state settles which reading applies.

  Caveats: (a) This is support for a conditional, not for the alternative reading. The literature does not establish that the monitor register *is* a log or watch-list; if a consumer and a terminal state are specified anywhere in 15d's charter, the queue reading is correct and Little's Law applies properly, in which case zero drain is a genuine finding. (b) The ISO 31000 practice literature cuts partly the other way: it treats an item with no recent *review* date as a red flag, so the log/watch-list reading does not license indifference — it substitutes a review-cadence obligation for a drain obligation, and a register with zero drain *and* zero review is pathological under either reading. This is the most important limitation on the presumption's implied "zero drain is correct." (c) Little's Law is more robust than course treatments suggest — it holds for essentially any arrival and service discipline where a steady state exists — so the objection is specifically about the absent steady state, not about distributional assumptions. (d) Thibodeau & Boroditsky's effect size has been the subject of replication debate (see the 2014 PLoS ONE follow-up, "When Do Natural Language Metaphors Influence Reasoning?"), so source 4 is directional rather than firm.
