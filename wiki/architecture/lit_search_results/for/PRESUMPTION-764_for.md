  Sources:
    1. Vaughan, Diane, 1996. The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA. University of Chicago Press. — Origin of "normalization of deviance": the gradual process by which departures from a declared standard, repeated without catastrophic consequence, become the operative norm. NASA engineers progressively tolerated O-ring erosion in breach of their own engineering thresholds because prior launches under similar breach had succeeded. Establishes that a limit breached without consequence is reclassified as acceptable rather than remaining a limit.
    2. Shrives, Philip J.; Brennan, Niamh M., 2015. "A typology for exploring the quality of explanations for non-compliance with UK corporate governance regulations." The British Accounting Review 47(1): 85–99. — Assesses FTSE 350 non-compliance explanations against seven quality characteristics (location, comprehensiveness, originality/mimetic behaviour, length, complexity, specificity, attestation) across 2004/05 and 2011/12. Finds the content of explanations is itself unregulated, so the "explain" limb of comply-or-explain carries no enforced standard.
    3. Shrives, Philip J.; Brennan, Niamh M., 2017. "Explanations for corporate governance non-compliance: A rhetorical analysis." Critical Perspectives on Accounting 49: 31–56. — Finds explanations for non-compliance rely on rhetorical strategies including minimisation of negative feelings, "weasel words" that disguise the breach, and transcendence (ends justify means), and that use of these strategies *increased* between 2004/05 and 2011/12 — i.e. the disclosure regime degraded over time toward explanations "more orientated towards misleading explanations than meaningful convincing rationales."

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: The presumption is supported on two independent tracks. Vaughan supplies the mechanism: a declared limit that is breached repeatedly without adverse consequence does not persist as an unmet standard but is progressively reinterpreted as the acceptable operating range, which is precisely the condition the item describes when breach is universal. Shrives and Brennan supply the empirical case from a regime structurally analogous to a declared-and-disclosed budget: under comply-or-explain, non-compliance is permitted provided it is declared, and their two studies show that in practice the declaration limb attracts no enforced quality standard and that firms' explanations drifted measurably toward mimetic, vague and rhetorically evasive text over an eight-year window. The combination supports the item's core inference: universal declaration of breach is not evidence that the constraint is operating, and may be the artefact that allows it to stop operating while appearing intact. The reflexive note in the item — that the intake run was itself in breach — is consistent with the normalization prediction rather than anomalous to it.

  Caveats: Neither track is about a resource budget. Vaughan's case is safety thresholds under schedule pressure, where the consequence of breach is catastrophic and delayed; Shrives and Brennan concern governance-code provisions with capital-market rather than operational consequences. A compute or token budget breached routinely may be a deliberately soft target used for monitoring rather than control, in which case universal breach indicates a mis-set number rather than a dead constraint — none of the located sources distinguishes these. Normalization of deviance is also a widely-invoked construct that has attracted criticism for post-hoc applicability to almost any failure, so it should be treated as an organising frame rather than a testable finding. The 2015 and 2017 papers share both authors and much of the same sample, so they are not fully independent evidence.
