  Sources:
    1. Huang, P., Guo, C., Zhou, L., Lorch, J.R., Dang, Y., Chintalapati, M., Yao, R. (2017). "Gray Failure: The Achilles' Heel of Cloud-Scale Systems." HotOS '17. — The single most directly relevant source. Defines gray failure and formalises *differential observability*: the system's own failure detectors report health while the application is afflicted. Argues that gray failure, not fail-stop, is the failure mode behind most large-scale cloud incidents. "Scheduler logged 78 OK while seven runs produced nothing" is a textbook instance of differential observability between the invoker's view and the work's view.

    2. Huang, P., et al. (2018). "Capturing and Enhancing In Situ System Observability for Failure Detection." OSDI '18 (Panorama). — Follow-on system built on the gray-failure diagnosis. Reports detecting all 15 reproduced real-world gray failures in under 7 seconds where existing approaches detected one in under 300 seconds — quantifying how badly conventional health signals miss this class. Direct support for the claim that "no instrument reports the number" is a designed-in gap rather than an oversight.

    3. Alpern, B., Schneider, F.B. (1985). "Defining Liveness." Information Processing Letters 21: 181–185. — The formal grounding for the distinction the presumption collapses. Safety properties state that a bad thing never happens; liveness properties state that a good thing eventually happens; every property is the intersection of a safety and a liveness property, and the two require fundamentally different proof techniques (invariance vs. well-foundedness). "The scheduler did not error" is a safety-shaped observation; "the run produced its output" is a liveness property, and no accumulation of the former establishes the latter.

    4. Avizienis, A., Laprie, J.-C., Randell, B., Landwehr, C. (2004). "Basic Concepts and Taxonomy of Dependable and Secure Computing." IEEE Transactions on Dependable and Secure Computing 1(1): 11–33. — The canonical fault/error/failure taxonomy, including the signalled vs. *unsignalled* failure distinction and the fault-forecasting/fault-removal means. Provides the vocabulary in which "produced nothing and reported OK" is an unsignalled (silent) failure rather than an absence of failure, and locates availability as an attribute distinct from correctness of service.

    5. Yuan, D., Luo, Y., Zhuang, X., Rodrigues, G.R., Zhao, X., Zhang, Y., Jain, P.U., Stumm, M. (2014). "Simple Testing Can Prevent Most Critical Failures: An Analysis of Production Failures in Distributed Data-Intensive Systems." OSDI '14. — 198 randomly sampled production failures across Cassandra, HBase, HDFS, Hadoop MapReduce and Redis. Finds that almost all catastrophic failures result from *incorrect handling of non-fatal errors explicitly signalled in software*, with 35% caused by trivial error-handling mistakes. Supports the mechanism by which a run can be swallowed and still be reported as OK.

    6. Hochschild, P.H., Turner, P., Mogul, J.C., et al. (2021). "Cores that don't count." HotOS '21; and Dixit, H.D., et al. (2021). "Silent Data Corruptions at Scale." arXiv:2102.11245 (Facebook/Meta). — Independent Google and Meta fleet studies of silent corrupt execution. Both report that the incidence of silent failure is "much higher than software engineers expect" and that it is "not captured by error reporting mechanisms within a CPU and hence not traceable at the hardware level." Establishes the general prior that silent-failure rates are systematically underestimated whenever the only instrument is the component's own success report — which is the shape of the 78-OK log.

    7. "SDL Design Patterns and Components — Watchdog and Heartbeat" (2004). Technical Report, University of Kaiserslautern. [unverified — author names not confirmed] — Presents watchdog and heartbeat as reusable design patterns specifically for detecting *fail-silent* nodes, i.e. nodes that stop producing without announcing it. The classical engineering answer to exactly this presumption.

    8. Practitioner "dead man's switch" / cron-monitoring literature (multiple independent sources, e.g. crontap, OnlineOrNot, UpDog, Dead Man's Snitch write-ups). — Converges on a single formulation directly restating the presumption: "traditional monitoring alerts on the presence of errors, not the absence of success signals — the absence of an error is not the same as the presence of success," and identifies jobs that never run (or run and silently produce nothing) as the failure mode all other monitoring misses. Non-peer-reviewed, but strikingly uniform across independent sources and operationally mature.

  Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: This presumption is not merely supported by literature — it is the subject of a named research programme. Huang et al.'s gray-failure work formalises the exact gap as *differential observability*: the failure detector's view diverging from the application's view, which they argue is the dominant failure mode in cloud-scale systems and which their Panorama follow-up shows conventional health signals miss by two orders of magnitude in detection latency. Formal methods supplies the reason the gap is structural rather than accidental: "the scheduler did not error" and "the run produced output" are safety- and liveness-shaped claims respectively, and Alpern and Schneider establish that these require different proof obligations, so no volume of the former entails the latter. The dependability taxonomy of Avizienis et al. names the resulting state — unsignalled failure — and distinguishes availability from correctness of delivered service, which is precisely the distinction "78 OK vs. seven mute runs" collapses. Empirically, the Google and Meta silent-corruption fleet studies both report that silent-failure incidence runs far higher than engineers expect wherever the only instrument is a component's own success report, and Yuan et al.'s production-failure corpus supplies the mechanism by which a swallowed error still yields an OK. Finally, the engineering response is settled and old: watchdog/heartbeat patterns exist specifically to detect fail-silent nodes, and operational practice has independently converged on the dead-man's-switch formulation, "the absence of an error is not the same as the presence of success."

  Caveats: (a) The strongest sources concern distributed infrastructure and hardware, not agent pipelines; the transfer is conceptual and the base rates do not carry over. (b) Alpern–Schneider is a formal result about properties of infinite executions, not an empirical claim about monitoring practice; using it to argue about a scheduler log is an analogy, though a tight one. (c) The dead-man's-switch material is vendor and blog literature, not measurement; it establishes that the pattern is recognised and that the framing is standard, not that 7/29 is a typical mute rate. (d) The literature supports the *presumption's falsity in general* (firing ≠ running) very strongly, but supplies no benchmark against which a 24% mute rate can be judged high or low, and no guidance on what mute rate should trigger action. (e) Source 7's authorship was not confirmed. (f) One asymmetry worth flagging for 15b: the same literature that supports "you cannot infer running from firing" also argues that adding detectors has costs and false-positive rates of its own; the FOR case here establishes the gap exists, not that instrumenting it is net-positive.
