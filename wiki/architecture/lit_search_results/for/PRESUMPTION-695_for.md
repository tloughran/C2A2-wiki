  Sources:
    1. Jergas, H. & Baethge, C., 2015. "Quotation accuracy in medical journal
       articles — a systematic review and meta-analysis." PeerJ 3:e1364 (DOI
       10.7717/peerj.1364). — The single most on-point source, and it denies
       the presumption with numbers. From 559 studies screened and 28 included,
       the pooled total quotation error rate is 25.4% (95% CI 19.5-32.4), of
       which major errors are 11.9% (8.4-16.6) and minor 11.5% (8.3-15.7).
       Quotation accuracy is defined as whether the cited source actually
       supports the citing author's statement. A quarter of object-level
       assertions failing that test is not a residual risk that can be left
       unaudited while attention goes to process.
    2. Mogull, S.A., 2017. "Accuracy of cited 'facts' in medical research
       articles: A review of study methodology and recalculation of quotation
       error rate." PLOS ONE 12(9): e0184727. — The methodologically stricter
       recalculation, and the more damaging finding for this item. Mogull
       revises the headline rate down to 14.5% (10.5-18.6) but shows that
       64.8% (56.1-73.5) of content errors are *major* — cases where the
       referenced source fails to substantiate, is unrelated to, or contradicts
       the assertion. Separately, improper secondary or indirect citation runs
       at 10.4% (3.4-17.5). That last figure is exactly the item's disclosed
       route: taking a claim from a publisher's description or a host's
       write-up rather than the source. It is a measured, named error class
       with a rate an order of magnitude above zero.
    3. Horton, J., Vandermeer, B., Hartling, L., Tjosvold, L., Klassen, T.P. &
       Buscemi, N., 2010. "Systematic review data extraction: cross-sectional
       study showed that experience did not increase accuracy." Journal of
       Clinical Epidemiology. — Removes the most likely defence, which is that
       a competent extractor does not need checking. Error rates ranged from
       28.3% to 31.2% across minimal, moderate and substantial experience
       levels, with omission errors at 11.3-16.4% and inaccuracy errors at
       13.9-17.9%. Experience did not improve accuracy. The control that works
       is structural duplication, not skill.
    4. Buscemi, N., Hartling, L., Vandermeer, B., Tjosvold, L. & Klassen, T.P.,
       2006. "Single data extraction generated more errors than double data
       extraction in systematic reviews." Journal of Clinical Epidemiology
       59(7): 697-703. [Volume/pages from established knowledge; title,
       authors and journal confirmed this session] — States the remedy the
       item's route omits. The field's standing recommendation, repeated across
       every methodological guide located, is that at least two reviewers
       independently extract data. Single extraction — which is what a
       disclosed-but-unaudited route is — is the condition measured to produce
       more errors.
    5. Wang, Z., Nayfeh, T., Tetzlaff, J., O'Blenis, P. & Murad, M.H., 2020.
       "Error rates of human reviewers during abstract screening in systematic
       reviews." PLOS ONE 15(1): e0227742. — Quantifies the abstract-only step
       specifically, over 139,467 citations and 329,332 decisions by 86
       reviewers. Total error rate (false inclusion plus false exclusion) was
       10.76% (95% CI 7.43-14.09). The authors' conclusion is the relevant one
       for this item: when judging automated selection, remember the human gold
       standard is not perfect. Decisions taken from abstracts and descriptions
       alone carry a measured, non-trivial error rate before any content
       extraction begins.
    6. Product versus process versus system audit taxonomy (quality-assurance
       practitioner literature, consistent across many independent sources this
       session; ASQ/IATF-lineage material, no peer-reviewed source located).
       — Addresses the process/product asymmetry the item names, and refuses
       the trade. A process audit measures conformance to the defined process
       and the adequacy of process controls; a product audit measures the
       finished output against design specification and requirements. The
       consistent framing is that both are required and that neither substitutes
       for the other: product inspection does not catch the process defects
       producing errors, and process conformance does not establish product
       quality. There is a defensible reason to weight process — process
       defects are systematic while product defects are sampled — but no
       located source supports weighting product to zero.

  Summary: No literature was found supporting the view that object-level
    content needs no auditing, and evidence synthesis — the field that has
    measured this most carefully — reports error rates on exactly the route
    this item describes. Quotation error meta-analyses put total error at 14.5%
    to 25.4% depending on method, with most content errors classed as major,
    meaning the cited source fails to substantiate or contradicts the claim
    made from it; improper secondary citation, which is the publisher-blurb and
    host-write-up route named here, is separately measured at around 10%. Data
    extraction error rates of 28-31% do not fall with reviewer experience, so
    the only control shown to work is independent duplication, which a
    disclosed-but-unaudited route by definition lacks. Abstract-only screening
    carries a further ~10.8% total error before extraction begins. The
    quality-assurance taxonomy addresses the item's process-versus-product
    asymmetry directly and holds that the two audits answer different questions
    and neither substitutes for the other. On the item's composition point —
    ~1,460 items about the machinery against comparatively few about the
    content — the literature's implication is uncomfortable and specific: the
    measured error rates apply to the under-audited side, so the register's
    shape concentrates scrutiny where errors are cheapest and withholds it
    where they are dearest.

  Caveats: The strongest sources are from medical evidence synthesis, where
    extraction targets are structured numerical outcomes and error is
    unusually well defined; a "PRS triplet" taken from a publisher's
    description may be a coarser object for which these rates are neither
    upper nor lower bounds but simply not calibrated. Transfer is therefore by
    analogy on rate, though the direction and the mechanism transfer cleanly.
    The item's own framing contains a mitigation the literature would credit:
    the route was *disclosed*. Disclosed provenance is not audit, but it is the
    precondition for one and it is substantially better than the undisclosed

    counterargument this file could not refute: process defects are systematic

    extreme. No source was located that quantifies an appropriate
    process-to-product audit ratio, so the ~1,460-to-few figure cannot be
    scored against a benchmark — only its direction can. Source 4's volume and
    pages are from established knowledge; source 6 is practitioner-only.
