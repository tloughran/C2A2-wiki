  Sources:
    1. Peerally, M.F., Carr, S., Waring, J., Dixon-Woods, M. (2017). "The problem with root cause analysis." BMJ Quality & Safety 26(5): 417–422. — Argues that RCA as practised produces causal narratives whose validity is rarely tested, that the singular-cause framing is often unwarranted, and that political and procedural pressures push investigations toward closure on the first defensible cause. The core support for "a recorded cause is a claim of a different epistemic kind than a recorded number, and is treated as though it weren't."

    2. Kellogg, K.M., et al. (2017). "Our current approach to root cause analysis: is it contributing to our failure to improve patient safety?" BMJ Quality & Safety 26(5): 381–387. — Empirical companion piece; reviews RCA outputs and finds recorded causes frequently yield weak or non-durable remediations and that the same events recur under the same recorded explanations. Establishes non-durability of recorded causes as measured rather than merely argued.

    3. Howard, J. (2019). "Premature Closure: Anchoring Bias, Occam's Error, Availability Bias, Search Satisficing, Yin-Yang Error, Diagnosis Momentum, Triage Cueing, and Unpacking Failure." In: *Cognitive Errors and Diagnostic Mistakes: A Case-Based Guide to Critical Thinking in Medicine*, Springer. — Names *diagnosis momentum* explicitly: a diagnostic label, once attached, gains momentum through repetition until it is treated as established fact. Also documents that "when the diagnosis is made, the thinking stops," and that electronic records exacerbate this because incorrect diagnoses "are often propagated in many notes and become increasingly entrenched as correct." This is the precise mechanism by which a recorded cause acquires the standing of a recorded measurement.

    4. Hsiao, T.-K., Schneider, J. (2021). "Continued use of retracted papers: Temporal trends in citations and (lack of) awareness of retractions shown in citation contexts in biomedicine." Quantitative Science Studies 2(4): 1144–1169. — Measures the survival of a claim after its formal overturning: 94.6% of post-retraction citation contexts show no awareness that the cited paper was retracted. The nearest available quantification of the "half-life of a retracted claim," and it is long. Supports the inference that overturning a cause in one place does not remove it from the records that carry it.

    5. Bar-Ilan, J., Halevi, G. and related scientometric work; see also "Why do some retracted articles continue to get cited?" Scientometrics (2024), doi:10.1007/s11192-024-05147-4, and "On the shoulders of fallen giants: What do references to retracted research tell us about citation behaviors?" Quantitative Science Studies 5(1) (2024). [author lists not individually verified — cite by title/venue] — Reinforce source 4: post-retraction citation is common, rarely critical, and disproportionately comes from readers unfamiliar with the retraction. Retraction is described in this literature as "an inadequate measure for eliminating invalid knowledge."

    6. W3C (2013). *PROV-DM: The PROV Data Model* and *PROV-O: The PROV Ontology*, W3C Recommendations (Provenance Working Group; PROV-DM edited by L. Moreau and P. Missier). — The standards answer to the format problem the presumption identifies. PROV distinguishes an Entity generated by an Activity (`wasGeneratedBy`) from one *derived* from another entity (`wasDerivedFrom`), where derivation "infers the existence of an activity" that may not be explicitly stated, and separately types attribution (`wasAttributedTo`) and association (`wasAssociatedWith`). It also formalises which further assertions are *inferred* rather than asserted. Establishes that the distinction between observation and inference is standard, expressible, and routinely omitted by ad-hoc record formats.

    7. ECRI / Partnership for Health IT Patient Safety. "Copy/Paste: Prevalence, Problems, and Best Practices" (literature review). — Documents that 66–90% of clinicians routinely copy-forward, that one JAMA study found over 80% of note text copied or imported, and that once an erroneous diagnosis enters a problem list "a busy practitioner might assume others have verified the diagnosis and simply cop[y] the list into their own note." The concrete propagation mechanism behind the durability the presumption flags.

    8. Parasuraman, R., Manzey, D.H. (2010). "Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation: An Attentional Integration." Human Factors 52(3): 381–410. — Supplies the general result that automated or system-supplied assertions are accepted heuristically in place of vigilant verification, in experts as well as novices. Explains why an inferred cause sitting in the same field format as a measured figure will be read with the same confidence as the figure.

  Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: The presumption identifies a real and well-documented epistemic conflation. The patient-safety literature attacks it head-on: Peerally et al. argue that recorded root causes are causal narratives whose validity is rarely tested and whose recording is driven partly by pressure to close, and Kellogg et al. show empirically that these recorded causes do not hold — the same events recur under the same explanations. The cognitive-error literature supplies the mechanism and even the name: *diagnosis momentum*, whereby a label gains standing through repetition, with the explicit finding that electronic records make this worse because propagated incorrect diagnoses "become increasingly entrenched as correct." Scientometrics supplies the closest thing to a measured half-life for an overturned claim, and it is unflattering: 94.6% of post-retraction citations show no awareness of the retraction, and the field's own summary is that retraction is inadequate for removing invalid knowledge from circulation. Finally, the W3C PROV recommendations show that the format-level fix the presumption implies — typing an assertion as generated-by-observation versus derived-by-inference, and marking which assertions are themselves inferred — is not novel but a standardised modelling requirement that ad-hoc registers routinely skip. Four causes overturned in a single day is exactly the frequency pattern one would predict if causes were being recorded with the durability affordances of measurements.

  Caveats: (a) The strongest evidence is clinical and scientometric; neither domain studies machine-generated diagnostic registers, and the propagation mechanisms differ (a clinician assuming prior verification vs. an agent re-reading a field). (b) The retraction-citation figures measure survival of a claim in a *distributed, uncoordinated* citation network; a single register under one system's control is far more correctable, so those numbers are an upper bound on the analogous problem here, not an estimate of it. (c) PROV is a modelling standard, not evidence that failing to distinguish observation from inference causes harm — it establishes feasibility and convention, not effect. (d) The counting is asymmetric in a way the FOR case cannot resolve: "four causes overturned, zero measurements overturned" is also consistent with there simply being more causes than measurements, or with causes being *easier* to check and therefore corrected faster — which would invert the reading. This is the most important weakness in the FOR case and should be flagged to 15b. (e) Source 5's author attributions were not individually verified.
