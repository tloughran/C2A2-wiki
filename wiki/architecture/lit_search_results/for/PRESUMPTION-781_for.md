    1. Meyer, J.W. & Rowan, B., 1977. "Institutionalized Organizations: Formal Structure as Myth and Ceremony." American Journal of Sociology 83(2):340-363. — The deepest and most direct theoretical grounding available, and it is stronger than the item claims. Organisations adopt formal structures to secure legitimacy, and then DECOUPLE those structures from actual activity: "integration is avoided, program implementation is neglected, and inspection and evaluation is ceremonialized." Decoupling is not deviance in this account; it is the predicted equilibrium, because tight coupling of formal structure to activity generates conflict and threatens legitimacy. Applied to C2A2: a register that is written to and not read is not a broken register, it is the modal outcome for a formal structure with no enforcement coupling.   Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: The corrective proposition is strongly supported, and the strongest sources go further than the item does. Meyer and Rowan's account makes decoupling of formal structure from operating activity the EXPECTED state rather than a failure — organisations adopt written structures for legitimacy and then avoid integrating them, because integration is what creates conflict — which reframes C2A2's re-invention of PREMISE-107 and PREMISE-110 as the normal behaviour of an unenforced register rather than as an oversight by any particular agent. Walsh and Ungson locate the binding constraint at retrieval rather than storage, and the lessons-learned literature has spent twenty-five years confirming it: the repositories get written, they do not get consulted, and the field's own conclusion is that the fix must be delivery into the decision moment, not a better repository. The Ontario checklist study supplies the hard empirical case that a mandated written control can be fully adopted on paper and produce no measurable effect, in a setting where nobody had an incentive for it to fail. And the documentation-use tradition supplies the mechanism at the individual level: readers arrive with a question and scan, so a register can only answer an agent that already suspects it holds the answer — which is precisely the case a minting agent is not in. The convergent conclusion is that a record with no named reader and no forcing function is inert by default, and that C2A2's register is currently of that kind.

  Caveats: (a) DOMAIN TRANSFER. Meyer and Rowan theorise human organisations with legitimacy interests; C2A2's agents have no legitimacy motive and do not decouple strategically. The transfer holds at the level of MECHANISM (a structure with no coupling to activity does not constrain activity) and not at the level of MOTIVE, and the item should not be read as attributing ceremonial behaviour to the agents. (b) The Ontario null has a live alternative explanation, stated by the WHO programme's own leadership and by the follow-up literature — that implementation was botched, not that checklists do not work. That reading actually strengthens the item (the artefact was present; the practice was not) but it does mean the study cannot be cited as evidence that written controls are useless in general. (c) The documentation-reading literature is more equivocal than its slogan: the located re-examination finds substantial documentation use, and around a third of developers report frequently consulting docs. What survives cleanly is selectivity, not non-use. (d) None of this establishes the counterfactual — that the pipeline WOULD have avoided re-inventing 107 and 110 had the minting agents been directed to read the register. That is testable inside C2A2 (direct one agent to grep the register and measure re-invention) and would be far better evidence than anything here.
