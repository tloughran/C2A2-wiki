  Sources:
    1. Goedel, K. (1931), second incompleteness theorem; see Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Goedel's Incompleteness Theorems." — No consistent formal system containing Peano arithmetic can prove its own consistency. The structural point the presumption relies on: a system's verdict about its own reliability is not obtainable from inside the system, and this is a theorem, not a caution. Critically for the presumption, the theorem constrains *self-endorsement*; it does not supply a licence for self-abstention, so it does not by itself make abstention the safe move.
    2. Yudkowsky, E., & Herreshoff, M. (2013). "Tiling Agents for Self-Modifying AI, and the Loebian Obstacle." MIRI technical report. — Formalises the Loebian obstacle: by Loeb's theorem, an agent that trusts its own soundness over a class of sentences thereby proves those sentences, so a straightforwardly-constructed agent cannot generically trust itself or its successors. The paper's response is not abstention but the construction of an explicit, restricted, externally-specified trust schema — i.e. the literature's remedy is a stated rule for what would license adoption, which is exactly what the in-house adjunct asks for ("what rule would ever move MONITOR-486 out of MONITOR?").
    3. Chow, C.K. (1970). "On Optimum Recognition Error and Reject Tradeoff." IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 16(1):41-46; see also Franc, Prusa & Voracek (2023), "Optimal Strategies for Reject Option Classifiers," JMLR 24. — The foundational treatment of abstention shows it is modelled as a *third action with its own cost*, and the optimal policy abstains only when the expected risk of deciding exceeds the reject cost. Abstention is therefore inside the decision problem, not outside it: it does not evade the loss function, it selects a different term of it. This is the formal version of the presumption's central move.
    4. Friedman, J. (2017). "Why Suspend Judging?" Nous 51(2):302-326; and Friedman, "Suspended Judgment," Philosophical Studies. — Argues that suspension of judgement is a genuine, committal doxastic attitude distinct from merely lacking a belief, with its own normative profile; one does not enter suspension merely by declining to answer. Independent philosophical support for "abstention is a position, not the absence of one" — so a refusal issued by the system under scrutiny is itself an output of that system requiring the same warrant.
    5. Nemo iudex in causa sua (Roman law; crystallised by Coke, 17th c.; a cardinal rule of natural justice). — The remedy prescribed for a decision-maker with an interest in the outcome is *recusal with transfer to a different decision-maker*, not termination of the matter. The rule specifically forecloses the outcome the presumption identifies as pathological: an issue rendered permanently unadjudicable because the only available adjudicator is disqualified.
    6. AI-governance escalation designs, e.g. Raji, Xu, Honigsberg & Ho (2022), "Outsider Oversight: Designing a Third Party Audit Ecosystem for AI Governance," AIES 2022 / arXiv:2206.04737; and the runtime-governance literature specifying escalation to a higher-authority adjudicator (human or differently-authorised agent) when an action is irreversible or the agent's own reasoning yields genuine uncertainty about permissibility. — The standard design pattern for an agent that cannot adjudicate a question is a *specified escalation path with a named external arbiter*, not indefinite self-suspension.

Three independent literatures converge on the presumption's core: the reject-option formalism treats abstention as an action with its own cost inside the same decision problem rather than an exit from it; the epistemology of suspension treats it as a committal attitude requiring its own warrant rather than the null state; and self-reference results constrain self-*endorsement* specifically, supplying no symmetric licence for self-abstention — so the asymmetry 15c relies on is not established by the theorems it gestures at. The institutional and AI-governance literatures reinforce the presumption's second and sharper point: where the only available adjudicator is disqualified, the prescribed response is recusal-with-transfer or escalation to a named external arbiter, and a design in which a question about the system's own reliability becomes permanently unadjudicable is recognised as a failure of the governance design rather than an acceptable outcome.

  Caveats: (a) The strongest reading of the presumption — that the refusal and an incorporation are epistemically *equivalent* because both are outputs of the same model — overreaches: the reject-option literature makes abstention a distinct action with a distinct (often lower) cost, so establishing that abstention is inside the decision problem does not establish that it is no better than adoption. The literature supports "abstention is consequential and requires warrant," not "abstention is as bad as adoption." (b) Goedel/Loeb results concern formal proof systems; their application to an LLM pipeline is analogical, and the pipeline is not a formal system in the relevant sense — the analogy is standard in the alignment literature but is not a theorem about this system. (c) Friedman's committal reading of suspension is contested in current epistemology (see the "suspension as a mood" line of work), so source 4 is support from a live debate rather than settled ground. (d) "Permanently unadoptable by construction" is a claim about the pipeline's actual disposition rules and is only supported here in the conditional form: *if* no rule specifies what would discharge the abstention, then the item is unadoptable by construction — which makes the in-house design question decisive.
