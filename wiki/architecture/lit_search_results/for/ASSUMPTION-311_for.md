  Sources:
    1. Wimsatt, William C. 2007. Re-Engineering Philosophy for Limited Beings: Piecewise Approximations to Reality. Harvard University Press. — Wimsatt defines robustness as things being "accessible (detectable, measurable, derivable, definable, producible, or the like) in a variety of independent ways." Robustness increases with the number of independent derivation routes, and justifies ontological and epistemic commitments. Multiple-starting-point derivability maps directly onto Wimsatt's core criterion for robust, credible results. This is the central theoretical warrant for the assumption's inferential structure.

    2. Weisberg, Michael. 2006. "Robustness Analysis." Philosophy of Science 73(5): 730–742. — Weisberg defines robustness analysis as the search for predictions common to several independent models. The methodology — often called "triangulation" — involves identifying sufficiently diverse models that converge on a common result and investigating whether they share a robust property. This directly supports treating multi-framework derivability as convergence evidence, and establishes the practice as methodologically reputable in philosophy of science.

    3. Whewell, William. 1840/1847. The Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences, Founded upon Their History. London: John W. Parker. — Whewell coined "consilience of inductions" to describe the epistemic gain when an induction obtained from one class of facts coincides with an induction from a different class: "Consilience is a test of the truth of the Theory in which it occurs." This is the classical precursor to modern robustness arguments and directly supports the convergence-evidence claim.

    4. Campbell, Donald T., and Donald W. Fiske. 1959. "Convergent and Discriminant Validation by the Multitrait-Multimethod Matrix." Psychological Bulletin 56(2): 81–105. — Campbell and Fiske introduced convergent validity as the degree to which theoretically related constructs are interrelated across independent measurement methods. When independent theoretical frameworks converge on a common result, this counts as convergent evidence in Campbell and Fiske's sense. The MTMM framework provides formal measurement-theoretic grounding for the convergence-evidence claim in the assumption.

    5. Levins, Richard. 1966. "The Strategy of Model Building in Population Biology." American Scientist 54(4): 421–431. — Levins introduced the notion that results robust across models built with different simplifying assumptions carry special epistemic weight. Wimsatt extended this; Weisberg refined it. Together they form the canonical lineage of multi-derivation robustness reasoning that underpins the assumption.
