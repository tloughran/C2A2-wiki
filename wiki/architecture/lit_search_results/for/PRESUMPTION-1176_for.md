  Sources:
    1. Vaughan, D., 1996. "The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA." University of Chicago Press. [snippet only] (secondary summaries in Flight Safety Australia, Times Higher Education and psychsafety.com; book not read). Vaughan defines normalization of deviance as the gradual process through which unacceptable practice becomes acceptable. In the secondary account, each lower-temperature launch was judged "not sufficiently different" from earlier successful flights, so prior acceptances served as the precedent for the next. The anomaly was seen and recorded but absorbed into the frame. Relevance: this is the closest theoretical analogue. Precedent-licensed relaxation ("team meetings were approved before") is the same comparison-to-last-acceptance logic, and the deviations were noted, not hidden, as with free-text disclosure. Domain transfer from engineering safety to corpus curation is by analogy only.
    2. Dekker, S., 2011. "Drift into Failure." Ashgate/Routledge. [snippet only] (book notes, a synthesis article and the publisher blurb; book not read). Drift proceeds in small, "locally rational" steps, each too minor to trigger an alarm. No one has a whole-system view, and per the synthesis it can occur without anyone breaking a rule. Relevance: this is theoretical grounding for the "without anyone deciding it should" clause. Each admission is locally justified, and the cumulative change has no owner.
    3. Hu, K.Y., Zhang, T., Zhang, W.Y., Kwong, J.S., Ma, Y.X., Ma, B. and Yang, K.H., 2019. "Inconsistencies are common between non-Cochrane systematic reviews and their protocols registered in PROSPERO." Cochrane Colloquium Santiago, poster abstract. [fetched/read in full — abstract page]. All 43 of 43 reviews (100%) changed from their registered protocol, and 81% had at least one section completely changed. "Changing inclusion criteria/exclusion criteria" is listed among changes with "a potential risk of introducing bias." The authors recommend appendices that document "the changes and reasons between the protocols and the full text." Relevance: this is direct empirical precedent that inclusion criteria in curated evidence bases routinely shift after the fact. The remedy offered is a structured change record, which supports the item's implied fix.
    4. Cochrane Methodology Review protocol MR000053, "Comparison of protocols and registry entries to published reports for systematic reviews." Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (authors not shown on the fetched page; year not confirmed). [fetched/read in full — protocol text]. Names "inclusion criteria bias" (changing criteria once candidate studies are known) and "selector bias" (unspecific criteria leaving the reviewer "free to choose", Felson 1992). Cites PRISMA 2009 that changes "should be fully reported" with a rationale, and AMSTAR 2 and ROBIS, which assess whether departures were explained. Relevance: two points. First, a catch-all clause is the textbook case of selector bias. Second, it shows that the field's standard response is to report deviations against a fixed, versioned protocol, not to list ad hoc exceptions.
    5. Page, M.J. et al., 2021. PRISMA 2020 statement, item 24c. [snippet only] (Northwestern Galter library summary and the UCL development paper; statement not read). Authors must "describe any amendments to information provided at registration or in the protocol." Relevance: this is normative support that amendments to eligibility should be recorded against the protocol. Item 24c itself allows the description to be narrative, so the sources do not show that free-text disclosure is insufficient.
    6. Archival collection-development policies (Weill Cornell Medicine Archives; Lancashire Archives; M&S Company Archive, 2023). [snippet only]. These policies keep written exceptions sections, and some exceptions persist because of "special collection agreements made in previous years". Lancashire allows its manager to accept out-of-scope material that is "at risk" and requires appraisal decisions to be "recorded in [the] collections management system". The policies are reviewed annually. Relevance: weak, practice-level support that precedent-based exceptions persist after scope changes and that structured recording plus periodic review is the profession's guard. No scholarly study of undocumented-exception drift was found.
    7. Qualitative codebook practice guides (Lumivero; Virginia Tech team-coding wiki; Atlas audit-trail guide). [snippet only, practitioner sources, not peer-reviewed]. A versioned codebook "reduces drift in interpretation". Recommended practice is to revise the codebook itself when edge cases arise and to log the change in the audit trail. Relevance: weak analogous support that edge-case rulings belong in the criteria document, not only in case notes. The search did not confirm the term "definitional drift" or attribute it to Miles and Huberman.

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: The literature broadly supports the concern. Normalization of deviance (Vaughan 1996) and drift into failure (Dekker 2011) describe the mechanism the item names: acceptance is judged against the last accepted case, each step is locally justified, and the operative standard moves with no one deciding it should. Both were read here only through secondary summaries. Systematic-review methodology gives empirical precedent in an evidence-curation setting. Registered protocols and published reviews diverge almost universally, including on inclusion criteria (Hu et al. 2019, abstract read in full). Methods bodies name the resulting risks (inclusion-criteria bias and selector bias from loose criteria such as catch-all clauses) and prescribe documenting changes against a fixed protocol (MR000053, read in full; PRISMA 2020 item 24c). On the sub-question, Vaughan's case suggests that disclosure alone does not stop normalization, because the deviations there were recorded yet still absorbed. Field practice consistently places the guard in a versioned criteria record with periodic review. Direct evidence that free-text disclosure accelerates drift, rather than merely failing to prevent it, was not found.

  Caveats:
    - Vaughan and Dekker were read only via secondary summaries. Their claims are about safety-critical organisations under production pressure, and C2A2 curation has no comparable pressure or catastrophic feedback, so transfer is by analogy.
    - PRISMA 24c permits narrative description of amendments. Free-text disclosure does meet current reporting standards, provided it is tied to a named protocol item. The sources support "record against the criterion", not "free text is inadequate" as such.
    - Admitting a thinker's team material may be a legitimate, deliberate scope decision. The literature supports the risk of an undecided change, not the claim that every relaxation is a drift.
    - The archival and codebook evidence is practitioner or policy-level and snippet-only, not empirical.
    - No source was found that measures whether free-text exception notes are later aggregated or audited. That is the crux of "prevent vs accelerate".

  Recommendation: PARTIALLY-SUPPORTED
