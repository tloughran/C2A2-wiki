  Sources:
    1. Fischhoff, B., Slovic, P., & Lichtenstein, S. (1977). "Knowing with Certainty: The Appropriateness of Extreme Confidence." Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance 3(4):552-564. — The founding calibration studies: subjects answered large batteries of general-knowledge items and stated the probability their answer was correct. Stated confidence systematically exceeded accuracy, and the miscalibration was worst at the extremes and on hard items (the hard-easy effect). The load-bearing methodological point for this presumption is the one the literature establishes structurally: calibration cannot be read off a single self-assigned label at all. It is a property recoverable only by comparing many stated confidences against many outcomes scored by an external key. A lone "Speculative" tag on one item therefore carries no calibration information whatsoever — not because the labeller is unreliable, but because a single self-report has no calibration curve to sit on. This is direct external support for "labelling is not verifying."
    2. Dunning, D., Heath, C., & Suls, J.M. (2004). "Flawed Self-Assessment: Implications for Health, Education, and the Workplace." Psychological Science in the Public Interest 5(3):69-106. — A commissioned PSPI review synthesising the self-assessment literature across health, education and workplace domains. Its headline finding is that self-views bear only a tenuous-to-modest relation to actual behaviour and performance, with self-rating/performance correlations characterised as moderate to meagre. This supports the presumption's central structural claim rather than a claim about any particular labeller: an assessment emitted by the same process that produced the work is not an independent measurement of that work, and the review documents that the correlation is weak enough that substituting self-assessment for external assessment loses most of the signal. Where the review finds self-assessment useful, it is as a supplement to external checks, never as a replacement.
    3. Groot, T., & Valdenegro-Toro, M. (2024). "Overconfidence is Key: Verbalized Uncertainty Evaluation in Large Language and Vision-Language Models." Proceedings of the 4th Workshop on Trustworthy Natural Language Processing (TrustNLP 2024), ACL Anthology 2024.trustnlp-1.13; arXiv:2405.02917. — Evaluates prompted, verbalized self-confidence in GPT-4, GPT-3.5, LLaMA-2, PaLM 2, GPT-4V and Gemini Pro Vision, introducing a Net Calibration Error to measure the *direction* of miscalibration. Finding: high calibration error and overconfidence in the majority of cases, across models and modalities. This is the closest available external referent to C2A2's actual situation — a self-assigned confidence label emitted by a language model about its own output — and it reports that such labels are systematically miscalibrated in the optimistic direction. Corroborated by a broader 2026 clinical evaluation across 48 models reporting poor self-estimation of certainty regardless of underlying accuracy.
    4. Cain, D.M., Loewenstein, G., & Moore, D.A. (2005). "The Dirt on Coming Clean: Perverse Effects of Disclosing Conflicts of Interest." Journal of Legal Studies 34(1):1-25. — The canonical experimental result on whether a disclosure label changes downstream use of compromised evidence. Two effects: recipients *insufficiently discount* advice even when the conflict is honestly disclosed, and disclosure can *increase* the bias in the advice because the discloser feels morally licensed to overreach, having warned. The transfer to this presumption is close: a "Confidence: Speculative" tag is a disclosure, and this literature is the strongest external evidence that a disclosure does not reliably produce the discounting it is presumed to authorise, and can license the very overreach it was meant to bound. A 2018 direct replication of the core effects exists, which strengthens rather than weakens the citation.
    5. Mogull, S.A. (2017). "Accuracy of cited 'facts' in medical research articles: A review of study methodology and recalculation of quotation error rate." PLOS ONE 12(9):e0184727. — A methodological review and recalculation across prior quotation-accuracy studies. Recalculated quotation error rate 14.5% (95% CI 10.5-18.6), of which 64.8% (95% CI 56.1-73.5) are *major* errors — the cited source fails to substantiate, is unrelated to, or contradicts the assertion made of it. Separately reports improper secondary (indirect) citation at 10.4% (95% CI 3.4-17.5): authors citing a source secondhand without consulting the original. This addresses the presumption's second, underneath clause directly and quantitatively: when a third-party description is the only evidence a source says what it is claimed to say, the base rate of that description being materially wrong is not small, and roughly two-thirds of the errors that occur are of the kind that would void the claim rather than merely blur it.

  Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: Supported, and EXTERNALLY ANCHORED across four independent literatures (judgment calibration; self-assessment accuracy; LLM verbalized uncertainty; conflict-of-interest disclosure), plus one quantitative source on secondhand citation. The calibration literature supplies the sharpest form of the presumption's first claim: calibration is a property of many judgments scored against an external key, so a single self-assigned "Speculative" tag is not a weak control but a non-measurement — there is nothing for it to be calibrated against. Dunning, Heath and Suls generalise this to self-assessment as such, finding self-rating/performance correlations moderate to meagre, which is the empirical content of "a label attached by the same process that produced the claim is not independent of it." Groot and Valdenegro-Toro make the transfer to the exact case at issue, showing that model-emitted verbalized confidence is not merely noisy but directionally overconfident. Cain, Loewenstein and Moore then close the loop on the "right handling" endorsement specifically: labels are the intervention whose *downstream* efficacy has been tested, and it under-delivers on discounting while licensing overreach — so calling the label "the right handling" is precisely the inference the disclosure literature warns against. On the underneath clause, Mogull's recalculated 14.5% quotation error rate with 64.8% of those major, and 10.4% improper secondary citation, supply the base rate that makes an unreadable source with only a third-party description a live rather than theoretical risk.

  Caveats: (a) Scope mismatch on strength. The literature strongly supports "a self-assigned label is not a verification"; it does not support the stronger reading that a self-assigned label is *worthless*. Calibration research also shows self-confidence carries real if modest discriminative signal, and disclosure research shows disclosure sometimes works, particularly where recipients can act on it independently and the discloser cannot adjust their behaviour in response. If C2A2 treats "Speculative" as a *routing* signal (send this for external check) rather than as a *discharge* signal (this is now adequately handled), the literature is broadly consistent with that use. The presumption's target is the discharge reading, and it is the discharge reading the evidence undercuts. (b) Cain et al. is an economics/psychology laboratory paradigm with financial incentives; the analogy to an epistemic confidence tag in a documentation pipeline is structural, not empirical, and the moral-licensing mechanism in particular may not transfer to a non-strategic labeller. The insufficient-discounting result transfers more safely than the increased-bias result. (c) Groot & Valdenegro-Toro is a workshop paper on a small model set (six systems) with a purpose-built dataset; the direction of the finding is corroborated widely but the specific magnitudes should not be relied on. (d) Mogull's rates are from the biomedical literature under peer review; whether they transfer to a wiki pipeline is untested, and they could plausibly be either lower (fewer intermediaries) or higher (no reviewer). (e) None of these sources shows that the specific item labelled "Speculative" is wrong. They establish that the label does not bear on whether it is wrong — which is the presumption's actual claim, and is a claim about warrant, not about content. (f) Search scope: moderate-to-comprehensive. Six targeted searches across four literatures, each source's author/year/venue confirmed by search before citation. Not searched: the archival-science literature on provenance of unretrievable holdings, and formal work on self-certification in assurance cases, either of which could add or qualify support.
