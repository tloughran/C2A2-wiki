    1. Ashton, 2000. "A review and analysis of research on the test–retest reliability of professional judgment." Journal of Behavioral Decision Making. — The key review: expert intra-rater reliability is often substantial (frequently r ≈ .7–.9 in structured domains like meteorology and auditing), giving real grounds for expecting a domain expert's judgments to be fairly stable.
    2. Einhorn, 1974. "Expert judgment: Some necessary conditions and an example." Journal of Applied Psychology. — Theoretical grounding: intra-rater consistency is a necessary condition of expertise; experts in well-structured tasks can meet it.

  Strength of support: Weak
  Summary: There is partial support for the stability premise: the test-retest literature on professional judgment shows experts can be highly self-consistent, especially in domains where they receive structured feedback and the judgment task is well-defined — and an MM-of-1 rating milestones of his own long-practiced tradition is arguably such a domain. The methodological literature also makes stability cheap to verify (re-present a sample of ratified items after an interval; compute weighted kappa/ICC). What finds no support is the second half of the presumption — treating assent-now as durable *without* any test-retest or drift detection: Ashton's review emphasizes that reliability is highly variable across experts, domains, and task structures, so stability is a property to be measured per-rater, not presumed. The literature supports "this rater may well be stable," not "stability may be assumed unchecked."
  Caveats: Reported expert stability comes from repeated judgments of fixed stimuli over short-to-medium intervals; milestone assent over months, where genuine conceptual development is expected (and is the project's point), confounds standard-drift with legitimate change — the literature offers little guidance on separating the two for N=1. Minimal remedy implied: periodic re-ratification of a sampled subset with an a-priori drift threshold.
