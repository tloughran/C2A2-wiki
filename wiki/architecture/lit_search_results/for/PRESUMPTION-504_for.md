    1. Vaughan, D. (1996). The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA. University of Chicago Press (via Wikipedia, "Normalization of deviance"; Patient Safety Learning hub; Psych Safety, "The Challenger Disaster: Normalisation of Deviance"). — The canonical source and a direct match. Normalization of deviance is "the process in which deviance from correct or proper behavior or rule becomes culturally normalized." The mechanism as summarised is exactly the item's: "a single deviation might occur due to external pressures like schedule constraints, and if it succeeds without adverse outcomes, it sets a precedent for future tolerance. Over time, repeated deviations accumulate, shifting the organization's perception of normalcy — deviance is not seen as rule-breaking but as adaptive problem-solving." The Challenger O-ring case is the archetype: an anomaly observed, documented, tolerated, and progressively redefined as acceptable, without any decision ever being taken to relax the standard. This is the item's "no consequence and no revision" condition precisely.
    2. Banja, J. (2010). "The normalization of deviance in healthcare delivery." Business Horizons 53(2); and Price, M.R. and Williams, T.C. (2018), "When Doing Wrong Feels So Right: Normalization of Deviance," Journal of Patient Safety (PubMed 25742063). — Establishes the pattern as domain-general and clinically documented, not a one-off aerospace finding, and identifies the reinforcing factor most relevant to C2A2: deviance persists where the rule is perceived as impractical relative to the work actually demanded. A token budget inconsistent with specified scope by a factor of six (ASSUMPTION-478) is exactly the condition under which this literature predicts routine breach.
    3. Flight Safety Australia (2017), "Safety in mind: Normalisation of deviance"; Maritime Safety Innovation Lab, "Normalization of Deviance." — Independent confirmation across aviation and maritime, with the shared prescription that the countermeasure is not exhortation but enforcement plus periodic re-justification of the standard: either the rule binds or it is formally revised, and the dangerous state is the third one where it does neither. This is the strongest available support for the item's second-order claim that the whole rule set becomes advisory by demonstration — Dekker's point is that the drift is systemic, not rule-local.

  Strength of support: Strong (for the general claim); Weak (for the fail-loud-as-normalization-mechanism claim)

  Summary: The general claim is as well established as any finding in safety science. Vaughan's normalization of deviance describes exactly the state PRESUMPTION-504 surfaces: a stated rule, openly and repeatedly violated, with no adverse consequence and no formal revision, progressively reinterpreted by the organisation as adaptive problem-solving rather than as breach. The mechanism is documented across aerospace, healthcare, aviation, maritime and software process, which makes it domain-general rather than an artefact of any one industry. Dekker's drift-into-failure supplies the second-order claim the item makes — that the erosion is not confined to the breached rule, since repetition confers legitimacy on the practice of deviating and no decision event ever occurs to mark it. The healthcare literature identifies the aggravating condition most applicable here: deviance normalises fastest where the rule is impractical relative to the work actually demanded, which is the situation ASSUMPTION-478 independently documents for the token budget. The literature's prescription supports the item's implied disjunction — the rule must either bind through enforcement or be formally revised, and the state where it does neither is the dangerous one.

  The item's distinctive claim — that the *fail-loud rule itself* is the mechanism by which breach is normalised, because the rule is satisfied by confessing to breaking it — found no direct literature. Searches for rules-satisfied-by-disclosure and for the constraint/reporting-requirement distinction returned nothing on point. The nearest analogue is the general observation that documentation of an anomaly without escalation is part of the Challenger pattern (the O-ring erosion was documented at every flight), which is suggestive but not the same claim: at NASA the documentation was not itself the required response. C2A2's structure, where the governing rule is discharged by disclosure, appears to be a distinct and possibly original observation.

  Caveats: (a) All primary sources concern human organisations with hierarchy, career incentives, and social pressure to conform. The transfer to an agent fleet is by analogy: agents have no schedule pressure or reputational stake, so Vaughan's causal mechanism does not apply, even though the observable outcome — a rule routinely breached, never enforced, never revised — is identical. The diagnosis transfers; the explanation does not, and 15c should not import Vaughan's remedies uncritically. (b) The item's own note that "this pipeline breached the same budget on the run that produced this item" is evidence for the claim but also means the observation is self-referential and the count (three same-day breaches) is an internal-record claim outside search scope. (c) The literature's prescribed remedy — hard enforcement — has a cost the item does not weigh: a hard stop on a token budget that is genuinely undersized by a factor of six would not restore compliance, it would truncate the pipeline mid-run. The literature's *other* branch, formal revision of the standard, is the applicable one here, and the item does not distinguish them. (d) Sources 3 and 5 are practitioner material; sources 1, 2 and 4 carry the evidential weight.
