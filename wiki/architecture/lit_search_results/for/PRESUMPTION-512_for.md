  Sources:
    1. Ancker, J.S. et al. (2017). "Effects of workload, work complexity, and repeated alerts on alert fatigue in a clinical decision support system." BMC Medical Informatics and Decision Making. PMC5387195. — The strongest and most directly applicable source. Measures alert acceptance as a function of *workload and cumulative alert exposure* rather than of the individual alert's merit, which is exactly the presumption 512 attacks: per-item response probability is not constant, it declines with volume. This is the load-dependence finding the item needs, in a peer-reviewed observational study of a real review channel.
    2. Clinical decision support override literature (PMC3128393, "Factors influencing alert acceptance"; PMC4052586, "Clinical Decision Support Alert Appropriateness"; JMIR Med Inform 10(10):e40511 / PMC9579928). — Documents override rates of 49-96% in operational systems and the finding that where acknowledgement was not required, acceptance fell to 1.4%. Establishes the magnitude of the effect: a saturated recipient channel converges toward near-total non-response regardless of individual item quality, which is 512's risk claim ("each additional correctly-argued item would lower the probability that any item is acted on") in measured form.
    3. Static-analysis alert-fatigue literature (Contrast Security, "The Truth About AppSec False Positives"; Corgea, "How to Reduce False Positives in SAST"; "Mitigating False Positive Static Analysis Warnings," 2023). — Cross-domain corroboration with an explicit causal loop: more findings → less trust → fewer fixes applied → more real defects surviving. Notably, this literature makes the point 512 needs about *correctly-argued* items specifically: desensitisation generalises from the volume of findings to all findings, so true positives are discounted alongside false ones.
    4. Danziger, S., Levav, J., Avnaim-Pesso, L. (2011). "Extraneous factors in judicial decisions." PNAS 108(17):6889-6892. — The canonical decision-fatigue result: 1,112 parole rulings by eight Israeli judges, with grant rates falling from roughly 65% at the start of a session toward near zero by its end and resetting after a break. The mechanism reported is a drift toward the low-effort default (deny/status quo) as decisions accumulate, which is the item's exact concern about position in the queue determining outcome.
    5. Glöckner, A. (2016). "The irrational hungry judge effect revisited: Simulations reveal that the magnitude of the effect is overestimated." Judgment and Decision Making 11(6):601-610; Weinshall-Margel, K. & Shapard, J. (2011). PNAS correspondence on case ordering. — Retrieved deliberately as a correction to source 4. Weinshall-Margel and Shapard show case ordering was not random (unrepresented prisoners went last and were less likely to be granted parole), and Glöckner's simulations indicate the effect magnitude, if real, is substantially overestimated. Danziger et al. responded that the trend holds controlling for representation without reporting whether magnitude survives. Reported here so the item's support is not overstated: the *direction* of decision fatigue has broad support, but the headline effect size does not.

  Strength of support: Moderate
  Summary: The presumption 512 surfaces — constant per-item review cost and reviewer capacity scaling with production — is contradicted by the best-studied analogue available. Alert-fatigue research measures acceptance as a function of workload and cumulative exposure rather than item merit, and the operational figures are stark: override rates of 49-96%, and acceptance falling to 1.4% where acknowledgement is not compelled. The static-analysis literature adds the clause that matters most for a pipeline producing high-quality items, namely that desensitisation generalises — a channel saturated by volume discounts true positives along with false ones, so producing a better-argued item does not exempt it. That is 512's counterintuitive risk claim, and it is supported. The decision-fatigue literature points the same way but should be cited carefully: the celebrated parole-board result has a well-documented case-ordering confound and a simulation-based critique concluding the magnitude is substantially overestimated, so it supports the direction of the effect and not the size. The item's proposed test is the strongest thing about it and requires no literature at all: sum every open item awaiting Tom, attach a time estimate to each, and report the total in hours. That number does not currently exist anywhere, which is itself the finding, and producing it is a single query.
  Caveats: (a) Alert fatigue is measured on high-frequency, low-latency interruptive alerts embedded in clinical workflow; C2A2's review items are low-frequency, asynchronous, and self-selected in timing. The mechanism (cumulative exposure degrades response) plausibly transfers; the rates certainly do not, and the 1.4% figure should not be quoted as a prediction. (b) The decision-fatigue evidence is materially weakened by Glöckner 2016 and Weinshall-Margel & Shapard 2011; any use of the 65%-to-near-zero figure without that qualification would misrepresent the state of the literature. (c) n=1 reviewer. Every source here concerns population-level rates across many operators, and individual variation in these effects is large; nothing retrieved licenses a claim about this particular reviewer's capacity. (d) The item's structural claim — that no artifact sums the open ask in hours — is an internal record claim outside search scope, though it is the cheapest element to check. (e) There is a tension the item does not resolve and the literature makes visible: the CDS remedy for saturation is to raise per-item precision, i.e. produce fewer and better items, but PRESUMPTION-509 warns that any resulting quality metric is Goodhart-vulnerable, and PRESUMPTION-510's throttle would reduce volume without addressing prioritisation. These three items propose interacting interventions on one channel and should be dispositioned together rather than independently. (f) Publication bias: dramatic fatigue effects are more publishable than null results, which is precisely how source 4 achieved its prominence before correction.
