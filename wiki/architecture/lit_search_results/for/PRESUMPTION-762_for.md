  Sources:
    1. Bachrach, Peter; Baratz, Morton S., 1962. "Two Faces of Power." American Political Science Review 56(4): 947–952. (developed further in their 1963 "Decisions and Non-Decisions: An Analytical Framework," APSR 57(3), and in Power and Poverty, 1970.) — Introduces non-decision-making: a non-decision is a decision that suppresses a latent or manifest challenge to the decision-maker's interests, and it is "easy to induce, for it requires no action." Establishes that leaving an item unresolved is an exercise of power with a determinate outcome, not an absence of one.
    2. Anderson, Christopher J., 2003. "The Psychology of Doing Nothing: Forms of Decision Avoidance Result from Reason and Emotion." Psychological Bulletin 129(1): 139–167. — Identifies four distinct decision-avoidance effects — choice deferral, status quo bias, omission bias, and inaction inertia — and models them via cost–benefit calculation, anticipated regret, and selection difficulty. Supplies the mechanism by which an item is *held* rather than decided, and predicts that difficulty and anticipated regret, not merit, drive holding.
    3. Security-exception and risk-waiver practice literature (multiple practitioner sources, incl. University of Colorado OIT "Risk Acceptance and Temporary Security Exceptions" standard; Azure PSRule Policy.WaiverExpiry rule). [Practitioner/grey literature — institutional standards and tooling, not research.] — Codifies the observation that an exception granted temporarily becomes a permanent fixture when no expiry is tracked, and responds by mandating expiry dates and forced periodic reassessment. Tooling that automatically fails waivers lacking an expiry is indirect evidence that the failure mode is common enough to automate against.

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: The presumption is well-supported as a general proposition: the distinction it draws is real and the default reading it warns against is the one the literature says people actually make. Bachrach and Baratz established sixty years ago that non-decision is a decision, and specifically that it is the low-effort path — which is exactly why a long-held item cannot be read as evidence of deliberate governance without independent confirmation that a deliberation occurred. Anderson (2003) supplies the individual-level mechanism and, importantly for C2A2, predicts that items are deferred when they are *difficult* or *regret-laden*, meaning age correlates with unresolved contention rather than with settled judgement. The practitioner standards close the loop operationally: whole institutional processes and static-analysis rules exist for the sole purpose of preventing untracked exceptions from silently becoming permanent, which is prima facie evidence that they otherwise do. The practical upshot is that duration alone is uninformative, and the discriminating evidence is whether a dated review or expiry exists.

  Caveats: The support is conceptual and, for the operational arm, grey. I found no empirical study measuring the base rate at which long-held queue items turn out to be deliberate holds versus defaults — the literature establishes that both exist and that inaction is cheap, not the proportion, so it cannot tell C2A2 how often its own aged items are one or the other. Bachrach and Baratz's non-decision is a construct developed for contested political power with an interested agent suppressing an issue, and it imports awkwardly into a low-stakes review queue where the more likely cause is simple capacity limits rather than interest-driven suppression. Source 3 is institutional/vendor material with no named authors and no measurement.
