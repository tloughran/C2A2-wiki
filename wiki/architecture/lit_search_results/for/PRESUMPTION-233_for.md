  Sources:
    1. MacIntyre (1988, ch. 18-19): tradition-constituted rational comparison via "epistemological crisis." — A tradition can be rationally judged superior when it resolves, in its own terms, problems the rival cannot; comparison without a neutral standard is possible.
    2. Kuhn (1969 Postscript; 1983 "Commensurability, Comparability, Communicability"). — Incommensurability means "no common measure," NOT incomparability; rival paradigms can still be compared.
    3. Davidson (1974) "On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme." — Argues against radical incommensurability: total failure of translation is incoherent, so some common ground always exists.

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: Commensurability-enough-for-comparison has a respectable defense, and crucially it is available from within the project's own MacIntyrean commitment: MacIntyre's epistemological-crisis mechanism lets one tradition be judged superior without a tradition-neutral yardstick. Kuhn's later clarifications and Davidson both deny that incommensurability blocks comparison. So the head-to-head is not doomed in principle. The support is moderate, not strong, because it licenses tradition-internal comparison, not the "shared neutral criteria" scoring the presumption actually describes — which is exactly what 15b contests.

  Caveats: The supportive route (MacIntyrean crisis resolution) is a DIFFERENT method than "shared criteria" scoring; if the experiment uses an imposed neutral scorecard, this support does not transfer.
