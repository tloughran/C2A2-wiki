  Sources:
    1. Gentner, D. (1983). "Structure-Mapping: A Theoretical Framework for Analogy." Cognitive Science 7(2):155-170. — The standard account of analogy in cognitive science and philosophy of science. Mapping rules "depend only on syntactic properties of the knowledge representation, and not on the specific content of the domains," and the systematicity principle holds that a predicate belonging to a mappable system of mutually interconnecting relations is imported into the target, whereas an isolated predicate is not. The framework's stated purpose includes distinguishing analogies "cleanly from literal similarity statements." A single shared LABEL is the limiting case of an isolated, non-systematic correspondence: it licenses nothing on its own. This directly supports the presumption's core objection — that the inference runs through the term rather than through an aligned system of relations. SEE CORRELATION DISCLOSURE below: this is the same primary source that already underpins PREMISE-127 and PREMISE-134.
    2. Gentner, D., Rattermann, M.J., & Forbus, K.D. (1993). "The roles of similarity in transfer: Separating retrievability and inferential soundness." Cognitive Psychology 25:524-575. — Experimentally dissociates two things a shared feature can do: drive RETRIEVAL from memory, and confer INFERENTIAL SOUNDNESS. Remindings driven by surface similarity far outnumbered structurally-driven remindings, yet participants' soundness judgements tracked structure, not surface. This is the sharpest available support for the presumption's specific diagnosis: a shared term such as "reference frame" is a retrieval cue — it is exactly what makes the Hawkins mechanism come to mind when PRS triplets are under discussion — and the literature's finding is that this retrieval route is empirically dissociable from, and a poor guide to, the soundness of the inference retrieved. The word explains why the connection was noticed; it does not bear on whether it holds. Also correlated with PREMISE-134 (see disclosure).
    3. Boyd, R. (1993). "Metaphor and theory change: what is 'metaphor' a metaphor for?" Chapter 21 in A. Ortony (ed.), Metaphor and Thought, 2nd ed. Cambridge University Press (originally 1979). — Introduces theory-constitutive metaphors: metaphors that do genuine scientific work by fixing reference to causal structure that cannot yet be stated literally, but which are characterised by OPEN-ENDEDNESS — they are not fully explicated and have a "programmatic character," inviting further research rather than settling it. This is independent external support for the presumption's precise shape, and it supports the boundary as well as the objection: adopting "reference frame" for PRS triplets is legitimate theory-constitutive work, but on Boyd's own account the metaphor's content is *not yet fixed*, so a mechanism discovered in the source domain cannot be inherited by the target as settled warrant. The metaphor's open-endedness is the reason a transfer check is owed, not a reason to skip one.
    4. Daugman, J.G. (1990). "Brain metaphor and brain theory." In E.L. Schwartz (ed.), Computational Neuroscience, MIT Press, pp. 9-18 (widely reprinted, incl. Bechtel et al. (eds.), Philosophy and the Neurosciences: A Reader, Blackwell, 2001); with Keller, E.F. (1995/1996). Refiguring Life: Metaphors of Twentieth-Century Biology. Columbia University Press. — Daugman traces the historical pattern in which each era's dominant material technology supplies the reigning metaphor for mind and brain — hydraulic for the pre-Socratics, clockwork in the 18th-19th centuries, computation now — and closes with the warning that "the enthusiastically embraced metaphors of each 'new era' can become, like their predecessors, as much the prisonhouse of thought as they at first appeared to represent its liberation." Keller documents the same loop in biology at the level of research practice: metaphors born of the computer revolution were appropriated by established biological research agendas and then directed the search for evidence. Both are external, published demonstrations of the exact failure mode the presumption names — a borrowed term ceasing to read as analogical and beginning to read as descriptive, at which point mechanism claims cross the boundary unchecked. 14b's stated reason the presumption went unstated ("the term was borrowed years of wiki-time ago and now reads as descriptive rather than analogical") is precisely the process these two sources document.
    5. Hawkins, J., Lewis, M., Klukas, M., Purdy, S., & Ahmad, S. (2019). "A Framework for Intelligence and Cortical Function Based on Grid Cells in the Neocortex." Frontiers in Neural Circuits 12:121; with Lindsay, G.W. (2020). "Attention in Psychology, Neuroscience, and Machine Learning." Frontiers in Computational Neuroscience 14:29. — The Hawkins paper is the source-domain referent: "reference frame" there denotes a grid-cell-based location code in which cortical columns represent input location in the external reference frame of the object being sensed. That usage is itself an import from physics and robotics into neuroscience, so the C2A2 usage is a second-order borrowing — and the specific content that makes the term load-bearing in Hawkins (a metric location space, movement-contingent prediction, sensorimotor grounding) is precisely what has not been shown to hold of a Problem-Research-Solution triplet. Lindsay's review supplies the contemporary methodological case: an engineering field borrowed "attention" from psychology and neuroscience, and the review's task is to work out case by case which machine-learning uses have biological counterparts and which do not — demonstrating in current practice that the correspondence must be established item by item and cannot be read off the shared word. Together these show that the transfer check the presumption demands is the normal standard of care in exactly this kind of borrowing, not an unusual burden invented for this case.

Structure-mapping theory holds that what transfers between domains is systematic relational structure, not shared labels or isolated predicates, so a shared term is the limiting case of a correspondence that licenses nothing on its own — and Gentner, Rattermann & Forbus's experimental dissociation of retrievability from inferential soundness gives the presumption its sharpest form: the shared word "reference frame" is exactly what makes the Hawkins mechanism come to mind here, and that retrieval route is empirically a poor guide to whether the inference holds. Boyd's account of theory-constitutive metaphors supports both the legitimacy of the original borrowing and the objection to this use of it: such metaphors are valuable precisely because they are open-ended and programmatic, which means their content is not yet fixed and a source-domain mechanism cannot be inherited as settled warrant. Daugman and Keller then document, historically and in research practice, the specific hardening the presumption identifies — a metaphor adopted long enough ago that it stops reading as analogical and starts reading as description, after which mechanism claims cross the boundary unchallenged. Finally, Hawkins et al. show what "reference frame" actually carries in the source domain (a grid-cell location code, movement-contingent prediction, sensorimotor grounding), none of which has been shown to hold of a PRS triplet, while Lindsay's attention review demonstrates that establishing such correspondences item by item is the ordinary standard of care in contemporary cross-domain borrowing.
