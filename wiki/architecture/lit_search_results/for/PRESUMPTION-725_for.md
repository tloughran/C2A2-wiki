  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: The scholarly-publishing retraction literature provides a well-documented, close analogue to this presumption: retractions are scoped to the originating artefact (a paper, a test, an escalation) and do not automatically propagate to derivative or downstream uses of the retracted content (citations, preprints, or — by analogy — the "88 phantom drift items" the item cites). Studies quantify this as a persistent, systemic problem (5x+ citation "domino effects" of retracted results) rather than an isolated glitch, and publisher policy explicitly defines correction scope as narrow. This gives moderate empirical precedent for the presumption's claim that narrow retraction of a criterion leaves its downstream instances unretracted "wherever they landed."

  Caveats: The analogy is domain-transferred — from scholarly citation networks to an internal automated-audit/escalation system — and the underlying propagation mechanisms (citation vs. whatever indexing the C2A2 system uses for drift items) are not identical, so transfer should be treated as illustrative rather than proof.
