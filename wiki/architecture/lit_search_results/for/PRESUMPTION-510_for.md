  Strength of support: Strong
  Summary: The item's core proposition is arithmetic before it is empirical. Where arrivals exceed service the queue grows without bound; where service is zero the steady-state relations Little's law describes do not hold at all, and cycle time is undefined rather than large. Production and judgement are therefore not independently schedulable in any system where the second gates the value of the first — a conclusion the Kanban and lean traditions built their central practice around, since a WIP limit placed upstream of the bottleneck exists specifically to protect a constrained stage by limiting the arrival rate. Distributed-systems practice states the same principle more sharply, treating back-pressure and bounded queues as a correctness requirement rather than an optimisation, with an explicit rule that a full queue must block, drop, or reject. Against that standard, a review channel with no admission control, no bounded state, and no "produced and unreviewable" status is missing a mechanism that two independent engineering traditions treat as mandatory. The item's further claim — that unbounded production actively lowers the record's value rather than merely deferring its use — is supported by the alert-fatigue literature, where increasing volume measurably reduces the recipient's response rate; that coupling is what defeats the independence presumption. The proposed test (apply PREMISE-106's arithmetic to the review channel; trial a one-HIGH-item-per-day admission cap) is exactly the intervention the WIP literature prescribes.
  Caveats: (a) Kanban and queueing sources assume a service rate that is positive and roughly stationary. A rate of zero across fifteen days may indicate an unavailable rather than a saturated consumer, and the remedies differ sharply: WIP limits help a bottleneck, but they do nothing about an absent one, and throttling production against an absent reviewer simply produces less of everything. Distinguishing these is the first measurement, and the item does not distinguish them. (b) Most retrieved WIP sources are practitioner material; Little's law itself is the rigorous element and it is being applied qualitatively here. (c) Blocking is not the only admission-control policy — the distributed-systems formulation offers block, drop, *or reject*, and for a knowledge pipeline "drop" (do not produce low-value items) and "reject" (produce but do not route) may dominate "block" (stop producing). The item defaults to throttling without considering that triage at the routing step preserves more value. (d) The arrival and service figures are internal record claims outside search scope. (e) There is a real cost to admission control the item does not price: an item not produced is not merely deferred but never exists, and PREMISE-106's own instability finding suggests the throughput estimate that would set the cap is not yet reliable.
