  Sources:
    1. Li G, Abbade LPF, Nwosu I, et al., 2017. "A scoping review of comparisons between abstracts and full reports in primary biomedical research." BMC Medical Research Methodology 17:181 (doi 10.1186/s12874-017-0459-5). [Access level: FULL TEXT fetched; abstract, discussion and conclusion read.] 17 studies. Inconsistency between abstract and full report had a median of 39% (IQR 14-54%, range 4-78%). Major inconsistency (design, sample size, primary outcome, main results, conclusions) had a median of 19% (range 5-45%). The authors conclude that abstracts are "frequently inconsistent with full reports." They note that structured abstracts and CONSORT-for-abstracts may not prevent this, and that the evidence is mostly from RCTs and conference abstracts.
    2. Pitkin RM, Branagan MA, Burmeister LF, 1999. "Accuracy of data in abstracts of published research articles." JAMA 281(12):1110-1111. [Access level: secondary summaries and citation in Li 2017.] Across six major general medical journals, 18-68% of abstracts contained data inconsistent with, or absent from, the article body. A companion RCT (Pitkin & Branagan 1998, JAMA 280:267-9, cited in Li 2017) found that giving authors instructions did not improve accuracy.
    3. Boutron I, Dutton S, Ravaud P, Altman DG, 2010. "Reporting and Interpretation of Randomized Controlled Trials With Statistically Nonsignificant Results for Primary Outcomes." JAMA 303(20):2058-2064. [Access level: abstract/snippet.] Of 72 RCTs with nonsignificant primary outcomes, "spin" appeared in 58.3% of abstract conclusions and 18% of titles. The follow-up SPIIN experiment (J Clin Oncol; details unverified) found that spin in abstracts changed how clinicians rated benefit. This shows that titles and abstracts can systematically misstate the claim structure of the full text.
    4. Peters U, Chin-Yee B, 2025. "Generalization bias in large language model summarization of scientific research." Royal Society Open Science (doi 10.1098/rsos.241776). [Access level: abstract/snippet.] 4,900 LLM summaries from 10 models, including Claude 3.7 Sonnet. Most models overgeneralized beyond the original texts even when prompted for accuracy (26-73% for some models). LLM summaries were about 4.85 times more likely than human summaries to contain broad generalizations, and newer models were often worse. This applies directly to "a pipeline's own card text" produced by LLM agents.
    5. Cochrane Colloquium abstracts (1998 Baltimore; 2015 Vienna; 2016 Seoul) on conference-abstract vs. full-report agreement. [Access level: snippets; these are themselves conference abstracts.] In ophthalmology RCTs, more than a third of primary outcomes differed between abstract and full report. Disagreement was frequent, but large differences in results were rarer.
    6. Genette G, 1987/1997. Paratexts: Thresholds of Interpretation (Seuils). Cambridge UP. [Access level: publisher/catalogue listings and a secondary article (Wachter, Dacoromania Litteraria) only.] Genette treats titles and intertitles (chapter titles) as thresholds that mediate between publisher and text. Per Wachter, titles often evoke the theme "symbolically or cryptically" rather than literally. This is theoretical, not empirical: it describes paratext as a mediating and often strategic device, not a faithful map of content. I found NO empirical study measuring how well chapter titles or tables of contents recover argument structure.

  Strength of support: Strong (for abstracts and LLM summaries); Weak/theoretical (for chapter titles and paratext)

  Summary: For abstracts, the evidence is strong and consistent. Across 17 studies, a median 39% of abstracts are inconsistent with their full reports and about 19% have major inconsistencies in outcomes, results or conclusions. Spin affects more than half the abstract conclusions of trials with null results. For LLM-generated summaries, the closest analogue to C2A2's card text, recent work finds systematic overgeneralization, worse than human summaries. For chapter titles and tables of contents, I found only Genette's theoretical framework, which treats titles as interpretive thresholds rather than literal content maps, and no empirical accuracy data. Taken together, the literature does not support the presumption that a work's structure is reliably recoverable from paratext or summary. It supports the concern the presumption raises. Carding from abstracts or pipeline card text carries a substantial, documented error rate that only a full-text check can catch.

  Caveats: (a) The abstract-inconsistency evidence is mostly from biomedical RCTs and conference abstracts. Philosophy and physics monographs (e.g., Hoffman, Friston) may behave differently, and "structure" (argument architecture) is not the same as "data accuracy." (b) A 39% inconsistency rate also means many abstracts ARE consistent, so summaries remain useful for triage and screening. (c) The Speculative tag already signals low confidence, which partly mitigates the risk. (d) Several sources were read only at snippet level, and the Genette coverage is secondary.
