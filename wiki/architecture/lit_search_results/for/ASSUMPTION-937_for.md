  Sources:
    1. Block, J. (1995). Psychological Bulletin — the classic modern restatement of the **jingle and jangle fallacies**. The jingle fallacy: giving the same name to measures of different constructs, so that conceptually distinct things are treated as equivalent *because they bear the same label*. This is the precise mechanism ASSUMPTION-937 names: shared vocabulary generating apparent identity. [Attribution to Block 1995 is standard and appeared consistently across sources; the exact article title was not independently re-checked this session.] Antecedents: Thorndike (1904) on jingle, Kelley (1927) on jangle.
    2. "Uncovering jingle and jangle fallacies: A systematic review mapping future directions." *Zeitschrift für Psychologie* 233(4) (2025/2026). — Systematic review documenting how widespread label-driven false convergence and false divergence are across psychology, and attributing their persistence partly to the absence of a shared scientific language (a "missing lingua franca"). Directly on point for a project that supplies its own lingua franca to a detector. [Title/venue verified via Hogrefe and PsycNET listings; author list not confirmed.]
    3. "Detecting jingle and jangle fallacies by identifying consistencies and variabilities in study specifications — a call for research." *Frontiers in Psychology* (2024) 15: 1404060. — Argues these fallacies are "rife in psychology" and a contributor to the validation and replication crises, and proposes specification-level detection. Supplies a candidate remedy design for the wiki: hold the label constant and vary the specification. [Title/venue verified; author list not confirmed.]
    4. Rosenthal, R., & Fode, K. L. (1963); Rosenthal, R. — **experimenter/observer expectancy effects**. The canonical demonstration that an observer's prior expectation systematically biases what the observer records: once an expectation is formed, hypothesis-consistent instances are preferentially noticed and confirming behaviour is elicited. Standard mitigations named in this literature — unambiguous operational definitions fixed in advance, multiple independent coders, blinding of coders to the hypothesis — are exactly the controls a single-pass cross-tradition detector lacks.
    5. Hurtado Bodell, M., Magnusson, M., & Keuschnigg, M. (2026). "Seeded Topic Models in Digital Archives: Analyzing Interpretations of Immigration in Swedish Newspapers, 1945–2019." *Sociological Methods & Research*. — The computational analogue. Seeded models return the concepts the researcher seeds; the authors' response is to require that researchers be explicit about how concepts are operationalised, precisely because the seeding constrains what the model can find. Author list verified via SAGE listing.
    6. Cornell Chronicle (October 2021), "Words used in text-mining research carry bias, study finds," reporting research on **seed-word bias in lexical bias measurement**. — Finding: word lists shared among researchers to measure bias in text carry baked-in biases, which then skew the findings; the worked example given is that including the seed term "mom" in an analysis of domestic work skews the result female. Dictionary-based methods are shown to produce biased text measures and different substantive conclusions depending on seed choice.   Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: I scored the affirmative reading — that terminological seeding does manufacture, at least in part, the convergence a detector subsequently finds. The mechanism is not speculative; it is separately documented in four literatures that have no contact with each other. Psychometrics calls it the jingle fallacy and treats it as a live contributor to the replication crisis. Experimental social psychology calls it observer-expectancy and has a century of evidence that coders record what they expect. Computational text analysis calls it seed bias and has shown that seeded topic models and dictionary methods return the researcher's own vocabulary, changing substantive conclusions with the seed list. What none of this literature contains is a study of the specific object here — an automated cross-tradition convergence detector operating over a corpus whose vocabulary was itself curated by the same programme. The support is therefore strong on mechanism and entirely by analogy on application. For the 90+ CROSS entries the practical implication is concrete and testable: the standard controls in all four literatures are the same, and none appears to be in place — pre-registered operational definitions of each cross-tradition construct, independent coders blind to the pairing hypothesis, and a held-out corpus that the wiki's own vocabulary did not touch. A CROSS entry that survives a re-run against unseeded source texts is evidence; one that does not is a jingle.

  Caveats:
    - This is analogical support. No source studies a C2A2-style detector, and none quantifies how much of a detected convergence is seeding-attributable. "Manufactures" in the strong sense (all of it) is not supported; "inflates" is.
    - The mechanism cuts both ways and the literature says so: the jangle fallacy is the mirror error, where genuinely identical constructs go undetected because two traditions name them differently. A detector that is de-seeded to avoid jingle will start committing jangle. The wiki should not treat de-seeding as a free improvement.
    - Sources 2, 3 and 6 have unverified author attributions and source 1's title was not re-checked. Verify before any of these appear in a wiki citation line.
    - Not novel as a general methodological worry — it is textbook. What is undocumented is its instantiation in an LLM-agent-run cross-tradition detector, and no NOVELTY flag is raised because the underlying claim is well covered.
