  Summary: The evidence-synthesis literature treats abstract-level reading as a legitimate and
  well-characterised first gate: dual abstract screening recovers about 97.5% of relevant studies
  (Gartlehner et al. 2020), Cochrane's rapid-review guidance formally permits abstract-level inclusion
  decisions under stated safeguards (Garritty et al. 2021), and abstract/full-text discrepancies, though
  frequent, are rarely decision-changing (Fontelo et al. 2013). The stage-gate literature (Cooper 1990)
  gives the "proposal plus explicit gate" structure a long-standing institutional pedigree. Taken
  together these support the practice of building a proposal from abstracts provided the proposal is
  marked provisional and a full-text (or equivalent) check is a named gate. Whether the noted gate
  actually changes downstream behaviour is much less well supported: the one direct trial found
  (Yavchitz et al. 2014) shows readers did not adjust their confidence when a limitations statement was
  added to an abstract.

  Caveats: (a) All screening-sensitivity figures are for screen-IN decisions with a downstream full-text
  stage; they say nothing about abstract-level reading as a terminal basis for a claim. (b) Single-reader
  abstract screening misses ~13% (Gartlehner et al. 2020) and 34-51% in a 2026 rapid-review meta-study
  (JCE, "Evaluating the accuracy of screening titles and abstracts... by one vs two independent
  reviewers", authors/pages NOT verified), so the support is for dual or safeguarded abstract reading,
  not a single pass. (c) Marcelo et al. 2013 found full text more accurate for clinical decisions, if
  only in one department. (d) The "gate survives into downstream use" half of the claim is not directly
  supported by anything found; Yavchitz et al. 2014 is the closest evidence and it is null. (e) Search
  scope: preliminary — 9 queries; not covered: the citation-fidelity / "telephone effect" literature on
  whether caveats survive citation chains (Yang, Gao et al. 2025 arXiv 2502.20581 surfaced but not
  examined), the Registered Reports / conditional-acceptance literature, and GRADE certainty-of-evidence
  propagation studies, all of which bear on sub-claim (ii).
