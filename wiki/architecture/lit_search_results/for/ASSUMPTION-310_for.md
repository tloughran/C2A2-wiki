  Sources:
    1. Schechtman, Marya. 1996. The Constitution of Selves. Cornell University Press. — Schechtman's narrative self-constitution thesis holds that persons constitute their identities by developing autobiographical narratives organized in relation to facts about their environment and other persons. The narrative principle plays a strongly individuating role: different narrative histories differentiate persons. This is the closest extant philosophical support for narrative as individuation principle, though Schechtman does not work within a CRm frame and does not assert narrative as the exclusive individuating factor across all possible ontologies.

    2. Ricoeur, Paul. 1992. Oneself as Another. University of Chicago Press (trans. Kathleen Blamey). — Ricoeur distinguishes idem-identity (numerical sameness) from ipse-identity (selfhood through narrative coherence over time). Narrative identity is the mediating form that synthesises succession into selfhood. Ricoeur's account supports narrative as the constitutive principle of personal perspective and individuation, but stops short of an exclusivity claim and does not engage cosmopsychist or monist metaphysics.

    3. Dennett, Daniel C. 1991. "The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity." In F. Kessel, P. Cole & D. Johnson (eds.), Self and Consciousness: Multiple Perspectives. Erlbaum. — Dennett proposes the self as an abstract "center of narrative gravity," an entity real in the same way that centers of gravity are real — picked out by narrative patterns rather than by a distinct metaphysical substance. Supports narrative individuation but from an eliminativist/functionalist direction, not from a monist realist ontology.

    4. Tononi, Giulio, et al. 2016. "Integrated Information Theory: From Consciousness to Its Physical Substrate." Nature Reviews Neuroscience 17: 450–461. — IIT's exclusion axiom and the related decomposition problem show that individuating experiential subjects from a background of integrated information is a non-trivial theoretical task. IIT confirms that the individuation question is live in consciousness studies, but resolves it via phi-maximisation rather than narrative structure, providing contrast evidence rather than direct support for the CRm-narrative claim.

    5. Goff, Philip. 2017. Consciousness and Fundamental Reality. Oxford University Press. — Goff's cosmopsychist programme confronts the individuation/decomposition problem head-on: how does a single cosmic mind decompose into individual perspectives? Goff's discussion confirms the problem is genuine within monist frameworks but does not invoke narrative as the solution, leaving the CRm exclusivity claim without external corroboration.

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: The narrative identity tradition — Ricoeur, Schechtman, Dennett — provides substantial philosophical grounding for the claim that narrative structure is a (perhaps the primary) principle of individuation for persons and perspectives. Schechtman's self-constitution thesis and Ricoeur's ipse-identity are the closest analogues. However, none of these authors makes the exclusivity claim ("nothing else") nor operates within a conscious-realist-monist (CRm) metaphysical frame. The individuation/decomposition problem within monist and cosmopsychist frameworks (IIT, Goff) is acknowledged in the literature as unsolved, which confirms the claim addresses a real gap but does not confirm the proposed solution. The "nothing else" rider thus goes beyond what the supporting literature warrants.

  Caveats: All supporting sources address individuation of persons/subjects within broadly naturalist or dualist-leaning frameworks. None addresses CRm specifically. The exclusivity formulation ("nothing else") is not attested in any external source reviewed; it is a novel and strong claim that requires internal systematic justification. Partial support should not be upgraded to full support without a CRm-internal derivation.
