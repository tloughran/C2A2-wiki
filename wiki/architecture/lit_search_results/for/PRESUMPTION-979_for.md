  Sources:
    1. Boutron, I., Altman, D.G., Hopewell, S., Vera-Badillo, F., Tannock, I. & Ravaud, P., 2014. "Impact
       of Spin in the Abstracts of Articles Reporting Results of Randomized Controlled Trials in the Field
       of Cancer: The SPIIN Randomized Controlled Trial." Journal of Clinical Oncology 32(36): 4120–4126.
       — The strongest FOR evidence found. 300 clinicians randomised to read the same cancer trial abstract
       with or without spin; presence of spin made clinicians more likely to judge the treatment
       beneficial, though the absolute difference was small. Notably, spin also made clinicians rate the
       study as *less* rigorous and made them more likely to want the full text — i.e. expert readers both
       registered the prose and reacted to its framing. Prose in a report is read and it moves judgement.
       **SECONDARY** — abstract level.
    2. Menon, K. & Williams, D.D., 2010. "Investor Reaction to Going Concern Audit Reports." The Accounting
       Review 85(6): 2075–2105. — Prose qualifications in a real-world high-stakes report are acted on, but
       conditionally: institutional investors drive the reaction, with no detectable reaction at low levels
       of institutional ownership and an increasingly negative reaction as institutional ownership rises.
       Reader sophistication, not the qualification's presence, determines whether it is acted on.
       **SECONDARY**.
    3. Cox, E.P., Wogalter, M.S., Stokes, S.L. & Murff, E.J.T., 1997. "Do Product Warnings Increase Safe
       Behavior? A Meta-Analysis." Journal of Public Policy & Marketing; and (2020) "How effective are
       warnings? A meta-analysis." Safety Science. Related: Magurno & Wogalter, 1994, on placement effects.
       — Warnings do increase safe behaviour, but with widely disparate success contingent on the variables
       manipulated. The moderators are exactly the ones this presumption turns on: visual salience,
       semantic clarity, size, proximity to the person at risk, integration into the task, cost of
       compliance, and — directly on point — that a warning placed *before* procedural instructions
       produces more compliance than one placed *after*. A caveat placed beneath a headline is, in this
       literature's terms, the low-compliance placement. **SECONDARY**.
    4. **Counter-finding, verified, reported here rather than suppressed.** (2014) "Impact of adding a
       limitations section to abstracts of systematic reviews on readers' interpretation: a randomized
       controlled trial." BMC Medical Research Methodology 14: 123. — The nearest direct test of this
       presumption's claim, and it is null. Adding a standardised PRISMA-conformant limitations section to
       systematic-review abstracts did not modify readers' interpretation: confidence in the results, mean
       difference 0.19 (95% CI −0.37 to 0.74), p = 0.50; confidence in the validity of the conclusions 0.07
       (−0.49 to 0.62), p = 0.80; benefit of the experimental intervention 0.12 (−0.42 to 0.44), p = 0.65.
       The paper's own conclusion is that adding a limitations section to a systematic-review abstract may
       not affect expert readers' interpretation. **VERIFIED** — these figures were read directly from the
       article text, not taken from a summary.
    5. Reporting-quality literature on limitations sections (e.g. the PLOS ONE survey of acknowledgment of
       limitations in biomedical studies; Clarke et al., 2024, Social and Personality Psychology Compass).
       — Establishes the upstream problem rather than the downstream one: ~27% of publications mention no
       limitation at all, and where present limitations are frequently superficial or selectively framed.
       **SECONDARY**; the 27% figure is from a search summary and is **DO-NOT-CITE as verified**.

  Strength of support: Weak

  Summary: Evidence that written qualifications are read and act on judgement exists but is weaker and more
    conditional than the presumption assumes. The SPIIN randomised trial shows that prose framing changes
    expert clinicians' conclusions from identical data, and the warnings meta-analyses show that written
    qualifications do change behaviour. But both establish the same conditionality: effect depends on
    salience, placement and reader sophistication, and the going-concern literature adds that only the
    sophisticated segment of the readership reacts at all. Against this, the single closest analogue to the
    estate's practice — adding a standardised limitations paragraph to an abstract and measuring whether
    expert readers' interpretation changed — returned a clean null on all three outcomes. And the warnings
    literature's placement finding is directly adverse to the estate's habit: a qualification placed after
    the headline is in the low-compliance position, whereas the scheduler check's practice of building the
    qualification into the FAIL row is the integrated, high-compliance position the literature endorses.
    The comparative claim proper — that prose caveats are read *at the same rate as* status fields — was
    searched for and no study of it was found in any domain.

  Caveats:
    - Support here is for a weaker proposition than the one filed. "Prose qualifications can change expert
      judgement" is moderately supported. "Prose caveats are read at the same rate as status fields" has no
      supporting evidence found, and the one direct analogue is null.
    - Effect sizes where reported are small. SPIIN's absolute difference was described by the authors as
      small; the warnings meta-analyses report "disparate levels of success."
    - Domain transfer: all four literatures concern human readers of published documents. The estate's
      readership of its own daily reports — human and agent — is not characterised, and the going-concern
      finding implies the answer depends heavily on who that readership is.
    - Publication-bias direction is worth naming: the reporting-quality literature is normative advocacy
      for limitations sections and is not a neutral source on whether they work.
    - Only source 4 was verified in full text. Sources 1, 2, 3 and 5 are SECONDARY at abstract or summary
      level, and no figure from them should be quoted as verified.

  Recommendation: PARTIALLY-SUPPORTED

  Searched: Randomised experiments on abstract framing and limitations sections in biomedicine; audit
    qualification and market reaction; warning comprehension and behavioural-compliance meta-analyses;
    reporting-quality literature on limitations.
  Finding: No existing literature was found that measures the *relative* uptake of a prose qualification
    against a structured status field in the same document — the precise comparison this presumption
    states. The literatures measure prose effects and structured-signal effects separately and never
    against each other.
  Implication: The presumption's comparative limb is untestable against existing literature and would
    require an original measurement. It is also directly actionable without one: the warnings literature's
    placement and integration findings, plus the estate's own scheduler-check counter-example, both point
    to the same remedy — move the load-bearing qualification into the status row rather than beneath it —
    and that remedy does not depend on resolving the comparative question.
  Recommended status: NOVEL (for the comparative rate limb only; the underlying "prose is read and acts on
    judgement" limb is covered by existing literature and is not novel)
