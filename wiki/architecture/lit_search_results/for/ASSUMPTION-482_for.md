The finding is that major discoveries are driven by a new method or instrument rather than by theory, and that "the gaps in our instruments can be just as crucial as the gaps in our knowledge." Supports the claim that an inquiry can be genuinely blocked at the measurement step while appearing to be blocked at the evidence step.

  Strength of support: Moderate
  Summary: The general proposition that an inquiry can be measurement-limited rather than evidence-limited is well established and recently quantified across the full population of scientific breakthroughs; the instrument gap and the knowledge gap are distinct, and the former is the more frequent binding one. Maintenance engineering supplies an independent, validated analogue: diagnosis dominates time-to-restore, and a cheap discriminating measurement is the standard highest-leverage intervention. Both literatures support 482's structure — that identifying six sub-hour measurements which collapse eleven open dispositions is a real and correct form of leverage identification. What the search does not support is the *exclusivity* of the diagnosis. Theory of Constraints, the framework 482 borrows, holds that policy constraints — rules, habits, approval requirements — "are the most common and often underlie other constraints," and that they are harder to see than physical ones precisely because they present as good management. A pipeline that has correctly enumerated its measurements and not taken them for fifteen days is the textbook presentation of a policy constraint misread as an information constraint.
  Caveats: (a) The discovery-tools literature concerns fields where the instrument does not yet exist; 482's measurements are stated to exist and cost under an hour each, which is a materially different situation and weakens the transfer. Where the tool already exists and is not used, the constraint is by construction not instrumental. (b) The MTTR analogy assumes a technician who will act once localization is complete; that assumption is exactly what PRESUMPTION-513 denies, so source 3 supports 482 only conditional on 513 being false. (c) The internal claims — 11 of 14, ~6 measurements, 5 under an hour — are record claims outside search scope and I make no finding on them. (d) TOC sources 4 are practitioner and encyclopaedic material rather than peer-reviewed; they carry framework weight, not empirical weight. (e) Publication bias runs toward the item here: "new instrument enabled discovery" is a far more publishable narrative than "the measurement was available and nobody took it," so the literature's balance likely overstates the measurement-limited case.
  Recommendation: PARTIALLY-SUPPORTED

  PAIR VERDICT (482 vs PRESUMPTION-513): The literature leans toward 513. Both items are supported on their positive content — 482 is right that measurement gaps are a real and common binding constraint, and right that the enumerated tests are cheap and discriminating. But 513's claim is the one with the more directly applicable evidence base: the know-do gap is one of the most heavily documented findings in implementation science, TOC's own doctrine names policy constraints as the most common category, and internal-audit practice treats unimplemented recommendations as a standing structural problem requiring escalation machinery rather than better recommendations. 482's evidence is largely from domains where the instrument did not exist; 513's is from domains where it did and went unused, which is the actual situation described. The two are not mutually exclusive — the measurements can be both correct and irrelevant to throughput — but as a diagnosis of the *binding* constraint, 513 is better supported. Note also that 513's test is asymmetric and cheap: one executed measurement falsifies it outright.
