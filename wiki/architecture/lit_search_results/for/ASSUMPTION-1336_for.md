  Sources:
    1. Loewenstein, W.R. & Kanno, Y., 1966. "Intercellular communication and the control of tissue growth:
       lack of communication between cancer cells." Nature. — The founding observation: electrical coupling
       present in normal hepatocytes was absent in liver tumours, generating the hypothesis that loss of
       gap-junctional intercellular communication (GJIC) is causal in carcinogenesis. This is the historical
       taproot of the coupling-loss gloss. **SECONDARY** — seen described in the 2019 review at
       PMC6721698, not read in the original.
    2. Mathews, J. & Levin, M., 2017. "Gap junctional signaling in pattern regulation: Physiological network
       connectivity instructs growth and form." Developmental Neurobiology 77(5): 643–673. — Directly
       supports the theoretical limb: gap junctions are treated as the computational elements by which cell
       collectives establish connectivity patterns and implement large-scale anatomical pattern; the review
       explicitly covers "cancer as developmental dysregulation" alongside planarian polarity and zebrafish
       fin growth. **SECONDARY** — abstract-level via search result, article not fetched.
    3. Levin, M., 2023. "Bioelectric networks: the cognitive glue enabling evolutionary scaling from
       physiology to mind." Animal Cognition (PMID 37204591). — States the mechanism the assumption
       depends on: bioelectric networks including gap junctions are the "cognitive glue" that scales
       individual cellular homeostatic setpoints into organism-level setpoints, i.e. that scales the
       cognitive light cone. **SECONDARY** — PubMed fetch blocked by reCAPTCHA; abstract content taken
       from search summary.
    4. Chernet, B.T. & Levin, M., 2015. "Long-range gap junctional signaling controls oncogene-mediated
       tumorigenesis in Xenopus laevis embryos." Frontiers in Physiology. — Experimental: disrupting GJC
       within tumours, within remote host tissue, or between host and tumour significantly *lowered* KRAS-
       induced tumour incidence, with the largest suppression from disruption farthest from the oncogene-
       expressing cells; conversely Cx26 overexpression *increased* tumour incidence. Supports the
       non-local, coupling-mediated control of tumorigenesis — but see Caveats, because the sign is the
       opposite of what a simple monotonic coupling-equals-cooperation reading predicts. **SECONDARY**.
    5. (2026) "Sub-millivolt voltage imaging reveals gap junction-mediated bioelectric contact inhibition."
       Nature Communications, s41467-026-76758-z. — Reports that cell–cell contact produces robust,
       cluster-size-dependent suppression of membrane-voltage fluctuations that strictly depends on
       electrical coupling through connexins ("bioelectric contact inhibition"). This is the cleanest
       recent mechanistic support that coupling produces collective-level regulation of individual cells.
       **SECONDARY** — page exceeded fetch limit; content from search summary.
    6. Grodstein, J., Mathews, J. & Levin, M., 2026. "Membrane voltage and connexin expression work

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: The supportive literature for the *directional* limb of this assumption is deep and old. Since
    Loewenstein & Kanno (1966) the loss of gap-junctional coupling has been a documented early event in
    carcinogenesis, and connexins are conventionally described as tumour suppressors. The specifically
    Levin-lab framing — that gap junctions are the "cognitive glue" scaling cellular setpoints into
    organism-level ones, so that decoupling shrinks the effective self — is stated explicitly in Mathews &
    Levin (2017) and Levin (2023), and a 2026 Nature Communications paper supplies a clean mechanism
    (connexin-dependent bioelectric contact inhibition) by which coupling subordinates individual cells to a
    collective. What the literature does **not** support is the *monotonicity* limb. Even sources found on
    the FOR side describe the connexin–cancer relation as stage-dependent and biphasic: down early, up in
    advanced and metastatic disease. The Grodstein–Mathews–Levin preprint does not sit outside this
    literature as an anomaly; it is written as an attempt to resolve a pattern the field already reports.

  Caveats:
    - The strongest counterweight surfaced on the FOR side, not the against side, and is reported here
      rather than suppressed: Chernet & Levin (2015) found that *enhancing* coupling (Cx26 overexpression)
      *increased* tumour incidence and that *disrupting* coupling *reduced* it. Whatever coupling is doing
      in that system, it is not behaving as a monotonic proxy for cooperative multicellularity.
    - "Coupling" is not one variable. GJIC, connexin transcript level, connexin protein localisation, and
      functional dye/electrical coupling dissociate from each other (the 1994 Krutovskikh liver-tumour work
      surfaced in search reports aberrant Cx32 *localisation* without gene mutation). An assumption stated
      over "connexin expression" and an assumption stated over "functional coupling" are not the same
      assumption, and the estate's gloss does not currently distinguish them.
    - The cognitive-light-cone reading is a theoretical framework, not an independently measured quantity.
      No source found operationalises light-cone size such that it could be shown to shrink. Support for it
      is therefore support for a redescription, not for a measurement.
    - Publication-bias note: the tumour-suppressor framing dominated the field for three decades and the
      pro-invasive connexin findings are comparatively recent, so a naive search weights the supportive
      direction. This was visible in the result ordering.
    - Six of seven sources are SECONDARY at the abstract/summary level. Only the existence and framing of
      the Grodstein–Mathews–Levin preprint was verified directly. No figure or effect size from any source
      in this file should be quoted as verified.
