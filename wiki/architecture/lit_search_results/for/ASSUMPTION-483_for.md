  Sources:
    1. "Nine Judges, Two Effective Votes: Correlated Errors Undermine LLM Evaluation Panels." arXiv:2605.29800 (also hosted at Apple Machine Learning Research). — The strongest available source and close to decisive for the item. A panel of nine frontier LLMs from seven distinct model families yields only about two independent votes' worth of information; roughly three-quarters of nominal independence is lost because the models err on the same items. Panel accuracy falls 8-22 points short of what independent voting would give, and the best single judge matches or beats the full panel. This establishes that the dominant correlation channel survives *maximal* architectural and vendor separation — far stronger separation than removing a shared read channel.
    2. "Correlated Errors in Large Language Models." arXiv:2506.07962. — Independent corroboration that error correlation across LLMs is a general, measurable property rather than an artifact of any one evaluation design.
    3. "How Independent are Large Language Models? A Statistical Framework for Auditing Behavioral Entanglement and Reweighting Verifier Ensembles." arXiv:2604.07650. — Names the mechanism the item requires: "many LLMs share substantial portions of their training pipelines, overlapping pre-training corpora, shared alignment procedures, and widespread knowledge distillation, which can induce hidden behavioral dependencies." Shared pretraining is identified as a first-order correlation source, which is 483's claim that the read channel was not the dominant one.
    4. "CARE: Confounder-Aware Aggregation for Reliable LLM Evaluation." arXiv:2603.00039. — Attributes correlated judge error to shared latent confounders (verbosity, stylistic preference, training artifacts) and shows that standard aggregation can amplify rather than damp systematic mistakes. Supports the item's implied ranking: the channels that matter are latent and shared, not procedural.
    5. van Rooyen, S. et al. (1998). "Effect of blinding and unmasking on the quality of peer review: a randomized trial." JAMA. PMID 9676666. — The named source, retrieved and confirmed. 527 BMJ manuscripts, two reviewers each, randomized to blinded or unblinded. Blinding succeeded for 73% of reviewers and produced a quality difference of 3.5 vs 3.1 on a 5-point scale — real but small, with no significant difference in publication recommendation. This is direct empirical support for 483's characterisation of a procedural blinding change as a genuine but *minor* intervention, and specifically supports not re-running a large historical record on the strength of it.
    6. van Rooyen, S. et al. (1999). "Effect of blinding and unmasking on the quality of peer review." J Gen Intern Med / PMID 10571708. — Follow-up in a second venue reaching a consistent conclusion; strengthens the finding that removing one identity channel changes review quality marginally.

  Strength of support: Strong (on the ranking claim); Moderate (on the specific "at least four" enumeration)
  Summary: The item's core assertion — that the read channel was not the dominant correlation source between two LLM search agents — is well supported and arguably understated. The most direct evidence is that nine models from seven different families, sharing no read channel at all, still collapse to about two independent votes; whatever produces that collapse is upstream of any procedural coupling C2A2 could remove. The auditing literature names the responsible channels explicitly as overlapping pre-training corpora, shared alignment procedures, and distillation, and the confounder work adds shared stylistic and length preferences. The blinding literature supplies the correct calibration for the procedural fix: van Rooyen's randomized trial found masking improved review quality by roughly 0.4 points on a five-point scale and changed no publication recommendations, which is the profile of a real but non-dominant channel. Both halves of the item's recommendation follow: relabelling from "independence established" to "read-channel independence enforced" is the accurate description, and re-running a 905-pair record on the strength of a minor channel removal is not warranted by the effect sizes involved.
  Caveats: (a) The item's specific enumeration — "the weakest of at least four" — is a claim about C2A2's internal architecture that the literature can rank but not count; I found support for at least three named channels (shared pretraining corpus, shared alignment/RLHF procedure, shared stylistic confounders) plus the read channel, which is consistent with four but does not verify it. (b) van Rooyen's trial concerns human reviewers and author-identity masking; the transfer to LLM agents reading a common source set is by analogy of role, not of mechanism, and the analogy is loose. (c) The strongest sources are 2025-2026 arXiv preprints; I did not confirm peer-review status for 2605.29800, 2603.00039, 2604.07650, or 2506.07962. (d) The item's practical conclusion is a decision not to spend effort re-measuring. That is the direction in which confirmation bias operates cheaply, so the finding should not be treated as licence to stop measuring correlation altogether — sources 1 and 3 both argue the opposite, that residual correlation must be measured rather than assumed away. The in-house test named in the item (source-citation overlap under full blocking) is the right measurement and is endorsed by this literature.
