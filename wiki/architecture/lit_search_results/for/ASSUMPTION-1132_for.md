  Sources:
    1. IAASB (2009, conformed/amended). *ISA 530: Audit Sampling*, International Standard on Auditing. — Requires that "the auditor shall perform audit procedures to obtain evidence that the population from which the sample is drawn is complete," and states explicitly that conclusions cannot be drawn about a period unless the auditor is satisfied the population includes all relevant items from throughout that period. This is the governing normative statement for exactly the failure mode described: if two calls four minutes apart disagree about what the population *is*, no conclusion about coverage can be drawn from a sample of either.

    2. Lindeman, M., Stark, P.B. (2012). "A Gentle Introduction to Risk-Limiting Audits." IEEE Security & Privacy 10(5): 42–49. — Foundational treatment of audits that provide a stated statistical guarantee. Critically, RLA practice makes the *selection itself* reproducible by construction: a publicly announced random seed plus a published open-source PRNG and a fixed ballot manifest, so that "anyone using the same open-source pseudo-random number program and plugging in the same seed will get the same list of ballots." The reproducibility of the draw is treated as constitutive of the guarantee, not an implementation nicety. (See also Stark, P.B. (2008), "Conservative statistical post-election audits," Annals of Applied Statistics, for the underlying risk-limiting framework.)

    3. Groves, R.M., et al. (2009). *Survey Methodology*, 2nd ed., Wiley; and Groves, R.M., Lyberg, L. (2010). "Total Survey Error: Past, Present, and Future." Public Opinion Quarterly 74(5): 849–879. — The Total Survey Error framework isolates *coverage error* as the mismatch between the target population and the sampling frame actually used, and notes that coverage error "exists before the sample is drawn and thus is not a problem arising because we do a sample survey." Directly supports the claim that the defect here is in the frame (the queue), not in the sample of six — and that no amount of care in reviewing the six repairs it.

    4. Zheng, S., Adams, B., Hassan, A.E. (2025). "On Build Hermeticity in Bazel-based Build Systems." IEEE Software 42(6). — Documents empirically that when the inputs to a repeatable process are not pinned, results vary between invocations in ways that go unnoticed because the process itself reports success. Supports the general form: a selector whose inputs are not fixed produces non-comparable outputs across runs.

    5. Lamb, C., Zacchiroli, S. (2021). "Reproducible Builds: Increasing the Integrity of Software Supply Chains." IEEE Software 39(2): 62–70. — Establishes determinism-of-output-given-input as an *integrity* property rather than a convenience, and distinguishes the two sources of irreproducibility (unstable inputs vs. non-deterministic tooling). The selector instability described in ASSUMPTION-1132 is the first category; the framing supports treating it as an integrity defect rather than noise.

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: The strongest support for this item is normative rather than empirical, and it is unambiguous. Auditing standards (ISA 530) make population completeness a precondition of drawing any conclusion from a sample; if two calls minutes apart disagree about the population, the precondition simply is not met and no coverage claim survives, regardless of how carefully the six sampled items were reviewed. Election risk-limiting-audit design goes further and makes the *selection procedure itself* publicly reproducible — same seed, same published PRNG, same manifest yields the same list — treating reproducibility of the draw as part of what makes the guarantee a guarantee rather than an assertion. Total-survey-error theory locates the defect precisely where ASSUMPTION-1132 locates it: coverage error is a property of the frame, existing before the sample is drawn, and not remediable downstream. On the software side, the reproducible-builds and order-dependent-test literatures independently establish that non-determinism in what gets selected voids the guarantees of the techniques consuming that selection, and treat determinism-given-inputs as an integrity property. The claim's inferential move — from "the selector is unstable" to "no run's review supports a coverage statement" — is exactly the move these frameworks license.

  Caveats: (a) The 90–96% residue figure is a local measurement; no literature was found that addresses it and none could, so that half of the claim is supported only in the weak sense that flaky/non-deterministic inspection queues are known to surface large volumes of non-signal. (b) The auditing and election-audit standards are normative frameworks written for adversarial, high-stakes, human-executed audits; their applicability to an internal automated QC pass is by analogy, and a proportionate-assurance argument could reasonably hold that a lower bar is appropriate here. (c) None of the sources quantifies *how much* selector instability voids *how much* coverage — the frameworks are threshold-shaped ("the population must be complete"), not graduated, which may overstate the severity of a partial overlap (nine shared of 31 and 24) relative to what a statistical treatment would say. (d) Sources 6 and 7 are cited by title/venue with unverified author lists and should be re-verified before external use. (e) A drifting frame is not necessarily a *biased* frame; the literature on coverage error concerns systematic mismatch with a target population, whereas the observed instability could in principle be unbiased churn — this distinction was not resolved by the search and is a genuine gap in the FOR case.
