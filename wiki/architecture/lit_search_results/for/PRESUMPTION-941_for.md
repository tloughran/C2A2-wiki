  Sources:
    1. Yorio, P.L. & Moore, S.M. (2018), "Examining Factors that Influence the Existence of Heinrich's
       Safety Triangle Using Site-Specific H&S Data from More than 25,000 Establishments," Risk
       Analysis 38(4):839-852, doi:10.1111/risa.12869.
       [VERIFIED — article body fetched and read this run via the PMC author manuscript (PMC6238149).
       Abstract, introduction, and results-discussion passages read verbatim. This is the only
       primary-source verification in the item.]
       — THE DECISIVE SOURCE, AND IT RUNS AGAINST THE PRESUMPTION WHILE SUPPLYING ITS BEST FOR LIMB.
       The study asked three questions of 25,000+ establishments over 13 years: are more lower-severity
       incidents significantly associated with the probability of a fatal event over time; do the
       effects decrease as severity decreases, taking the form of a triangle; and do different methods
       of delineating severity affect the result. Verbatim: "The answer to all three questions was yes
       with the triangle form being dependent upon how severity was delineated." Lower-severity events
       ARE predictive of subsequent fatality. An averted harm carries information about future realised
       harm, at scale, measured.
       THE FOR LIMB, and it is real but small. Verbatim from the results discussion: "a one unit
       increase in the most severe injury severity category (i.e., permanently disabling injuries) had
       the largest effect and a one unit increase in the least severe category (i.e., reportable
       noninjuries) had the smallest effect." The near-miss tier carries the LEAST per-event
       information of the four classes studied. And the paper's own finding complicates the triangle
       further: "a one unit increase in reportable injuries … had a larger influence on the probability
       of a subsequent year fatal event than the effect of days lost injuries," an inversion which
       "somewhat undermine[s] the premise contained in the triangle structure." In the fully controlled
       model, "only the odds ratio for permanent disabling injuries and reportable, noninjury events
       were significant."
       WEIGHT: Strong evidence that averted-harm events carry signal (against 941). Moderate evidence
       for a bounded triage principle (weakly for 941): per event, the no-injury tier carries the
       smallest effect, so treating every averted harm as equal in weight to a realised one is not
       supported either. What is NOT supported by any reading is CLOSING the item.

  Summary: The FOR direction turned up one large, verified study and it settles the empirical question
  against the presumption. Yorio & Moore, across more than 25,000 establishments over 13 years, found
  that lower-severity events including no-injury events are significantly associated with the
  probability of a fatal event in a subsequent year — an averted harm carries information about
  realised harm, measured at scale. The genuine support the search found is narrower than the
  presumption and worth carrying: within that same study, the no-injury tier had the SMALLEST per-event
  effect of the four classes, and the triangle's shape depended on how severity was delineated, with
  one inversion the authors say "somewhat undermine[s]" the classical structure. Separately, the Baker
  Panel's finding that BP's personal-injury rates were not predictive of process-safety performance is
  real support for a hazard-class boundary: an averted harm in one class tells you nothing about
  another. Neither finding reaches the estate's case. ASSUMPTION-1294's over-deletion is a within-class
  near-miss — same mechanism, same asset, outcome differing only by luck — which is precisely the
  configuration Yorio & Moore measured and found predictive. And the register's own standing caution
  applies to the remedy rather than to the diagnosis: Hallowell et al.'s TRIR result, already held and
  verified, warns that converting absences into counted events can install random variation where an
  honest silence stood. The defensible position the search supports is therefore neither "close it" nor
  "count everything": it is that a within-class averted harm opens an item whose weight is set by
  POTENTIAL consequence, and that the item's value is the mechanism it exposes, not its contribution
  to a rate.
