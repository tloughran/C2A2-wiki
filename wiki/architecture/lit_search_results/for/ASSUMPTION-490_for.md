  Sources:
    1. NIST, "Metrological Traceability: Frequently Asked Questions and NIST Policy." nist.gov/metrology/metrological-traceability. — The authoritative statement of the arbitration rule the item needs. Metrological traceability is defined as the property of a measurement result whereby it can be related to a reference through a documented unbroken chain of calibrations, each contributing to measurement uncertainty. The rule this yields is unambiguous and directly answers the item's question: authority does not come from a reading, it comes from the chain. Two readings with no chain to a common reference cannot arbitrate between themselves at all.
    2. Eurachem/CITAC Guide, "Metrological Traceability in Chemical Measurement" (2019 EN, P1). — Formal guidance establishing that comparability of results requires an unbroken calibration chain to a common higher standard, and that in its absence results are not merely uncertain but *incommensurable*. This is the strong form of support for the item: the correct disposition of three disagreeing counts is not to pick one but to recognise that no basis for picking exists until a reference definition is fixed.
    3. Acute Care Testing, "Establishment of metrological traceability for quality control materials and calibrators"; ATT Metrology Solutions, "What Is Traceability in Metrology?"; Process Sensing, "Metrological Traceability." — Practical statement of the failure mode: "two instruments calibrated at different labs, using different reference standards of unknown origin, may produce readings that cannot be meaningfully compared." Applies directly — QC, verification, and nightly are three instruments with no shared reference standard, and the item's observation that a pending decision rests on which is right identifies the precise point at which incommensurability becomes costly.
    4. GMP out-of-tolerance practice (gmpsop, "Instrument calibration procedure for pharmaceutical industry"; gxpready, "Why Calibration Programs Fail GMP Audits"; NIST GMP 11, "Assignment and Adjustment of Calibration Intervals"). — Supplies the procedural rule for the item's second component. When an instrument is found out of tolerance the required action is to stop its use, raise a deviation, and perform a retrospective impact assessment identifying every measurement made since last calibration. Noting the out-of-tolerance condition and continuing without that assessment is explicitly described as a serious inspection finding. The inflated declared length_actual_words masking a 0.77x under-run is an out-of-tolerance declaration on a measuring instrument, and the prescribed response is retrospective assessment of every dependent figure, not a forward-only fix.

  Strength of support: Moderate-to-Strong
  Summary: On the narrow question the item was queued for, metrology gives a clear and well-established answer, and it is not the answer of picking a winner. Authority is a property of a documented chain to an external reference, not of any instrument's reading; where no such chain exists, disagreeing instruments are formally incommensurable and no internal arbitration rule can be constructed from them. The applicable rule for C2A2 is therefore: fix the counting definition first — explicitly, including the Notes-stripping rule the item names — designate it as the reference, and re-derive all three counts against it. That is exactly the item's proposed in-house test (re-derive all three counts over one frozen snapshot with the Notes-stripping rule stated), and metrology endorses it as the only procedure that can resolve the disagreement rather than merely adjudicate it. The declared-length component has its own well-specified rule from GMP practice: an instrument found out of tolerance triggers a retrospective impact assessment over every measurement taken since the last known-good calibration, and proceeding without one is a recognised compliance failure. Both halves of the item thus have named, standard procedures attached; neither has been performed.
  Caveats: (a) Physical metrology's apparatus assumes stable units and quantifiable uncertainty budgets. Word counts under differing inclusion rules are a *definitional* disagreement, not a precision disagreement — the three instruments may each be perfectly accurate about different quantities. The metrology transfer is of the arbitration principle (an external reference is required) rather than of the uncertainty machinery, and the definitional framing arguably belongs to PREMISE-105 rather than here. (b) A consequence the item does not draw: if the three counts measure different constructs, then designating one as the reference does not make the other two wrong, and the tier-calibration decision may need to specify which construct it depends on before any count can be authoritative. (c) The GMP sources are practitioner and regulatory-guidance material; NIST and Eurachem carry the weight. (d) The internal figures (5 / 15 / 20, 0.77x) are outside search scope and I make no finding on them. (e) Freezing a definition is cheap but not free: it invalidates comparability with the historical record collected under the old rules, which is the standard cost of a definitional break and is why official statistics publish revision policies alongside such changes (see PRESUMPTION-507).
