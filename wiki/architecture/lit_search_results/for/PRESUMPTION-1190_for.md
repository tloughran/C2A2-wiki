    2. Shaw, W. M. 1985. "Critical thresholds in co-citation graphs." JASIS 36(1): 38–43 (via IDEAS/RePEc). [snippet only]. Shaw found that at both high and low co-citation strengths the partition co-citation produces "may be statistically invalid", and that it is interpretable only in a narrow range. Relevance: analogous support for (b). Co-occurrence links need validation before being read as intellectual structure.
    3. General citation-motive critiques: arXiv physics/0701012, "The Rise and Rise of Citation Analysis"; SciELO blog (2014) on a taxonomy of citation motives; Surrey repository abstract on authority-citing. [snippet only, secondary]. Citation motives are unknowable from counts, and ceremonial or authority citation is common. Relevance: weak, indirect grounding. Co-occurrence signals can reflect social or venue factors, not substantive linkage.

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: BBS's own instructions confirm that commentators are selected and invited by editors, with author input and systematic searches, so that a target article draws a broad, multidisciplinary set of responses. Co-commentary on one target is therefore partly produced by the venue's design. Bibliometric work also warns that co-occurrence structures (co-citation) are valid only under limited conditions and that citation motives are opaque. Together these support the item's concern that shared-target co-commentary alone is weak evidence of convergence between traditions without content-level analysis.

  Caveats:
    - The BBS instructions found come from older volumes. Current invitation practice was not verified.
    - Co-commentary is not co-citation. Transferring co-citation validity results is an analogy.
    - Commentaries can still converge in substance. The literature supports "the format is a confound", not "there is no connection".
    - The core co-citation sources (Small 1973; White & McCain 1998) were not reached.
