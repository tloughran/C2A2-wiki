    1. Gentner, D. (1983). "Structure-Mapping: A Theoretical Framework for Analogy." Cognitive Science 7(2):155-170; and Gentner, D. & Markman, A.B. (1997). "Structure Mapping in Analogy and Similarity." American Psychologist 52(1):45-56. — Structure-mapping theory's central claim is that an analogy is characterised by similarity at the level of *relations between objects*, not at the level of the objects or their *attributes*; validity depends on shared systems of interconnected relations. "Abstract," "non-physical" and "causally instructive" are attribute-level descriptors, so on the field's own standard three shared descriptors do not constitute a mapping — they constitute the weakest available evidence for one. This is direct, named, external support for the presumption's core distinction.
    2. Gentner, D., Rattermann, M.J., & Forbus, K.D. (1993). "The Roles of Similarity in Transfer: Separating Retrievability from Inferential Soundness." Cognitive Psychology 25(4):524-575. — Experimentally dissociates the two: participants were reminded of surface-similar cases (which were not useful) while relationally similar cases went unretrieved, yet on explicit rating the same participants judged the surface matches poor and the relational matches inferentially sound. The mechanism that *produces* an analogy in the mind is therefore not the mechanism that makes it sound, and the two must be evaluated separately. This is the empirical basis for requiring an explicit transfer-condition check rather than trusting the salience of the match — precisely the remedy the in-house adjunct proposes.
    3. Markman, A.B., & Gentner, D. (1993). "Splitting the Differences: A Structural Alignment View of Similarity." Journal of Memory and Language 32:517-535; and Markman, A.B. & Gentner, D. (1996). "Commonalities and Differences in Similarity Comparisons." Memory & Cognition 24(2):235-249. — Alignable differences (differences that occupy corresponding roles in the common structure) are far more salient and reduce judged similarity more than nonalignable differences; in the 1996 stimuli, nonalignable-difference items were chosen as more similar on 88% of trials. The consequence for the presumption: judged similarity systematically *under-weights* the differences that do not line up, so a comparison resting on three shared attributes will feel stronger than it is, and the differences most likely to break the transfer are the ones least likely to be noticed.
