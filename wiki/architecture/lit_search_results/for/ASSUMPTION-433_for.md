  Summary: A substantial empirical literature on inventory record inaccuracy (IRI) supports the claim's core premise: manually maintained or carried-forward record counts routinely diverge from physical reality, with studies finding 50–70% of records inaccurate at any moment. The literature consistently treats the fresh physical count as the authoritative measurement and the running tally as an error-accumulating estimate, matching the claim's framing of "110 is the measurement, 116 was the estimate." "Phantom inventory" — records showing items that never existed or were double-counted — is a well-documented mechanism producing exactly this signature (recorded > actual) without loss. Clinical registry audit literature similarly treats periodic verification counts as the corrective standard against administrative tallies.

  Caveats: The literature supports drift as the most common explanation, not a guarantee: recorded-greater-than-actual discrepancies in inventory contexts are also caused by genuine shrinkage (loss/theft). The "no items were lost" clause is an inference the literature makes probable, not certain; only item-level reconciliation (the queued empirical test) can confirm it. Support is strongest by analogy — the studies concern retail/clinical inventories, not agent-maintained backlog files.
