    1. Walters, W. H., & Wilder, E. I., 2023. "Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT." Scientific Reports 13, doi:10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5. — 636 citations across 84 generated literature reviews on 42 topics. 55% of GPT-3.5 and 18% of GPT-4 citations wholly fabricated; among the *real* citations, 43% (GPT-3.5) and 24% (GPT-4) contain substantive errors. The high substantive-error rate among genuinely existing references is the direct support for the class of failure in 1136: the paper is real, the attribution is not.
    2. Linardon, J., et al., 2025. "Influence of Topic Familiarity and Prompt Specificity on Citation Fabrication in Mental Health Research Using Large Language Models: Experimental Study." JMIR Mental Health 2025;12:e80371, PMID 41223407. — 19.9% of GPT-4o citations wholly fabricated; 45.4% of the apparently-real remainder carried bibliographic errors; fabrication rate rose to 28–29% for low-visibility topics and above 80% for low-income-country content, versus 6% for high-visibility topics. The systematic topic-conditioning of the rate is what makes recurrence predictable rather than random — direct support for sub-claim (i).
    3. Magesh, V., Surani, F., Dahl, M., Suzgun, M., Manning, C. D., & Ho, D. E., 2025. "Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools." Journal of Empirical Legal Studies (arXiv:2405.20362). — Measured retrieval-augmented, vendor-guarded systems and still found 17% (Lexis+ AI) and 33% (Westlaw AI-AR) hallucination rates against 43% for bare GPT-4. Separates *correctness* from *groundedness* and names the residual class "misgrounded". This is the clearest published support for sub-claim (ii): retrieval-stage failure survives the addition of a real corpus and is a distinct error type, not a smaller amount of the same error.
    4. Liu, N. F., Zhang, T., & Liang, P., 2023. "Evaluating Verifiability in Generative Search Engines." Findings of EMNLP 2023 (arXiv:2304.09848). — Across Bing Chat, NeevaAI, perplexity.ai and YouChat, only 74.5% of citations support the sentence they are attached to. All four systems are retrieval-backed, so the errors are attribution errors over real retrieved documents — again supporting the retrieval-stage/generation-stage distinction.
    5. Manakul, P., Liusie, A., & Gales, M. J. F., 2023. "SelfCheckGPT: Zero-Resource Black-Box Hallucination Detection for Generative Large Language Models." EMNLP 2023. — The sampling-consistency premise is that hallucinations diverge across resamples while known facts reproduce. Reported failure analyses of this method note the converse case directly: a model can be confidently and consistently wrong, regenerating the same unsupported claim across samples and scoring as non-hallucinated. This is the mechanism by which a specific fabrication becomes reproducible across independent runs, as 1136 claims.

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: Two of the three sub-claims are well supported. Recurrence is not surprising: Walters & Wilder (2023) and Linardon et al. (2025) both find that a large fraction of *non-fabricated* references still carry substantive bibliographic errors (24–45%), and Linardon et al. show the rate is systematically conditioned on topic visibility rather than randomly distributed — a low-visibility source will therefore fail repeatedly at a predictable rate, which is what "reproducible" means operationally. The self-consistency literature supplies the mechanism: the assumption behind SelfCheckGPT is that hallucinations vary across resamples, and its documented failure case is exactly a model that reproduces the same unsupported claim consistently, defeating consistency-based detection. The retrieval/generation distinction is supported strongly by Magesh et al. (2025), whose correctness-vs-groundedness split shows that grounding a system in a real corpus reduces but does not eliminate attribution error and produces a qualitatively different residue ("misgrounded"), and by Liu, Zhang & Liang (2023), where a quarter of citations from retrieval-backed search engines fail to support their sentence. The third sub-claim — that author-list verification is an effective guard — is the weak link: tools implementing exactly this check exist and name the exact failure mode, but no peer-reviewed evaluation of author-list verification as a guard was located, only vendor-reported accuracy.

  Caveats: (a) "Reproducible" in 1136 means the same fabrication recurred across four runs in five days; the literature supports *rate stability and topic-conditioning*, which is a weaker and different claim than run-to-run identity of a specific fabricated attribution. No study located directly measures whether an individual fabricated author list recurs verbatim across independent sessions — that would need an internal experiment, not a literature search. (b) The published rates all predate or sit alongside rapid model turnover; Walters & Wilder's GPT-3.5-vs-GPT-4 gap (55% → 18%) shows the base rate moves fast, so absolute numbers should not be carried forward. (c) Sub-claim (iii) rests on gray literature and should be treated as unestablished until an internal measurement is run. (d) Several search hits in this area (arXiv:2603.03299 "How LLMs Cite and Why It Matters"; arXiv:2604.03159 "BibTeX Citation Hallucinations in Scientific Publishing Agents"; arXiv:2604.03173 "Detecting and Correcting Reference Hallucinations") appear topically on point but their author lists and venues could not be confirmed and they are excluded [unverified — title/venue not confirmed].
