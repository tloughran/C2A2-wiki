  Sources:
    1. Belkin, N.J., Oddy, R.N. & Brooks, H.M. (1982). "ASK for Information Retrieval: Part I. Background and Theory." Journal of Documentation 38(2):61-71 (Part II, 38(3):145-164). — The Anomalous State of Knowledge hypothesis is the closest thing in the literature to a direct statement of 14b's point. Belkin et al. hold that an information need arises precisely from a recognised anomaly in the seeker's state of knowledge, and that "in general, the user is unable to specify precisely what is needed to resolve that anomaly." Their whole design programme is a response to that inability: the retrieval system is built to elicit and structure an under-specified need rather than to require a well-formed query as the price of entry. Transposed onto REVISE-251, the intake rule asks 14b to do the one thing the ASK hypothesis says an inquirer characteristically cannot do — state the resolving quantity in advance of resolution — and it attaches a sanction (no search) to the failure. This is direct theoretical support for the presumption's negative claim, and it comes from the discipline whose object is exactly this gate.
    2. Taylor, R.S. (1968). "Question-Negotiation and Information Seeking in Libraries." College & Research Libraries 29(3):178-194. — Taylor's four-level model distinguishes the visceral need (real but unexpressed), the conscious need (felt but ill-formed), the formalised need (statable), and the compromised need (as put to the system). The model's operational moral is that the formalised statement is an *output* of negotiation with the collection and the intermediary, not a precondition of access to them; a reference interaction that demanded a formalised question at the door would systematically turn away visceral and conscious needs. That is structurally identical to REVISE-251's rule, which requires a formalised settling quantity before a search may be consumed. Bibliometric review of the paper's reception (Information Processing & Management, 2013) records the four-level model as its most-cited contribution, so this is a load-bearing rather than incidental result in the field.
    3. Kraemer, H.C., Mintz, J., Noda, A., Tinklenberg, J. & Yesavage, J.A. (2006). "Caution Regarding the Use of Pilot Studies to Guide Power Calculations for Study Proposals." Archives of General Psychiatry 63(5):484-489. — This is the best-documented real-world instance of the exact circularity 14b describes: funders and review boards require a sample-size justification, the justification requires an effect-size estimate, and the effect size is what the study is being proposed to measure. Kraemer et al. show quantitatively that using a small pilot's effect-size estimate to power the main study is not merely imprecise but systematically misleading, because the estimate's own sampling distribution makes both gross under- and over-powering likely. The relevance is twofold: it confirms that gates of this shape do arise and persist in mature, heavily-reviewed institutional processes, and it shows that satisfying such a gate with a *pre-search guess* is worse than useless — it manufactures a number that the gate then treats as information. That is the specific failure mode a "name the settling quantity or lose your search" rule would incentivise at 14b's intake.
    4. Ferdinands, G., Schram, R., de Bruin, J., Bagheri, A., Oberski, D.L., Tummers, L., Teijema, J.J. & van de Schoot, R. (2023). "Performance of active learning models for screening prioritization in systematic reviews: a simulation study into the Average Time to Discover relevant records." Systematic Reviews 12:100. — The screening-prioritisation literature names 14b's problem as the cold-start problem and treats it as a design constraint rather than a user failing. The simulation results establish that a screening pipeline can begin with no labelled prior knowledge at all, but that without priors the model must fall back on effectively random ordering, and that even a very small quantity of prior labels sharply improves early discovery of relevant records. The design response in this literature is uniformly to *seed* the process cheaply and let the ranking improve as evidence arrives — never to refuse to screen items whose relevance signal is not statable in advance. This is applied support for the presumption's implied remedy: provisional entry with revision, not exclusion at the gate.
    5. Boudreau, K.J., Guinan, E.C., Lakhani, K.R. & Riedl, C. (2016). "Looking Across and Looking Beyond the Knowledge Frontier: Intellectual Distance, Novelty, and Resource Allocation in Science." Management Science 62(10):2765-2783. — Using a randomised assignment of evaluators to proposals at a leading research university (2,130 evaluator-proposal pairs), the authors find that evaluators systematically score *more novel* proposals lower. This speaks directly to 14b's stated risk-if-wrong: an intake filter that rewards articulable, already-quantified questions will preferentially admit items that are close to what is already known and preferentially exclude the ones whose value per search is highest. The mechanism the paper identifies (evaluators discount what they cannot readily assess against existing knowledge) is the same mechanism a settling-quantity requirement would install procedurally rather than psychologically. It supplies the false-negative half of the presumption, which the ASK and cold-start sources do not.
    6. PCAOB AS 2810 / ISA 450 and ISA 320 (materiality) as documented practice. — Auditing standards face the same structural problem — the quantity that governs evaluation (materiality, and the aggregation threshold for accumulating misstatements) must be set at planning, before the evidence that would fix it exists — and the standards' answer is explicitly *provisional setting plus mandatory revision*: materiality is determined at planning on estimated figures and revised when the auditor becomes aware of information that would have led to a different figure, with the accumulated-misstatement evaluation performed at the end. No auditing standard withholds procedures from an account because the auditor could not name the settling amount in advance. This is documented professional practice supporting the presumption's positive alternative: where the gate quantity is a product of the work, the discipline is applied by requiring the quantity to be *recorded and revised*, not by gating access to the work.

  Strength of support: Strong

  Summary: Supported, and EXTERNALLY ANCHORED (named external referents: Belkin, Oddy & Brooks 1982 in Journal of Documentation; Taylor 1968 in College & Research Libraries; Kraemer et al. 2006 in Archives of General Psychiatry; Ferdinands et al. 2023 in Systematic Reviews; Boudreau et al. 2016 in Management Science; PCAOB AS 2810 / ISA 320 and ISA 450 as standards texts). Information science has a named, fifty-year-old theory whose central claim is 14b's claim: the inquirer cannot in general specify what would resolve the anomaly that created the need, and systems must be designed to elicit rather than to demand that specification (Belkin et al. 1982; Taylor 1968). Clinical-trial methodology supplies the documented instance of the same circular gate surviving inside a heavily-reviewed institution, together with quantitative evidence that a pre-search guess offered to satisfy such a gate actively misleads (Kraemer et al. 2006). The screening literature names the condition — cold start — and its remedy is cheap seeding with revision, never exclusion (Ferdinands et al. 2023). Boudreau et al. 2016 supplies the false-negative cost that 14b's risk-if-wrong asserts, with randomised evidence that evaluation filters penalise novelty. Auditing standards independently show the accepted institutional solution to a gate quantity produced by the gated step: set it provisionally, record it, revise it — which is precisely the amendment the presumption implies REVISE-251 needs, and is a weaker and cheaper rule than the one proposed.

  Caveats: (a) The support is for the presumption's *negative* claim (the rule as stated demands what the search supplies) and for its *remedy* (provisional entry with revision). It is not support for the stronger reading that no intake discipline is possible. Every source above is compatible with — and Ferdinands et al. positively recommends — requiring a *provisional* or *candidate* settling quantity at intake, which would satisfy most of REVISE-251's stated purpose at near-zero false-negative cost. If REVISE-251 is read charitably as already permitting provisional quantities, the presumption's force drops considerably. (b) The settling quantity 14b names — the fraction of the last N dispositioned items whose settling quantity was nameable at filing time — is not measured by any source here, and cannot be: it is an in-house count. The literature establishes that the fraction is *plausibly low* in inquiry generally; it does not establish 14b's specific band (above 2/3 vs. below 1/3). The in-house retrospective count remains the decisive test and none of this support substitutes for it. (c) Domain transfer: Belkin and Taylor concern human information seekers with genuine ignorance of their own need state. A presumption-detecting agent is differently placed — it has just constructed the item and may have better access to what would settle it than a library patron has to their visceral need. The transfer is by structural analogy (a formalised specification demanded before the process that formalises it), and the analogy would weaken if 14b's presumptions are in fact typically accompanied by an obvious candidate metric. 14b's own three counter-instances (NUREG/CR-5485 dependence betas, ISA 450 denominators, alert-fatigue costings) are the strongest evidence here and they are internal to the pipeline, not literature. (d) Publication bias runs mildly *against* this item's support in one respect: the cold-start literature is written by tool builders with an interest in showing their tools work from a cold start, so the "priors are not required" finding is the one they are motivated to report. It runs mildly *for* the item in another: Kraemer et al. and Boudreau et al. are both critical findings published against institutional interest, which is the harder direction to publish. (e) The standards citation (item 6) is documented practice, not empirical research; it shows what a mature profession decided, not that the decision is optimal. It is included because 14b's rule is explicitly modelled on audit practice, which makes the profession's own handling of the same circularity directly relevant. (f) Search scope: six targeted verification searches, one per source. Two adjacent literatures were identified and not exhausted — the pre-registration debate on exploratory versus confirmatory research (where the same "state your hypothesis first" gate is contested), and the triage literature on the cost of undertriage at intake. Both would likely add applied corroboration rather than change direction. Preliminary-to-moderate scope; a broader search is recommended if this item's disposition turns on the false-negative magnitude rather than on the existence of the circularity.
