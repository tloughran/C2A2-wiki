  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: There is a real and well-established body of support for the *function-allocation* reading of this presumption — that a monitor's specified obligation terminates at faithful detection and reporting. Formal methods draws the line explicitly: runtime verification monitors are defined as passive verdict-producers that refrain from modifying system behaviour, with modification belonging to the separate discipline of runtime enforcement. Governance frameworks encode the same split, with NIST CSF 2.0 treating DETECT and RESPOND as separate Functions carrying separate outcomes. Two regulated domains go further and make the separation legally or professionally binding: under ISA 265 the auditor's duty is complete on written, timely communication of a deficiency, and under 31 U.S.C. §5318(g) a SAR filer's obligation is discharged on accurate filing with statutory immunity regardless of what any authority subsequently does. Process-industry alarm management (ANSI/ISA-18.2) supplies partial support from a different angle, recognising a legitimate non-actionable "alert"/"event" class whose whole purpose is awareness and record, and clinical decision support research supplies the methodological analogue by scoring alert appropriateness independently of override.

  Caveats: (a) Every supporting source establishes the claim as a statement about *allocated obligation*, not about *delivered outcome* — none asserts that a monitoring system delivers the safety or governance benefit its existence is justified by, absent a responder. The claim is supported de jure, not de facto. (b) ANSI/ISA-18.2's support is conditional and narrow: the standard sanctions non-actionable annunciation only when it is classified and routed as an alert or event, and its defining criterion for an *alarm* is precisely that a defined operator action exists; presenting non-actionable output through an alarm channel is what the standard treats as the pathology. If C2A2's watchdog output is framed as an alarm rather than an alert, this source stops supporting and starts constraining. (c) The engineering-ethics source supports the claim only in its first stage: reporting to the immediate employer or manager discharges the duty *initially*, but the Engineering Council guidance is explicit that where internal reporting "has not resolved the concern," the engineer is obliged to escalate, including externally. Persistent non-response is treated there as a trigger for a *new* obligation on the reporter, not as a neutral condition. (d) Domain-transfer risk is substantial: the ISA 265 and SAR analogues both rest on an institutional recipient with an independent legal duty to receive and act. Neither addresses the case where the reporting channel has no attending party at all. (e) The clinical decision support sources measure appropriateness on individual alerts; they do not license the aggregate inference across an extended unheeded series.

  Recommendation: PARTIALLY-SUPPORTED
