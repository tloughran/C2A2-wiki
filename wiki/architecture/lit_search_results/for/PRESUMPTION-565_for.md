    1. Wang, R.Y., & Strong, D.M. (1996). "Beyond Accuracy: What Data Quality Means to Data Consumers." Journal of Management Information Systems 12(4):5-33. — The empirically derived data-quality framework separates *intrinsic* dimensions (accuracy, objectivity, believability, reputation) as distinct constructs. Believability and reputation are consumer perceptions of a source; accuracy is a property of the data. A file's own disclaimer bears on believability and reputation only; it cannot move accuracy, because accuracy is established against a referent outside the file. This is direct, named, external support for the presumption's core move: self-description is not evidence of the property it describes.
    2. Vaughan, D. (1996). The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA. University of Chicago Press. — The normalization-of-deviance account: O-ring anomalies were repeatedly recorded as falling "within the bounds of acceptable risk," and each documented-and-survived anomaly made the next more acceptable, so that the question of whether the results actually constituted acceptable risk gradually lost force. The documented known issue was the *mechanism* of the failure, not a safeguard against it. This is the canonical external case for the presumption's "documented-known-issue as an accepted-risk failure mode" target, and it makes the specific point that a labelled anomaly is more dangerous than an unlabelled one because the label discharges the obligation to investigate.
    3. Taylor, J.R. (1997). An Introduction to Error Analysis: The Study of Uncertainties in Physical Measurements, 2nd ed., University Science Books; and the standard systematic-versus-random error distinction (see e.g. Penn State STAT 509 course notes). — A systematic error has a net direction and magnitude that does not average out; random error has no preferred direction. A single systematic counting artifact — a consistently applied inclusion or exclusion rule — therefore produces discrepancies of consistent sign across the affected categories. Discrepancies of mixed sign (here one category under and others over) are the signature of either multiple distinct causes or of category-dependent rule differences, not of one uniform artifact. This supports the presumption's specific and testable observation about direction, which is its strongest element.
    4. COSO, Internal Control - Integrated Framework (2013); GAO, Government Auditing Standards (GAO-02-388G and successors); and the data-reconciliation practice literature. — Reconciliation is classified as a *detective* control: the discrepancy is settled by independent recomputation against a defined authoritative system of record, and the practice literature is explicit that without a defined authoritative source beforehand, reconciliation cannot resolve a divergence — it can only report one. The audit standards' self-review-threat principle further forecloses an entity certifying its own records. Together these support both the presumption's negative claim (the disclaimer does not settle it) and its positive one (reconciliation under a stated counting rule is the settling test) — which is exactly the in-house adjunct.
