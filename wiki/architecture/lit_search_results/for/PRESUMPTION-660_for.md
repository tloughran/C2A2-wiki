  Sources:
    1. Prentice, R.L., 1989. "Surrogate endpoints in clinical trials:
       definition and operational criteria." Statistics in Medicine 8(4). —
       The supporting strand, and the source of the condition: a surrogate may
       substitute for a true endpoint, but only under a restrictive criterion
       requiring the true outcome to be conditionally independent of treatment
       given the surrogate. Substitution is licensed by demonstration, not by
       designation.
    2. Fleming, T.R. & DeMets, D.L., 1996. "Surrogate End Points in Clinical
       Trials: Are We Being Misled?" Annals of Internal Medicine 125(7). —
       The counterweight, and the field's standard warning that a proxy which
       tracks the true endpoint observationally can nonetheless fail to
       transmit the effect of an intervention.
    3. "Frequently asked questions on surrogate endpoints in oncology —
       opportunities, pitfalls, and the way forward." eClinicalMedicine, 2024.
       — Reports that most surrogate measures correlate weakly with outcomes
       that matter to patients, and that validity requires evidence at both
       patient level and trial level; unvalidated surrogates risk endorsing
       ineffective interventions.
    4. Construct-validity literature, including "The Proxy Presumption: From
       Semantic Embeddings to Valid Social Measures" (arXiv:2605.07409). —
       States the general requirement that variation in a proxy be attributable
       to the intended construct rather than to superficial regularities, and
       warns against the jangle fallacy in which non-commensurate measures
       accumulate under a shared label.
    5. Cross-sectional study of validated and nonvalidated surrogate endpoints
       in two EMA expedited approval pathways, products authorised 2011-2018
       (PMC6736244). — Empirical evidence that unvalidated proxies are in
       routine regulatory use, i.e. that the substitution-without-validation
       pattern is common rather than exotic.

  Strength of support: Weak

  Summary: The literature supports the presumption in principle and withholds
    it in practice. Prentice established that a proxy can legitimately replace
    a true measure, which is direct theoretical support for the possibility of
    a valid fallback check — but the same paper establishes that the licence
    comes from a demonstrated statistical relationship, deliberately set at a
    restrictive bar. The subsequent thirty-five years of evidence run against
    casual substitution: most proxies correlate weakly with the outcomes they
    stand in for, and proxies that look adequate observationally routinely fail
    to preserve the property that matters under intervention. That last point
    maps directly onto the alerting question — a fallback may track the same
    quantity in the steady state and still not fire under the conditions the
    original check existed to catch. The failure of the qualifications to reach
    the artifact carrying the PASS is separately unsupported: the located
    literature's consistent prescription is that surrogate status travels with
    the finding, which is why validated and nonvalidated surrogates are tracked
    distinctly in regulatory records.

  Caveats: The evidence base is clinical and its transfer to monitoring
    substitutions is analogical; a fallback check may be far more tightly
    coupled to its target than a biomarker is to survival, in which case
    construct validity is easier to establish. The support here is genuinely
    conditional — it becomes real support if C2A2 can demonstrate the fallbacks
    retain the alerting properties of the checks they replace, and evaporates
    otherwise. No literature was found on degraded-mode monitoring specifically
    addressing whether alerting properties survive substitution; that narrower
    question appears thinly covered.
