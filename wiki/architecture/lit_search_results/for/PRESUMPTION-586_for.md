  Sources:
    1. Koops, B.-J. (2021). "The concept of function creep." Law, Innovation and Technology 13(1):29-56. — Koops defines function creep as "an imperceptibly transformative and therewith contestable change in a data-processing system's proper activity", and his central analytic claim is that what distinguishes function creep from ordinary innovation is that the change "is insufficiently acknowledged as transformative and therefore requiring discussion." That is PRESUMPTION-586 stated as a general concept: a change in what a system does, recorded as unremarkable, thereby escaping the discussion that would have decided whether it is permitted. The paper explicitly situates function creep alongside ratchet effects, incrementalism, self-augmentation of technology and slippery-slope argumentation, and its normative point is that the appropriate response is to *surface* the change as contestable rather than to treat its occurrence as settling its propriety. 14b's move — declining to let the observation stand in for an OPEN entry — is exactly the response Koops's framework prescribes.
    2. Vaughan, D. (1996). The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA. University of Chicago Press. — Vaughan's normalization of deviance is the canonical empirical case study of description hardening into norm without a decision being taken. Each flight flown with O-ring erosion made the next erosion more acceptable; the deviation was progressively rationalised into the working group's expected variation and then into normal operation, with no meeting at which anyone resolved that the new baseline was permissible. Vaughan's contribution, as characterised in the sociology-of-organisations literature, is the demonstration that an organisation's own rules can generate a culture in which departure from them becomes acceptable and even expected. This supports the presumption's risk-if-wrong clause with the highest-consequence documented instance available: authority and tolerance both grew by the drift mechanism, and the drift was invisible from inside precisely because each increment was recorded as ordinary.
    3. The Institute of Internal Auditors (2024). Global Internal Audit Standards, Domain III (Governing the Internal Audit Function), Standards 6.1-6.3. — The profession that most closely matches the self-knowledge layer's role has settled the normative question the presumption says is open, and settled it against acquisition by practice. Under the 2024 Standards, the internal audit *mandate* — why the function exists, and what authority, role and responsibilities it has — is granted by the board or by law, and the board must approve both the mandate and the internal audit charter that documents it; the board also approves the internal audit plan and oversees the quality assurance and improvement programme. The function does not set its own remit. This is directly supportive on the presumption's checkable half: the recognised standard is a recorded delegation with a named approver, which is exactly what 14b reports is absent for the intake and scoring rules now in force at 14a/14b/15*. It also identifies the countermeasure — a mandate document with a sign-off — rather than merely naming the problem.
    4. Suber, P. (1990). The Paradox of Self-Amendment: A Study of Law, Logic, Omnipotence, and Change. Peter Lang. — The only full-length study of self-reference and paradox in law, addressing precisely the question of whether a rule may serve as the authority for its own amendment. Suber's finding is nuanced in a way that supports the presumption rather than dissolving it: self-amendment is historically commonplace and is generally held lawful notwithstanding the logical difficulty, *but* its lawfulness is procedural — it depends on the amendment being made through the recognised amending procedure by the body authorised to use it. Nothing in Suber licenses amendment by practice. The relevant contrast is his distinction between direct and indirect self-amendment and his treatment of models of legal change, which together establish that the question "who may change this rule, and by what procedure" is prior to and independent of the fact that the rule has begun to be applied differently. Applied here: even if the self-knowledge layer *may* eventually be authorised to set its own intake criteria, that authorisation would have to arrive through a sign-off, not through first use.
    5. McCubbins, M.D., Noll, R.G. & Weingast, B.R. (1987). "Administrative Procedures as Instruments of Political Control." The Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization 3(2):243-277. — The foundational positive-political-theory account of why delegating bodies impose procedural requirements on the bodies they delegate to: procedures exist to counter information asymmetry and to keep an agency's decisions aligned with the delegator's intent, i.e. to prevent bureaucratic drift. The paper's relevance is that it treats drift as the *expected* behaviour of a delegated body absent procedural constraint, and treats the delegator's remedy as procedural rather than substantive. This supports the presumption's structural claim on two counts: the layer's authority growing by practice is the predicted default rather than an anomaly, and the correct instrument is a recorded procedure for adopting intake rules — which converts 14b's normative worry into an actionable design requirement.
    6. Power, M. (1997). The Audit Society: Rituals of Verification. Oxford University Press. — Power documents the expansion of audit from financial into medical, environmental, quality, value-for-money and teaching domains, and argues that the new demands placed on audit sit uneasily with its operational capabilities. His central distinction between the *operational* and the *normative* characteristics of monitoring practices is the general form of the presumption's is/ought complaint: what audit in fact does and what audit is warranted to do come apart, and the expansion typically occurs on the operational side while the normative warrant is assumed retrospectively. This supplies the base-rate observation the presumption implicitly relies on — that self-monitoring apparatus tends to expand its remit — for a comparison class of real institutions rather than for a single pipeline.

  Summary: Supported, and EXTERNALLY ANCHORED (named external referents: Koops 2021 in Law, Innovation and Technology; Vaughan 1996, University of Chicago Press; IIA Global Internal Audit Standards 2024, Domain III; Suber 1990, Peter Lang; McCubbins, Noll & Weingast 1987 in JLEO; Power 1997, Oxford University Press). The presumption's core — that a change in what a self-monitoring layer does cannot settle what it is permitted to do — is not merely defensible but is the settled position across four independent literatures. Koops 2021 names the failure mode with unusual exactness: the defining feature of function creep is that the transformative change is insufficiently acknowledged as requiring discussion, which is precisely the objection to letting an observation occupy the slot where an OPEN entry would have gone. Vaughan 1996 supplies the highest-consequence documented case of a norm being established by accumulated practice with no decision point. The IIA's 2024 Standards answer the checkable half institutionally: the recognised professional practice is a board-granted mandate and an approved charter, i.e. authority by recorded delegation, and an internal audit function does not set its own remit. Suber 1990 establishes that even where self-amendment is lawful it is lawful *procedurally*, never by usage; and McCubbins et al. 1987 shows that drift is the expected default of a delegated body and that the remedy is a procedure, which converts the presumption into a concrete design requirement (a recorded sign-off for any intake or scoring rule the layer adopts).

  Caveats: (a) The presumption has two halves and the support is asymmetric between them. The general claim — practice does not confer permission; the is/ought gap is not closed by a role change — is strongly supported. The specific claim — that REVISE-251 is *correctly read* as OPEN-138's answer arriving as a demand — is an interpretive judgment about a particular in-pipeline text, and no literature can support or refute it. A reader who thinks REVISE-251 was offered only as a recommendation awaiting sign-off would find the presumption's premise unmet, and nothing here settles that. (b) The IIA and Suber sources cut partly against the presumption's implicit alarm. Both establish that self-amendment and self-defined procedure are *commonplace and often legitimate*: internal audit functions routinely draft their own charters and audit plans, which the board then approves. If the pipeline's practice is that 14b *proposes* and Tom *approves*, then REVISE-251 is the normal first step of a legitimate process, not drift. What the literature condemns is the missing approval, not the proposing. The presumption is therefore best read as identifying a gap in the record rather than a usurpation, and 14b's own checkable quantity (how many rules were adopted by recorded sign-off vs. by first use) is exactly the right measurement. (c) Domain transfer: Koops's function creep is defined for data-processing systems and carries a privacy-law normative freight that does not apply here; Vaughan's case is a physical-risk organisation with fatal consequences, and the analogy risks overstating the stakes; McCubbins et al. concerns elected principals and administrative agencies, where the delegator's interests are contested in a way a single-principal pipeline's are not. The shared structure in all three is the mechanism (incremental change without a decision point), not the setting. (d) Publication bias: the drift/creep literatures are constituted by cases where drift was found, and there is no comparable literature cataloguing self-monitoring functions that *did not* expand their remit. The base rate implied by Power 1997 is therefore unreliable as a base rate. Vaughan in particular is a single case selected for its outcome, which is the standard objection to it. (e) The is/ought half of the presumption was searched for in the philosophical literature (Hume's law, derivations of "ought" from "is") and is deliberately not cited: the philosophical dispute over whether the gap is bridgeable in principle is orthogonal to whether *this* description warrants *this* permission, and citing it would have added authority without adding relevance. That is a scope limit, not an absence of literature. (f) Search scope: six targeted verification searches. Two adjacent areas were not exhausted — standards-body practice on self-amendment beyond Suber (ISO/IEC directives, IETF process for changing its own process), and the constitutional-law literature on convention and desuetude, where practice-based authority is directly theorised. Either could strengthen the institutional half. Moderate search scope; a broader search is recommended before this item is treated as closed on the normative question, which remains partly non-empirical by 14b's own framing.
