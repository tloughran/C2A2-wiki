  Original statement: Whether a retired upstream claim invalidates downstream work citing it. Tested here as the operative proposition acted on in the pipeline: that withdrawal of a cited upstream claim propagates to and invalidates the downstream work resting on it.

  Supporting evidence found: Partial

  Sources:
    1. "An investigation into the impact and implications of published papers from retracted research: systematic search of affected literature," 2019, PMID 31666272 / PMC6830710 [author list not independently confirmed]. — Of 88 systematic reviews and clinical guidelines citing retracted trials, 45 (51%) had findings likely to change if the retracted reports were removed, and in 39 of those 45 (87%) the change was substantial — a change in the *direction* of the finding. By 2018 only one of 68 citing publications had undertaken a reassessment. This is the strongest single support for the presumption: retraction upstream frequently does invalidate downstream conclusions, and correction does not happen spontaneously.
    2. "Inclusion of Retracted Studies in Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Interventions: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis," 2025, PMC11959482 [author list not independently confirmed]. — Larger-cohort quantification: excluding retracted trials changed the *direction* of the pooled effect in 8.4% of meta-analyses, changed statistical significance in 16.0%, changed both in 3.9%, and changed effect magnitude by more than 50% in 15.7%. Supports material downstream impact while bounding it well below universality.
    3. "Investigating the impact of trial retractions on the healthcare evidence ecosystem (VITALITY Study I): retrospective cohort study," 2025, PMC12015725 [author list not independently confirmed]. — Retrospective cohort tracing retraction effects through the evidence ecosystem; effect estimates changed by ≥10% in 42% of meta-analyses, ≥30% in 25%, and ≥50% in 19% for primary outcomes.
    4. Schneider, J., et al., 2020. "Continued post-retraction citation of a fraudulent clinical trial report, 11 years after it was retracted for falsifying data." Scientometrics, doi:10.1007/s11192-020-03631-1. [First author Schneider confirmed from search results; full co-author list not independently verified.] — Case study showing a retracted falsified trial still cited positively and uncritically 11 years post-retraction to support a medical nutrition intervention, with no acknowledgment of the retraction. Establishes that downstream work does *not* self-invalidate, which is why an explicit propagation rule is needed.
    5. "Continued use of retracted papers: Temporal trends in citations and (lack of) awareness of retractions shown in citation contexts in biomedicine," Quantitative Science Studies 2(4), 1144–1169, 2021 [author list not independently confirmed — title, venue, volume and pages confirmed]. — Citation-context analysis showing retracted papers continue to be used substantively; only a small minority (commonly cited in the 3–8% range across studies) of post-retraction citations acknowledge the retraction, and one analysis found 92% of 358 post-retraction citations treated the retracted article as legitimate work.
    6. "Propagation of errors in citation networks: a study involving the entire citation network of a widely cited paper published in, and later retracted from, the journal Nature," 2017/2018, PMC5793988 [author list not independently confirmed]. — Traces error propagation through a full citation network of a retracted Nature paper, demonstrating that invalidation is a network-scale phenomenon reaching beyond first-order citers.
    7. Magesh, V., Surani, F., Dahl, M., Suzgun, M., Manning, C. D., & Ho, D. E., 2025. "Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools." Journal of Empirical Legal Studies (arXiv:2405.20362). — Coded citations pointing to *overruled* authority as "misgrounded" rather than as correct. A directly analogous domain (legal precedent) treats retirement of the upstream authority as invalidating the downstream citation by default, which is normative support for the pipeline's withdrawal rule.

  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: The presumption is supported as a frequent and material consequence, not as a logical entailment. The strongest result is the 2019 systematic search of affected literature: 51% of citing systematic reviews and guidelines had findings likely to change once retracted trials were removed, and in 87% of those the change was a reversal of direction. Larger and more recent cohorts moderate this — the 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found direction changes in 8.4% and significance changes in 16.0% of meta-analyses, and the VITALITY cohort found ≥50% magnitude changes in 19% — so the honest reading is "often invalidating, usually materially perturbing, not always fatal." Two further lines strengthen the operational case: Schneider et al. (2020) and the 2021 QSS citation-context study (source 5) show downstream work does not self-correct (post-retraction citation continues for a decade or more, with only 3–8% of citing contexts acknowledging the retraction), and the citation-network error-propagation study shows the effect reaches past first-order citers. Magesh et al. (2025) supply the normative analogue: in law, citing an overruled authority is scored as an error even though the authority still exists. Withdrawal is therefore a defensible default, but the literature suggests reassessment-then-decide would be better calibrated than automatic withdrawal.

  Caveats: (a) The rate at which retraction actually flips a downstream conclusion varies by an order of magnitude across cohorts (8.4% to 51%), driven by cohort composition and by the threshold chosen for "changed"; no single number should be carried forward. (b) All the quantitative work is in biomedicine/evidence synthesis, where downstream work is a pooled estimate and the arithmetic of removal is explicit — an identifier-anchoring pipeline has no analogous pooling, so the transfer is by analogy. (c) "Retired" in the pipeline is not necessarily equivalent to "retracted for fraud"; the retraction literature is dominated by data falsification and fake peer review, and the invalidation warrant is likely weaker for benign withdrawal or supersession. (d) Author lists for sources 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 could not be independently verified before the session's web-search budget was exhausted; they are cited by title, venue/PMID and year only, per project integrity rules. Source 4 has a confirmed first author only. Source 7 has a fully verified attribution.
