    Three things follow, and they matter.
      (a) These are **not Fredrickson's studies** in the sense FLAG-019 implies. Fredrickson is a middle author on a Lindquist/Cohen-lab affective-neuroscience pair; the wiki filed them under `tradition_key: fredrickson` and the flag then speaks of "Fredrickson's own N=73 cohort."
      (b) The paradigm is **solitary fMRI visualisation** of neutral / pleasant / unpleasant self-nominated acquaintances. There is no dyad, no interaction, no shared affect, no community outcome, and no measure of mutual care. The "dynamic coherence effects appear in pleasant and unpleasant conditions alike" finding is a within-brain functional-connectivity result about loneliness × age, not a result about shared affect at all.
      (c) The wiki's own companion note already flags the over-read ("This is weaker than it looks and should not be ingested as broadening evidence without the full text").

  Sources:
    1. Páez, D., Rimé, B., Basabe, N., Wlodarczyk, A., & Zumeta, L. (2015). "Psychosocial effects of perceived emotional synchrony in collective gatherings." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 108(5): 711–729. (PubMed 25822033) — The closest direct test in the literature. Four studies (two correlational, one semi-longitudinal, one experimental) explicitly compare **positively valenced** gatherings (folkloric marches) with **negatively valenced** ones (protest demonstrations), and find perceived emotional synchrony predicting social integration, convergence of personal and collective identity, self-transcendence, and perceived societal cohesion across both. This is precisely the valence-independence FLAG-019 asserts, tested at community scale.

"Contributions of group identification and emotional synchrony in understanding collective gatherings: A meta-analysis of 13 studies." *Group Processes & Intergroup Relations*. — Meta-analytic aggregation across gathering types; emotional synchrony carries the cohesion outcomes rather than the affective tone of the gathering. Verified via SAGE listing.

"Emotional processes, collective behavior, and social movements: A meta-analytic review of collective effervescence outcomes during collective gatherings and demonstrations." *Frontiers in Psychology* (2022) 13: 974683. — States that collective effervescence can result from negatively valenced emotions such as pain, sadness and grief at a funerary ritual, and reports the roles of anger and sadness in demonstration and mourning outcomes respectively — while noting an inhibitory role for negative emotion in *festive* gatherings specifically. (PubMed 25193943) — Three experiments; shared *painful* (negative-valence) experience causally increased perceived bonding among strangers and cooperation in economic games relative to a no-pain control. Direct causal evidence that shared negative affect produces a prosocial community-level good.

Shared negative affect producing the strongest form of measured group bond.
    6. Jong, J., Whitehouse, H., Kavanagh, C., & Lane, J. (2015). "Shared negative experiences lead to identity fusion via personal reflection." *PLoS ONE* 10(12): e0145611. — The mechanism study for source 5; shared negative experience → identity fusion, mediated by personal reflection.
    7. Prickett, P. J. (2022). "'If no one grieves, no one will remember': Cultural palimpsests and the creation of social ties through rituals." *The British Journal of Sociology*. — Ethnographic evidence that intensive collective effervescence in mourning contexts lets participants process personal loss in public and constitutes an occasion for community integration. "Well-being correlates of perceived positivity resonance: Evidence from trait and episode-level assessments." *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*. The wiki's proposed re-boundary is therefore partly a move *within* Fredrickson's own definition rather than against it.

  Strength of support: Strong for the claim that shared affect of *negative* valence produces community-health / cohesion outcomes. Moderate for the narrower claim that *mutual care* is specifically the operative moderator that distinguishes cohesive from non-cohesive shared negative affect.

  Summary: I scored the affirmative of FLAG-019 — that shared affect with mutual care, rather than positive valence, is the correct boundary for community-health effects. On the valence half, the support is strong and comes from a literature the wiki has not yet ingested: Páez et al.'s 2015 JPSP paper deliberately contrasts positively and negatively valenced collective gatherings and finds the cohesion outcomes in both, a 2024 meta-analysis of 13 studies replicates the pattern, and Bastian et al., Whitehouse et al. and Jong et al. supply causal and field evidence that shared pain and shared dysphoria bond groups. Prickett's ethnography carries the specific grief-and-lament case. On the mutual-care half the support is real but softer: mutual care and concern is already one of the three defining components of Fredrickson's positivity-resonance construct, so re-drawing the boundary around it is defensible on the theory's own terms — but I found no study that experimentally manipulates mutual care while holding shared affect and valence constant, which is what would settle it. The most consequential finding of this search is negative and concerns provenance rather than the claim: the "Fredrickson N=73 cohort" is a Lindquist/Cohen-lab pair of solitary-visualisation fMRI studies on loneliness and ageing on which Fredrickson is a middle author, with no shared affect, no dyads and no community outcome anywhere in the design. FLAG-019 reaches a well-supported conclusion from evidence that does not support it. The flag should be kept and re-grounded, not withdrawn.

  Caveats:
    - The evidence base above is about *collective gatherings, rituals and dyads*, not about the C2A2 "community health" metric as operationalised. Whether the construct the wiki is measuring is the same construct these studies measure is an open question — and is itself an instance of ASSUMPTION-937's worry.
    - Bastian, Jetten & Ferris (2014) has a partially-failed replication: Qi, Sun, Yao, Dai, Wang & Liu (2020), "Pain and sharing: A re-examination of the findings of Bastian, Jetten, and Ferris (2014)," *Journal of Pacific Rim Psychology*. I record this here rather than leaving it to 15b because it bears on how much weight source 4 can carry.
    - The Frontiers 2022 meta-analytic review reports that negative emotions play an *inhibitory* role in festive collective gatherings. So the correct statement is not "valence is irrelevant" but "valence interacts with setting": negative affect is cohesive in mourning, protest and ordeal settings and corrosive in celebratory ones. A boundary of "shared affect with mutual care" does not by itself capture that interaction.
