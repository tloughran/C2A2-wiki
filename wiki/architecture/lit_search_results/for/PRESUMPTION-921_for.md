  Strength of support: Moderate

  Summary: The literature on how traditions are individuated leans heavily on founders. Kuhn's paradigm
  is at bottom an exemplar, and the exemplars he names are single-author texts; the research-schools
  historiography (Morrell, Geison) and Mullins' theory-group sociology identify schools by their leader
  and treat the leader's programme as the school's content; Merton documents eponymy as science's own
  institutional device for tying a corpus to a person; bibliometric work (Price and Beaver; the
  phenomenology co-citation study) shows that output and citation structure concentrate on a few
  founder-nodes, so a founder's corpus carries most of the recoverable signal. Even MacIntyre, the
  strongest "tradition as community" theorist, names his traditions after founders. Taken together
  this supports founder-centred representation as a defensible proxy, particularly for young or
  small traditions and for the origin phase of any tradition.

  Caveats: (a) All the supportive sources describe founders as the ANCHOR of a tradition; none claims
  the founder's output exhausts the tradition's activity, and Mullins, Geison and Kuhn all describe
  later stages in which the school's activity diverges from the founder. Support is for "valid proxy,"
  not for "the tradition is an author." (b) Merton's point that eponymy goes to the influential rather
  than the originator implies that the eponym can misrepresent who did the work. (c) Several of the
  C2A2 traditions (Friston, Levin, Hawkins) have large collaborator networks in which the founder is
  first or last author on a minority of output; the concentration argument (Source 4) then supports
  the founder as a hub, not as the sole author. (d) The N=1 consensus-metric problem noted by 14b is
  not addressed by any source found: nothing here says intra-tradition agreement is well-defined when
  a tradition is reduced to one voice. (e) Search scope: preliminary — 8 queries; not covered:
  Lakatos' MSRP text on whether a programme's hard core is founder-authored (only secondary summaries
  seen), Collins on intellectual networks (The Sociology of Philosophies, 1998), Lotka's law primary
  sources, and the "great man" historiography debate (Carlyle vs Spencer) which was not searched
  directly.
