  Summary: The LLM-annotation literature supports the first half of the presumption fairly well: independent runs of the same model under the same prompt agree with each other at kappa levels (≈0.89–0.97; 90–98% exact agreement) that exceed typical human intercoder agreement, and Gilardi et al. (2023) found LLM intercoder agreement above that of trained human annotators on topic and frame tasks — the closest analogue to node assignment. Deterministic decoding makes parallel batches essentially replicates of one annotator, which removes the classic source of inter-annotator drift (different people). Human annotation methodology (Text Annotation Handbook; DECOR-style practice) endorses the design pattern the pipeline used — shared written spec, independent workers — but always with an agreement measurement on an overlap sample as the licence to parallelise. That measurement is the part the record lacks; the ~40-cell row-header check is a single-facet spot check, not an overlap-sample agreement figure, and no source found treats one facet's consistency as evidence for another's.

  Caveats: (a) The high intra-model consistency figures are for repeated calls on the *same* items; the eight batches saw *different* items, so the relevant question is whether the model's decision boundary is stable across item populations, which the consistency studies do not directly test. (b) Consistency is task-dependent: the same literature (seen incidentally, e.g. arXiv 2510.27106 "Rating Roulette: Self-Inconsistency in LLM-As-A-Judge Frameworks"; arXiv 2405.01724 "Large Language Models are Inconsistent and Biased Evaluators") reports Krippendorff's alpha as low as 0.27–0.56 on judgement-heavy tasks; boundary node choices (III.3 vs III.4) are closer to judgement than to topic tagging. (c) Batch-level context effects (position in a long prompt, the particular neighbours in a batch) are a known source of variance not covered by the sources found.
