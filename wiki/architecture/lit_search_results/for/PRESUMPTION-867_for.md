  Strength of support: Moderate (Strong for clause 1; Weak-to-None for clause 2)

  Summary: The first clause of this presumption — that a "fail loud" norm improves system health — is well supported and supported from several independent directions. Formal fault-tolerance theory has treated visible, immediate halting as a *desirable and specifiable* failure semantics since Schlichting and Schneider's 1983 fail-stop model; software engineering practice has consolidated the same idea as the fail-fast principle, with the mechanism given as fault localisation, cheaper repair, and prevention of cascade; organisational research in the Edmondson lineage finds that higher error-reporting rates accompany better team performance rather than worse; and aviation's voluntary reporting programmes provide the large-scale operational instance. Recent LLM-agent-specific work strengthens the case within C2A2's own system class by documenting "fail-plausible" silent failure as the dominant and hardest failure mode, making loud failure the comparatively benign alternative. The second clause — that the norm's second-order effect on success accounting is benign — found essentially no direct literature. The one genuine precedent is the fail-stop model itself, where correct halting counts as correct component behaviour by construction; that is a definitional warrant rather than an empirical finding, and it does not speak to whether an aggregate health or completion metric built over such runs remains meaningful.

  Caveats: (a) The clause-1 evidence is strong but the mechanism it supports is *visibility enabling remedial action*, not visibility alone. Every strong instance is paired: the andon cord halts the line and summons a team leader; ASRS reports are analysed and fed into procedure changes; Edmondson's high-performing teams report errors *and* learn from them. The literature supports the norm as the first half of a loop, and none of these sources tests the norm's value with the second half absent. (b) Practitioner sources dominate the fail-fast material; they are convergent but not peer-reviewed, and the DZone/CodeReliant/Elegant sources cite no measured outcomes. (c) The Edmondson findings are cited here from secondary summaries; the primary papers were not retrieved and their details are marked unverified. (d) Some quantitative figures surfaced in search summaries (e.g. "near-miss reporting increased 2.5x; change failure rate fell from 14% to 7%") come from an undated vendor blog and are deliberately excluded from the Sources list as unverifiable. (e) Clause 2 is carried almost entirely by an analogy to fail-stop semantics; that analogy licenses "a failure-reporting run is a correctly-behaving run" but says nothing about whether the *accounting layer above* such runs stays informative.

  Recommendation: PARTIALLY-SUPPORTED

  PARTIAL NOVELTY-FLAG:
    Addressed by literature: that immediate, visible failure reporting is a recognised and beneficial design norm; that error-reporting cultures correlate with better measured outcomes; that silent/plausible failure is the worse failure class, specifically in LLM agent runtimes.
    Unaddressed sub-claim (precise): the second-order accounting claim — that adopting a fail-loud norm has a *benign* effect on how success is counted. Specifically, no located source addresses whether a run that terminates by faithfully reporting total task failure should be, or safely can be, recorded as a completed/successful run of the reporting system; nor whether an aggregate health metric composed of such runs remains a valid indicator of the health of the thing being reported on. The literature treats failure *disclosure* and failure *resolution* as connected in practice but does not examine the metric-inflation pathway between them. This sub-claim appears genuinely unaddressed in the supportive direction and should be treated as novel.
