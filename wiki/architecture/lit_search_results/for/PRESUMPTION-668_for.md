  Sources:
    1. van Dyck, C., Frese, M., Baer, M. & Sonnentag, S., 2005. "Organizational
       error management culture and its impact on performance: A two-study
       replication." Journal of Applied Psychology 90(6):1228-1240. — The
       strongest support found. Error management culture — norms of
       communicating about, detecting, analysing and correcting errors quickly —
       was positively related to firm performance in 65 Dutch organisations
       (goal achievement plus an objective economic indicator) and replicated
       against change-of-profitability data in 47 German organisations. This
       supports the value of the catching capability itself, independent of
       whether every catch is followed by a repair. Note that the construct
       bundles detection *with* analysis and correction; it is not a measure of
       detection alone.
    2. Phimister, J.R., Oktem, U., Kleindorfer, P.R. & Kunreuther, H., 2003.
       "Near-Miss Incident Management in the Chemical Process Industry." Risk
       Analysis 23(3):445-459. doi:10.1111/1539-6924.00326. — Empirical
       precedent from 100+ interviews at 20 chemical and pharmaceutical
       facilities, yielding a seven-stage near-miss management framework. Two
       readings, both reported: the paper supports that processing caught events
       reduces future incidents, and it simultaneously locates identification and
       reporting as the first two of seven stages, with causal analysis, solution
       identification, dissemination and resolution following. On this framework
       a catch that stops at correction has completed roughly a quarter of the
       cycle and the paper's whole diagnostic purpose is finding where sites'
       programmes break off early.
    3. ISO 9001:2015, Clause 10.2 (Nonconformity and corrective action), and the
       quality-management commentary literature on it. — Directly names the
       distinction the presumption elides. A *correction* eliminates the detected
       nonconformity in the output and is legitimate as an interim measure that
       lets operations resume; a *corrective action* addresses the cause so the
       nonconformity does not recur. The standard requires the second and
       requires verification of its effectiveness, and the commentary literature
       identifies failure to monitor effectiveness as the direct cause of
       recurring issues and repeat findings. The twice-observed false-positive
       sweep (07-31, 08-04) is the textbook signature of correction without
       corrective action.
    4. Dillon, R.L. & Tinsley, C.H., 2008. "How Near-Misses Influence Decision
       Making Under Risk: A Missed Opportunity for Learning." Management Science
       54(8):1425-1440. Related: Tinsley, C.H., Dillon, R.L. & Cronin, M.A.,
       2012. "How Near-Miss Events Amplify or Attenuate Risky Decision Making."
       Management Science 58(9); Dillon, R.L., Tinsley, C.H., Madsen, P.M. &
       Rogers, E.W., 2016. "Organizational Correctives for Improving Recognition
       of Near-Miss Events." Journal of Management 42(3). — Reported in full per
       the no-cherry-picking rule; this is the item's own named search target and
       it goes against. Across experiments and field data (NASA, BP, JetBlue,
       airline industry), people given prior near-miss information subsequently
       make riskier decisions than those given none, discounting probability
       information by failing to see the independence of events. A successful
       catch is read as evidence the risk was manageable. The 2016 paper is the
       constructive complement: it proposes organisational correctives precisely
       because recognition does not occur unaided.
    5. Near-miss reporting programme effectiveness literature (industrial safety
       reviews, 2018-2024, incl. Gnoni & Saleh's near-miss management systems
       review in Safety Science). — Reports that higher near-miss reporting
       frequency is associated with reduced major-accident frequency. One
       secondary source states a 23% reduction in major accidents within three
       years for organisations with formal programmes; that specific figure could
       not be traced to a primary study and is marked UNVERIFIED. The
       association, where reported, is for *programmes* — reporting plus
       analysis plus corrective action — not for catching alone.

  Strength of support: Weak

  Summary: The literature strongly supports that an organisation which catches
    its own errors is better off than one which does not — van Dyck et al. is a
    two-country replication tying error management culture to objective
    performance, and the near-miss corpus ties reporting programmes to reduced
    accident rates. That is genuine support for the value of the three
    self-retractions as events. It is not support for the presumption as stated.
    Every source that decomposes the process places detection at the front of a
    longer chain and attributes the benefit to the chain: Phimister's seven
    stages, van Dyck's bundled detect-analyse-correct construct, ISO 10.2's
    explicit split between correcting the output and correcting the cause. No
    source was found holding that catching alone changes recurrence rates. The
    directly on-point behavioural literature — Dillon and Tinsley, which the item
    itself names — finds the opposite: repeated successful catches lower
    perceived risk and increase subsequent risk-taking, which makes an
    accumulating retraction count a warning signal rather than a reassurance.
    The twice-observed false-positive sweep is the predicted outcome of
    correction without corrective action.

  Caveats: The supporting evidence is correlational and cross-sectional (van
    Dyck: self-reported culture measures against firm outcomes; near-miss
    programme studies: association, not controlled comparison), so it cannot
    establish that catching *causes* reduced recurrence even within the bundled
    construct. Domain transfer is substantial: all sources concern human
    organisations and physical-process safety, where the producing instrument is
    a procedure or a person and repair means retraining or redesign; here the
    instrument is a method executed by an agent, and it is not established that
    the same recurrence dynamics apply. The Dillon-Tinsley near-miss bias is a
    human cognitive finding and its transfer to an agent pipeline is analogical —
    though the pipeline's own record of the same sweep recurring after a
    disclosed catch is direct local evidence of the pattern regardless of
    mechanism. Publication bias is a live concern in the near-miss programme
    literature, which is largely written by programme advocates.
