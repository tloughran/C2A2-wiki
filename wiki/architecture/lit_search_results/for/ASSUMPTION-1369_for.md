    Restated so both limbs are searchable:

        claim is no longer valid. A time-based re-check trigger therefore carries real information.
      L2 (the item as written, reported but not searched FOR): a 6/6 REVISE rate among items selected

        truth.
    L1 is where the literature is, and it is reported below as the primary result. L2 is a statistical
    objection, not an empirical claim, and 14a explicitly asked this agent to address only the FOR side
    of it — evidence that base rates make 6/6 informative despite the selection. That is handled in the
    section "The selection objection, FOR side" and is the weakest part of this file.

    One further observation the polarity inversion exposes, recorded because it belongs to the FOR side:
    the originating run's inference runs through the word "confirmed." In the closest literature analogue
    (AHRQ Technical Review 6, source 1) the audit of an aged register produced a **three-way** outcome —
    *withdraw*, *retain-with-append*, *retain* — and the middle category means the item was **still

    confirmed" does not establish that six claims were false, and the run's gloss does not follow from
    its own data. Whether REVISE maps to *withdraw* or to *retain-with-append* is not resolvable from the
    item text and is the single largest scope limitation on this search.

    7. Barlow, R.E. & Proschan, F., 1965. *Mathematical Theory of Reliability.* New York: Wiley. (And the

       proportional hazards models relating failure risk to concurrent aging and time-varying covariates;

       — **The theoretical grounding, and the source of the sharpest boundary condition in this file.**
       The classical result: an age-based preventive replacement policy is worth operating **only when the
       lifetime distribution has an increasing failure rate (IFR)**. Under a constant hazard (exponential)
       age-based replacement buys nothing; under a decreasing hazard it is actively harmful, because the
       oldest surviving items are the *least* likely to fail next. The condition-based literature's
       standard framing is that time-based inspection applies a fixed interval to all components whereas
       condition-based inspection responds to observed state, and that a proportional hazards model — age
       *plus* time-varying covariates — dominates attributing health to age alone.
       **SECONDARY** — the IFR/age-replacement result is a textbook classic reported here from search
       summary and secondary chapter descriptions; Barlow & Proschan was not read. No page citation

       stale-downgrade rule is sound **exactly to the extent that the invalidity hazard of estate blocks

       heterogeneous item classes — which is what a single stale-downgrade cadence is. The paper's remedy

  The selection objection, FOR side:
    14a asked for evidence that base rates make the 6/6 informative despite the selection. No paper was
    found that argues this. What the literature supplies instead is the **base rate**, which is the input
    the argument needs, and the argument can then be constructed. It is set out here as arithmetic on
    published figures, clearly labelled, because no source states it.

    The objection is that a rule which selects old items and then finds them revisable has manufactured
    its own result. That is true only if the selected items' revise rate is no higher than the register's.
    So the question is what fraction of *unselected* blocks would also go to REVISE.

    Take the null H0: the stale flag carries no information, and every drained block independently has
    probability p of needing revision, where p is the register-wide rate. Then P(6 of 6 revise | H0) = p⁶.

      - At p = 0.221 — the verified 4-year revise rate for curated recommendations (source 2) —
        P(6/6) ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻⁴.
      - At p = 0.50, P(6/6) ≈ 0.016.
      - At p = 0.765 — the AHRQ rate of aged guidelines needing *any* change, 13/17 (source 1) —
        P(6/6) ≈ 0.20.

    **These three calculations are this agent's own arithmetic on published base rates. They are not
    findings from any source and must not be quoted as such.**

    The shape of the answer: 6/6 is strongly informative if the estate's register-wide revise rate is
    below about one half, and close to uninformative if the register revises most of what it touches
    anyway. The guideline literature's own base rates straddle that threshold — 22% by one measure, 76%
    by another — so the literature cannot settle it. The discriminating quantity is the **confirm rate
    among drained blocks that were not stale-flagged**, and the estate has exactly one such block in this
    window (7 drained, 6 flagged). One is not enough. Source 3 is the methodological template: it
    established predictive validity precisely by having low-priority items to compare against, and its
    most load-bearing finding is the negative one — that both low-priority reviews had no substantive
    changes. Without a comparable non-stale arm, ASSUMPTION-1369's data cannot discriminate in either
    direction, and this agent found no literature that lets it.

    One asymmetry worth recording on the FOR side: the originating run treats 6/6 REVISE as *damaging* to
    the rule. In the screening-trigger framing that sources 3 and 7 both use, a trigger whose flagged
    items all turn out to need work is exhibiting high positive predictive value, which is a success
    criterion, not a failure. The run's reading only holds if REVISE means "changed for cadence reasons
    despite being true" — see the POLARITY DECLARATION.

  Summary: The assigned proposition — that time since last validation predicts invalidity in a curated
    register — is supported across several independent literatures. The strongest evidence is Martínez
    García et al. 2014, where the probability that a guideline recommendation remains valid falls
    monotonically from 92% at one year to 77.8% at four, and the AHRQ audit of 17 aged guidelines, where
    only three of seventeen survived intact. Link rot rises monotonically with article age in every corpus
    and every prior study Klein et al. surveyed. So aged items in curated registers really are more likely
    to be wrong, and a currency trigger really can predict that: Shekelle et al. 2014 achieved κ=0.74
    agreement between predicted updating priority and actual change in conclusions. But the support is for
    a *currency trigger*, not for *a uniform time rule*, and three findings mark the boundary. Shekelle's
    validated trigger is risk-based, searching for new evidence rather than counting months. Age-based
    replacement theory holds that a time rule is justified only under an increasing hazard, and Martínez
    García's own increments decelerate rather than accelerate. And the one direct test found in a
    machine-curated knowledge register reports the opposite hazard shape entirely — Weibull κ<1, older
    facts *less* likely to be superseded — with uniform decay performing 18× worse than no temporal
    weighting at all. Cochrane, which ran a fixed two-year cadence over the largest such register in
    medicine, abandoned the default interval for priority-based updating. The literature's verdict on the
    estate's rule is therefore not "sound" or "unsound" but "sound if and only if the hazard is
    increasing, and per item class rather than uniformly," and that is an empirical question about the
    estate that no external literature can answer.

  Finding: The general proposition — curated claims decay with age — is extensively covered and is not
    novel. Two narrower things were searched for and not found. First, no study was found that estimates
    the **hazard shape** of invalidity for a register of *reasoning artefacts* (premises, presumptions,
    architectural claims about a system's own operation) as opposed to empirical claims about an external
    world. Every hazard estimate located concerns the latter, and the two available estimates disagree in
    sign — increasing for clinical recommendations, decreasing (Lindy) for knowledge-graph facts — which
    means the sign cannot be assumed for a third class by analogy. Second, no source was found that
    addresses the selection objection directly; the base-rate argument in this file had to be constructed
    from published rates rather than cited.
  Implication: The estate is holding the only data that could settle its own question, and the analysis
    that would settle it is standard and cheap: record the revise/confirm outcome for drained blocks
    **split by whether they were stale-flagged**, and fit the hazard against age. This is exactly the
    design of Shekelle et al. 2014 (low-priority arm as the comparator) and exactly the recommendation of
    the adaptive-decay preprint (fit decay per cluster rather than uniformly). A register that did this
    over its own blocks would produce a genuinely original result — the first hazard estimate for
    invalidity of self-referential reasoning artefacts — and would be reporting something no literature
    currently contains.
  Recommended status: NOVEL (for the hazard-shape question on reasoning artefacts, and for the
    selection-adjusted evaluation of a staleness trigger; the underlying "curated claims decay with age"
    limb is well covered and is not novel)
