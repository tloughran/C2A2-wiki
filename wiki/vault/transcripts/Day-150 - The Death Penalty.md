# Day 150 — The Death Penalty

*Questions 64 and 65 of the Secunda Secundae — the vices opposed to commutative justice, beginning with murder and other injuries to the person. Q.64: it is not unlawful to kill plants and animals, since the imperfect exists for the perfect in the order of nature (a.1); the state may lawfully execute an evildoer for the sake of the common good, as a diseased member is cut from the body, though prudence may counsel restraint (a.2); a private individual may not, for that usurps public authority (a.3); clerics may not, by their sacramental conformity to Christ the lamb (a.4); suicide is gravely wrong — against community, self-love, and God’s dominion over life (a.5); the innocent may never be slain (a.6); killing in self-defense is lawful when intended as self-preservation and proportionate (a.7, the seed of double-effect); and one can be guilty of unintended homicide through culpable omission (a.8). Q.65 extends the analysis to maiming (a.1), the corrective striking of children and subordinates by imperfect domestic authority (a.2), imprisonment (a.3), and the aggravation of injuries done to those connected with others (a.4).*

## Notes

- The part–whole / common-good argument reaches its sharpest form (a.2): the evildoer is excised “as a diseased member” for the health of the whole body politic. The whole-over-part logic that governs the entire Justice treatise here licenses killing — the synthesis must engage this directly and mark where it parts from Aquinas.
- Lawful (a.2) vs prudent (same article): Aquinas grants the state’s right to execute but immediately notes our Lord counsels letting the wicked live lest the good be uprooted with them — the competence/telos seam from the prudence treatise resurfaces inside justice.
- Self-defense (a.7) is the historical seed of the principle of double effect: the act is licensed by the *intention* of self-preservation, with the aggressor’s death praeter intentionem and bounded by proportionality. A key hook for agency, intention, and responsibility.
- Killing by culpable omission (a.8): “he who does not remove something whence homicide results, whereas he ought to remove it, is in a sense guilty.” Responsibility extends to what one fails to prevent — omission as causation.
- Q.64 a.1’s ranking of beings (plants/animals for man; the imperfect for the perfect) is the strongly anthropocentric, hierarchical-teleology premise most in tension with the mind-monist / agency-at-all-scales frame — flag as the locus of sharpest reframing.
