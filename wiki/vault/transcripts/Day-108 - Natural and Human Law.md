# Day 108 — Natural and Human Law (Q.94-95)

After Q.93 on the eternal law, Q.94-95 take up its two derivations available to natural reason: the natural law (knowable to all rational creatures) and human law (the particular determinations a polity makes of those general principles).

## Transcript

### Q.94 — The Natural Law

[00:01] **Article 1 — Whether the natural law is a habit.** Habit (Augustine): "that whereby something is done when necessary" — a quality modifying a faculty that one possesses even when not utilizing it (knowledge of English, the virtue of courage). The natural law is the law of right and wrong as accessible by the natural light of reason, flowing from our nature. Coming into the world a *tabula rasa*, we must learn it from experience. Its first principle: "good is to be done and pursued; evil avoided" — all other content of natural law builds on this. The natural law *in itself* is not a habit but a law outside us we come to learn; once held in mind, it can be called a habit in the way our knowledge of English is.

[00:03] **Article 2 — Whether the natural law contains several precepts or only one.** Both. There is one foundational principle ("good is to be pursued, evil avoided") that the world reveals to us in such a way that we cannot sincerely admit its falsity. Other precepts build on it because we have an objective, specific kind of nature that lets us discern objectively what is or is not good for us. (Example offered: drunkenness — voluntary recreational impairment of the rational animal's capacity to reason is not in accord with our nature. *Note: as with day 106, this is a tradition-specific application offered by the host as an example of an inference from natural law.*) Aquinas: "All other precepts of the natural law are based upon this: that whatever the practical reason naturally apprehends as man's good (or evil) belongs to the precepts of the natural law as something to be done or avoided."

[00:04] **Article 3 — Whether all acts of virtue are prescribed by the natural law.** Since virtue makes a man good, the natural law in prescribing pursuit of the good proper to him does prescribe virtues and virtuous acts: prudence, justice, temperance, courage. But not every act of every virtue is found in every individual (virginity is not held within married life; martyrdom is not for all). Aquinas: "Since the rational soul is the proper form of man, there is in every man a natural inclination to act according to reason, and this is to act according to virtue. Considered thus, all acts of virtue are prescribed by the natural law. But if we speak of virtuous acts considered in themselves — in their proper species — thus not all virtuous acts are prescribed by the natural law."

[00:06] **Article 4 — Whether the natural law is the same in all men.** Aquinas: "We must say that the natural law as to general principles is the same for all, but as to certain matters of detail, which are conclusions in some few cases, it may fail both as to rectitude and as to knowledge." The law is the same generally; specific applications can differ either through personal ignorance/vicious habit (Aquinas notes that theft "was not considered wrong among the Germans, as Julius Caesar relates") or through circumstances (return of borrowed goods may be wrong if the lender would use them to betray the country).

[00:08] **Article 5 — Whether the natural law can be changed.** The natural law is as fixed as our nature; as long as we are the rational animal, the particular goods we ought to pursue and evils we ought to avoid remain the same. Human laws change, and may be confused with natural law, but the natural law itself does not change. Aquinas: "The written law is said to be given for the correction of the natural law, either because it supplies what was wanting to the natural law, or because the natural law was perverted in the hearts of some men as to certain matters, so that they esteemed those things good which are naturally evil."

[00:09] **Article 6 — Whether the law of nature can be abolished from the heart of man.** No: man cannot abolish the first principle of practical reason or conscience from his heart. He can persuade himself that specific evils are good or specific goods are evil — confusion of the secondary precepts, or of the conclusions which flow from the first principles. Aquinas: "In the secondary precepts, the natural law can be blotted out from the heart of man, either by evil persuasions… or by vicious customs or corrupt habits, as among men theft, and even unnatural vices, as Paul says in Romans 1, were not esteemed sinful."

### Q.95 — Human Law

[00:11] **Article 1 — Whether it was useful for laws to be framed by men.** Yes: we have a natural inclination to act according to reason, but the young are easily confused as to the path of right reason, and we have rogue passions. We need a teacher and training. Parental instruction may carry some young people far, but for many — perverted by passion or culpable ignorance — a more terrifying, more binding law is needed. This is human law, enforced by the state. Aquinas: "In order that man might have peace and virtue, it was necessary for laws to be framed; for, as the Philosopher says, as man is the most noble of animals if he be perfect in virtue, so he is the lowest of all if he be severed from law and righteousness. And Isidore says: laws were made that in fear thereof human audacity might be held in check, that innocence might be safeguarded in the midst of wickedness, and that the dread of punishment might prevent the wicked from doing harm."

[00:12] **Article 2 — Whether every human law is derived from the natural law.** Aquinas: "In human affairs a thing is said to be just from being right according to the rule of reason. But the first rule of reason is the law of nature. Consequently, every human law has just so much of the nature of law as it is derived from the law of nature. But if in any point it deflects from the law of nature, it is no longer a law but a perversion of law."

[00:13] **Article 3 — Whether Isidore's description of the quality of positive laws is appropriate.** Isidore: "Law shall be virtuous, just, possible to nature, according to the custom of the country, suitable to place in time, necessary, useful, clearly expressed lest by its obscurity it lead to misunderstanding, framed for no private benefit but for the common good." Aquinas approves: positive law should be proportionate to the divine law (fostering religious practice), suited to the natural law (source of virtuous discipline), and directed to the common good.

[00:14] **Article 4 — Whether Isidore's division of human laws is appropriate.** Isidore divides into civil law and the law of nations. Law of nations: those laws common across nations that arise out of man's social nature simply, necessary for social interactions within and across nations (e.g., laws governing contracts, buying and selling). Civil law: derived from natural law in the particular determinations each state makes for its own pursuit of the common good. Aquinas: "Positive law is divided into the law of nations and civil law. To the law of nations belong those things which are derived from the law of nature as conclusions from premises… But those things which are derived from the law of nature by way of particular determination belong to the civil law, according as each state decides on what is best for itself."

## Notes

- Q.94 a.1's *tabula rasa* + "the natural law in itself is not a habit but a law outside us which we come to learn" is striking from a 13th-century thinker: it sets up exactly the question contemporary developmental and predictive-processing accounts now formalize. *Where does the prior come from, and how does it get loaded into the agent?*
- Q.94 a.4's allowance that natural-law conclusions can fail "both as to rectitude and as to knowledge" is a remarkable admission of perspective-dependence inside a fundamentally objectivist framework. Aquinas grants that the same general law is differently visible from different cultural vantage points — Caesar on Germanic theft is the classical example.
- The host's confidence about contemporary applications of natural law (sexual ethics, drug legalization) should be taken as one tradition's reading. The synthesis should hold the *form* of the argument (rationally-discernible goods following from a discernible nature) without auto-endorsing the specific empirical claims.
- Q.95 a.1's "we need a teacher and training" is the bridge to the Day-95 Beatitudes / Day-94 greatest-virtue material from earlier weeks: law is not the same kind of thing as virtue, but its function is to make virtue acquirable.
- The natural/positive law split, and the "law of nations" vs "civil law" distinction, anticipate the much later modern emergence of public international law — and, importantly, the *difference* between universal moral facts and convention-dependent coordination devices.
