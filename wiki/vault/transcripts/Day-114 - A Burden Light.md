# Day 114 — A Burden Light (Q.106-107)

After 16 questions on the Old Law (Q.98-105), Aquinas turns to the New Law. Q.106 treats the new law *in itself* — its essential nature as the grace of the Holy Spirit. Q.107 compares it with the Old Law.

## Transcript

### Q.106 — The Law of the Gospel (the New Law) Considered in Itself

[00:01] **Article 1 — Whether the new law is a written law.** Aquinas: "We must say that the new law is in the first place a law that is inscribed on our hearts, but that secondarily it is a written law." Written: the Gospel's call to baptism, the worship of God via the Eucharist, and the tenets of the faith. Inscribed: the gift of God *as a person* is the Holy Spirit, dwelling in our hearts through baptism and in all in a state of grace. The Holy Spirit is the *efficient cause* of fulfilling this law, since we fulfill a supernatural law only through supernatural grace and virtue, poured into our heart (Rom 5: "because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us").

[00:02] **Article 2 — Whether the new law justifies.** Yes. Rom 1:16: "I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who has faith — to the Jew first and also to the Greek." Baptism justifies (Titus 3:5: "he saved us… by the washing of regeneration and renewal in the Holy Spirit"; Acts 2: "Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins"). The sacrament of confession restores a man to grace after grievous sin (John 20: "If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven").

[00:04] **Article 3 — Whether the new law should have been given from the beginning of the world.** No — God acts over human history analogously to how a father acts with a maturing son. Aquinas: "First, because the new law consists chiefly in the grace of the Holy Spirit, which it behooved not to be given abundantly until sin, which is an obstacle to grace, had been cast out of man through the accomplishment of his Redemption by Christ. A second reason may be taken from the perfection of the new law, because a thing is not brought to perfection from the outset, but through an orderly succession of time… The third reason is that the new law is the law of grace, wherefore it behooved man first of all to be left to himself under the state of the Old Law, so that, through falling into sin, he might realize his weakness and acknowledge his need of grace."

[00:05] **Article 4 — Whether the new law will last till the end of the world.** Yes — Matt 24: "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away." The pilgrim state under the new law is succeeded only by the beatific vision in heaven (or its absence in hell), not by another law on earth. Aquinas cites Dionysius's three states: under the Old Law; under the new law; the third in heaven, not on earth.

### Q.107 — The New Law as Compared with the Old

[00:07] **Article 1 — Whether the new law is distinct from the Old Law.** The two laws are not distinct in *end* (both direct man to subordination to God) but in *perfection of direction toward that end*. Old Law: guided man as he could be guided then — through faith and figures, ceremonial practices. New Law: guides man directly through justification received in the sacraments and through faith not in an expected Messiah but in the *actually incarnate* Messiah. Aquinas: "All the differences assigned between the old and new laws are gathered from their relative perfection and imperfection. The Old Law, which was given to men who were imperfect (i.e., who had not yet received spiritual grace), was called the law of fear, inasmuch as it induced men to observe its commandments by threatening them with penalties, and is spoken of as containing temporal promises. But those who are possessed of virtue are inclined to do virtuous deeds through love of virtue, not on account of some extrinsic punishment or reward. Hence the new law, which derives its preeminence from the spiritual grace instilled into our hearts, is called the law of love, and it is described as containing spiritual and eternal promises, which are the objects of the virtues, chiefly of charity."

[00:10] **Article 2 — Whether the new law fulfills the old.** Yes — Matt 5: "Think not that I have come to abolish the law and the prophets; I have come not to abolish them but to fulfill them." Christ fulfills the Old Law (i) by being the Messiah it pointed to, (ii) by submitting himself to it (circumcision, Passover), (iii) by instructing the *spirit* of that law (raising the standard from adultery/murder to lust/wrath), (iv) by safeguarding the Old Law with additional precepts (not swearing by this or that, Matt 5), (v) by calling to perfect imitation of Christ (the rich young ruler in Matt 19).

[00:11] **Article 3 — Whether the new law is contained in the old.** Aquinas: "Whatever is set down in the New Testament explicitly and openly as a point of faith is contained in the Old Testament as a matter of belief, but implicitly, under a figure. And accordingly, even as to those things which we are bound to believe, the new law is contained in the old." (The host recommends Scott Hahn and Brant Pitre on the typological connections.)

[00:13] **Article 4 — Whether the new law is more burdensome than the old.** Aquinas: in some respect *less* burdensome — fewer ceremonial-ritual rules. In another respect *more* exacting — the heart must be monitored, not just the deed. Yet *ultimately* the new law is "a yoke that is easy, a burden that is light" because it gives the infused gifts and virtues and the indwelling of the Holy Spirit. 1 John 5:3 cited via Augustine: "his commandments are not heavy" — "not heavy to the man that loves; a burden to him that loves not." Love is charity, received with the Holy Spirit (Rom 5 again).

## Notes

- Q.106 a.1 is the single most decisive move in the whole treatise on law: *the new law is principally not text but person*. The new law just *is* the grace of the Holy Spirit inscribed on the heart. This is precisely the move that links the law treatise (Q.90-108) to the grace treatise (Q.109-114): law and grace are not opposed but co-instituted in the New Covenant. This is also where Rohr's *Universal Christ* and Wright's Pauline-covenant theology come together: the indwelling Christ-presence as the principle of the covenant community's holy living.
- Q.106 a.3's *historical-developmental* argument for why the new law was not given from the beginning is the closest Aquinas comes to articulating something like a *learning curriculum* over salvation-history. The bridge to Fredrickson is precise: developmental sequences require positive-affect broadening over time; one cannot bootstrap a "law of love" into an organism that has not first been formed under "law of fear" until its capacity for higher-order self-regulation has matured. The bridge to Friston: priors must be updated by experience before a more refined generative model becomes tractable.
- Q.107 a.1's "law of fear" vs "law of love" is the textbook articulation of *extrinsic* vs *intrinsic* motivation. Modern psychology has thoroughly validated the claim that intrinsically motivated behavior is more durable, more flexible, and more generative than extrinsically constrained behavior. The synthesis can make this bridge with citations to Self-Determination Theory.
- Q.107 a.4's "easy yoke" is the central Christological-pastoral claim that *fulfilling the new law is not painful when one is loving*. This is descriptively accurate: agents *experience* costly action as costly only when the action is misaligned with their dominant motivation. When motivation is aligned, the same objective action is experienced as flourishing.
- The desert-father quotation about "what loss of any common thing will injure him who boasts of perfect renunciation" is the asceticism of *advanced* practitioners; the synthesis should hold this carefully — the desert tradition makes specific psychological claims that don't generalize without grace and maturation, but at the limit they do describe something real about the structure of advanced spiritual practice.
