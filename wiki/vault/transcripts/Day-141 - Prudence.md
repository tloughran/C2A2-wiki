# Day 141 — Prudence

*Questions 46 and 47 of the Secunda Secundae — on folly, which is opposed to wisdom, and on prudence considered in itself. Q.46: folly (stultitia, from stupor, “numbness”) is contrary to wisdom (sapientia, from sapor, “taste”), the dullness that cannot taste or discern the highest cause (a.1); it is a sin in so far as a man voluntarily plunges his sense into earthly things and is rendered incapable of perceiving divine things (a.2); and it is a daughter of lust, which draws the mind downward to the greatest of bodily pleasures and blunts its capacity to be illumined from above (a.3). Q.47, prudence in itself (sixteen articles): prudence is in the reason, not the will (a.1) — “right reason applied to action,” concerned with the means to an already-intended end; it belongs to the practical reason alone (a.2); it takes cognizance of singulars, since particular circumstances change the right course (a.3); it is a virtue (a.4), a special intellectual virtue distinct from the moral virtues (a.5); it does not appoint the end of the moral virtues but ensures the rectitude of the means (a.6–7); its chief act is command, built upon counsel and judgment (a.8); solicitude belongs to it (a.9); it extends to the governing of many (a.10) in distinct species — individual, domestic, political (a.11); it is in subjects as well as rulers (a.12); true prudence is only in those with the right end, in a state of grace (a.13), and so in all who have charity (a.14); it is acquired by teaching and experience, not by nature (a.15); and it is corrupted not by forgetfulness but by the passions (a.16).*

## Notes

- “Right reason applied to action,” concerned only with the means; the three-act structure counsel → judgment → command, with command as the chief act (a.8) — maps onto active inference: policy exploration, evaluation, action.
- Prudence “takes cognizance of singulars” (a.3): the perspectival/particular bite — universals applied to contingent particulars; attention to the concrete.
- Prudence governs the MEANS; the END is set by the moral virtues / charity (a.6–7, a.13). Competence vs. telos — the hinge of the whole arc, and of Day 145's false prudence.
- True prudence only with the right end / in grace (a.13–14); the “sons of this world wiser in their generation” (Luke 16) = competence pointed at the wrong end.
- Folly as lust's daughter (a.3) and prudence corrupted by the passions, not forgetfulness (a.16): appetite degrades the precision of the model (pleasure/sorrow pervert the estimation).
