# Day 109 — Changing Laws (Q.96-97)

Q.96 covers the *power* of human law (its scope, limits, and the conditions under which it binds), and Q.97 covers how human laws *change* (and why change itself is costly).

## Transcript

### Q.96 — The Power of Human Law

[00:01] **Article 1 — Whether human law should be framed for the community rather than for the individual.** Law exists for the common good, so it must be framed for the sake of all — generally applicable, enduring across time and across persons. A traffic-safety rule that "everyone must use crosswalks" works for the common good even if some individuals could probably navigate the street without it; the state has no principled way to single out who is and who isn't excused.

[00:02] **Article 2 — Whether it belongs to the human law to repress all vices.** No. Aquinas: "Human law is framed for a number of human beings, the majority of whom are not perfect in virtue; wherefore human laws do not forbid all vices, from which the virtuous abstain, but only the more grievous vices, from which it is possible for the majority to abstain — and chiefly those that are to the hurt of others, without the prohibition of which human society could not be maintained. Thus human law prohibits murder, theft, and such like." Trying to forbid (e.g.) gossip by criminal sanction would either be held in contempt or provoke revolt.

[00:03] **Article 3 — Whether human law prescribes acts of all the virtues.** Human law, directed to the common good, requires some acts of some virtues — honesty in court, discretion with sensitive state information, courage in defense of the polity. It does not prescribe all acts of all virtues for all persons.

[00:04] **Article 4 — Whether human law binds a man in conscience.** Aquinas: "Laws framed by man are either just or unjust. If they be just, they have the power of binding in conscience, from the eternal law whence they are derived, according to Proverbs 8 — by Me kings reign, and lawgivers decree just things." Because the state arises naturally from man's social nature, it is divinely endowed with the right to legislate; conscience, well-formed, recognizes this and is guilty before God for disobedience to just laws. Romans 13 again grounds the point.

[00:06] **Article 5 — Whether all are subject to the law.** Some individuals are subject to certain laws not applicable to others (minors vs. adults). In a strict monarchy, the monarch may dispense himself from laws normally binding all — though he answers to God for genuine pursuit of the common good. Laws may apply to citizens but not to foreigners, or differently to those in subject vs. ruling states. Aquinas cites the Matthew 17 passage where Jesus tells Peter that kings of the earth take toll from others, not from sons, but pays the temple tax with the coin from the fish's mouth "lest we give offense."

[00:08] **Article 6 — Whether one under a law may act beside the letter of the law.** Aquinas: "It happens often that the observance of some point of law conduces to the common good in the majority of instances, and yet in some cases it is very hurtful. For instance, suppose that in a besieged city it be an established law that the gates of the city are to be kept closed. This is good for public welfare as a general rule; but if it were to happen that the enemy are in pursuit of certain citizens who are defenders of the city, it would be a great loss to the city if the gates were not opened to them. And so in that case the gates ought to be opened contrary to the letter of the law, in order to maintain the common welfare which the lawgiver had in view." The spirit of law trumps the letter when their conjunction defeats the lawgiver's intent.

### Q.97 — Change in Laws

[00:09] **Article 1 — Whether human law should be changed in any way.** One might expect not: just law is "an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community, and promulgated," and natural/eternal law are fixed. But human law should change when (a) a society has progressed such that a more perfect law is possible (Jesus on Moses permitting divorce "because of hardness of heart" but "from the beginning it was not so"); (b) a society has *degenerated* and previously enforceable laws can no longer be maintained without revolution; (c) a society changes its form of government to one better suited to its citizens' actual moral condition. Aquinas: "If the people have a sense of moderation and responsibility and are most careful guardians of the common good, it is right to enact a law allowing such a people to choose their own magistrates… But if, as time goes on, the same people become so corrupt as to sell their votes and entrust the government to scoundrels and criminals, then the right of appointing their public officials is rightly forfeit to such a people, and the choice devolves to a few good men."

[00:11] **Article 2 — Whether human law should always be changed whenever something better occurs.** No. Aquinas: "Human law is rightly changed in so far as such change is conducive to the common good. But, to a certain extent, the mere change of a law is of itself prejudicial to the common good." A law perceived as "just the way things are and always will be" is more likely to be obeyed because it stands as a settled dictate of reason and has stood the test of time. Changing a law itself dilutes the force of law. "When a law is changed, the binding power of the law is diminished in so far as custom is abolished. Wherefore human law should never be changed unless in some way or other the common good be compensated according to the extent of the harm done in this respect."

[00:13] **Article 3 — Whether custom can obtain force of law.** Yes: just as the will of the people or the sovereign can be expressed in spoken or written words, it can be expressed in deeds. Customs can become encoded in law, or they may have binding force themselves by showing tacit consensus among rulers and people. Aquinas: "If they are free and able to make their own laws, the consent of the whole people expressed by a custom counts far more in favor of a particular observance than does the authority of the sovereign, who has not the power to frame laws except as representing the people. If however the people have not the free power to make their own laws… a prevailing custom obtains force of law in so far as it is tolerated by those to whom it belongs to make laws for that people."

[00:15] **Article 4 — Whether the rulers of the people can dispense from human laws.** Yes — and in some cases ought to, since the ruler is the guardian of the common good. But dispensation must be granted prudently and exceptionally, the right belonging only to the supreme ruler(s). Aquinas warns: "If however he grant this permission without any such reason, or of his mere will, he will be an unfaithful or an imprudent dispenser — unfaithful if he has not the common good in view; imprudent if he ignores the reasons for granting dispensations."

## Notes

- Q.96 a.2's recognition that human law shouldn't try to forbid all vices — and the explanation that overreach would either be contemptuously ignored or provoke revolt — is a remarkably modern point about the *epistemic and motivational limits* of legal coercion. It anticipates contemporary discussion of legal moralism's enforcement costs and legitimacy problems.
- Q.96 a.6 (spirit vs letter) is the central anti-rigorist move in the whole treatise. Aquinas explicitly grants that the lawgiver's *intent* can demand violation of the law's surface text. This is a far more flexible Aquinas than the popular caricature.
- Q.97 a.1's two-way reasoning about democracy (good people earn self-rule; corrupt people forfeit it to "a few good men") is striking and worth weighing carefully — it expresses both the dignity-of-self-government intuition and a sober diagnostic about how cultural decline interacts with political form. A close reading would notice that Aquinas is *describing* rather than *commending* the second case.
- Q.97 a.2's insight that *change itself has costs* — that a law's perceived stability is part of its binding force — is exactly the result modern economics and behavioral science have arrived at (Schelling-point coordination, status-quo bias, rule-utilitarian commitment value).
- Q.97 a.3 anticipates the common-law tradition: deeds (customs) settle into legal force through tacit consent, not only through promulgation. The synthesis can connect this to contemporary work on emergent norms in agent-based systems.
