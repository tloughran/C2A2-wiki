# Day 205 — Kinds of Prophecy

Q.174 treats the division of prophecy — its kinds and degrees, with Moses as the greatest of prophets; Q.175 treats rapture, chiefly St. Paul's being "caught up to the third heaven."

We're covering today questions 174 and 175 of the Secunda Secundae, that is of the division of prophecy and of rapture. The translation that I always use in these

And so here we go. Article one, whether prophecy is fittingly divided into the prophecy of divine predestination or foreknowledge and of denunciation. These kinds of prophecy are distinguished according to their object. What kind of supernatural knowledge do they convey? Beginning with the prophecy of denunciation. This is prophetic knowledge of the future based

[01:16] on a contingent cause, of which the city of Nineveh is a perfect example. God prophesied through Jonah that in 40 days Nineveh would be destroyed, contingent upon it not repenting, which it did, and so was not destroyed. This kind of prophecy, St. Thomas teaches, foretells the relationship of cause to effect, which is sometimes hindered by some other occurrence supervening. And this can not only go for prophetic warnings but also promises as well. For a man could debar himself of some promised good if he drop present personal goodness.

[01:53] This kind of prophetic knowledge, based on the impending effect contingent on a current cause and subject to change, is called the prophecy of denunciation. There's also prophecy called foreknowledge, which is the prediction of some certain future effect which is known in itself as already present to God and therefore can be revealed to the prophet as that which will certainly come to pass. Foreknowledge, lastly, specifically referring to some certain and predicted good which will come to pass, is called predestination. Article two, whether the prophecy which is accompanied by intellective and imaginative vision is more excellent than that which is accompanied by intellective vision alone. In a sentence from Aquinas, the most excellent manner of prophecy is when a man prophesies by the mere inspiration of the Holy Ghost apart from any outward assistance of deed, word,

[02:26] vision, or dream. And this on account of being a more excellent transfer of truth, such as that similar to how angels receive knowledge and how man will someday partake in the vision of God. We also have an analogy here between school students and their teachers, that the more perfect student is the one able to simply grasp the concept conveyed, while the less capable require particular and

[03:02] multiple examples in order to grasp that more abstract principle. All this to say, the prophetic knowledge received into the intellect directly is more excellent than that which is conveyed via the imagination. Article three, whether the degrees of prophecy can be distinguished according to the imaginary vision. Within this article, St. Thomas rates the highest form of prophecy to the lowest. The highest form, as we've been saying, is when God impresses a truth directly into the mind. Beneath

[03:36] that, prophecy is judged more exalted according to the conveyor of that truth — like seeing an image representing God speaking or showing something, than an angel, than a man — along with the explicitness of the truth being conveyed. So by words being better than images only; also a waking vision will be considered superior to a sleeping vision, since the former comes with an affected withdrawal from the senses. Lastly, and also treated here, is what is considered underneath prophecy as normally understood. So one could have supernatural strengthening of natural truths, like Solomon had in judging and understanding, as is mentioned in First Kings chapter 4, as well as if a man be given a supernatural impulse to act, such

[04:12] as in Judges 15 where it is written of Samson, "The spirit of the Lord came mightily upon him, and the ropes which were on his arms became as flax that has caught fire, and his bonds melted off his hands." Article four, whether Moses was the greatest of the prophets. As it is written in Deuteronomy, "there arose no more a prophet in Israel like unto Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face, and all the signs and wonders which he sent by him to do in the land of Egypt, to Pharaoh, and to all his servants, and to his whole land." This would make Moses the greatest prophet not only because he spoke to God face to face, which

[04:47] describes the exalted way in which prophetic knowledge reached him, but also in Moses because of the greatness of his miracles by which he confirmed such prophetic knowledge — through all the plagues in Egypt, the parting of the Red Sea, the manna in the desert. Therefore, both according to the manner in which truth was conveyed to Moses along with the manner in which that truth was confirmed by miraculous works, Moses was the greatest of the prophets. Article five, whether there is a degree of prophecy in the blessed. Prophetic knowledge denotes remoteness from the prophet, either because the truth

[05:20] conveyed is something to occur in the distant future, or because of the remoteness of the medium conveying the truth, which is often through images which are of themselves remote from that truth being conveyed. The source of the prophetic knowledge is also something remote from the prophet himself, since the prophet is here on earth traveling towards heavenly union with God but is currently absent from that supernatural union with God; and since none of this will be the case for those being enlightened by the beatific vision in heaven, St. Thomas concludes that the blessed are not to be called prophets.

[05:55] Article six, whether the degrees of prophecy change as time goes on. And Aquinas says very bluntly that since Moses was the greatest of prophets, prophecy did not advance in degree as time went on. But probably more interestingly than that, St. Thomas teaches here that under each of the three divisions of human history — so before the law, after the Mosaic law, and after Christ — in each of these periods, the greatest prophets were the first to receive prophetic revelation in those periods. That would mean Abraham before the law, Moses who gave the law, and then the apostles after Christ. And in

[06:28] Aquinas's words, "the first revelation, before the law, was given to Abraham. For it was at that time that men began to stray from faith in one God by turning aside to idolatry, whereas hitherto no such revelation was necessary, while all persevered in the worship of one God. Again, in the state of the law, the first revelation, which was given to Moses, was more excellent. And on this revelation all the other revelations to the prophets were founded. And so too, in the time of grace, the entire faith of the church is founded on the revelation vouchsafed to the apostles concerning the faith in one God and

[07:01] three persons." Moving then on to question 175 of rapture. Article one, whether the soul of man is carried away to things divine. As it is written in the second letter to the Corinthians, "I know a man in Christ who 14 years ago was caught up" — raptum in the Latin, so "raptured" in English — "to the third heaven. Whether in the body or out of the body, I do not know. God knows." Therefore, a man can be carried away or raptured in the mind towards supernatural things. And what do we mean by being raptured away? Exactly. St. Thomas writes, "rapt, that is to say, uplifted contrary to nature." And since

[07:40] it is according to our nature to receive knowledge through the external senses and by then abstracting the essence from those things, then to be elevated in the mind towards knowledge of the supernatural while at the same time being withdrawn from the external senses is something contrary to our nature. This is what rapture is. And as we see in scripture and in response to our article, a man can be carried away to things divine. Article two, whether rapture pertains to the cognitive rather than to the appetitive power. And in a sentence from this article, "we speak of ecstasy not when the mind wanders

[08:16] through fear, but when it is carried aloft on the wings of revelation. Now, revelation pertains to the intellective power. Therefore, ecstasy" — or rapture — "does also." Article three, whether Paul, when in rapture, saw the essence of God. And Augustine on this topic writes, "Possibly God's very substance was seen by some while yet in this

[08:52] life, for instance, by Moses, and by Paul, who in rapture heard unspeakable words which it is not granted unto man to utter." Yet Aquinas steps in to say that the light of glory — which is what allows the mind to be informed directly by the essence of God, and was granted to such men in their rapture to know God as he is — was not an abiding quality in them, which is what heaven is, but instead something transitory. So did St. Paul see the essence of God when he was caught up to what he called the third heaven? Aquinas is of the opinion here, "man's mind is rapt by God to the contemplation of divine truth in one way, so that he contemplates it in its essence." Such was the rapture of St. Paul, also of Moses; and not without reason, since as Moses

[09:21] was the first teacher of the Jews, so was Paul the first teacher of the Gentiles. Article four, whether Paul in rapture was withdrawn from his senses. Normally, when we know something, we have to abstract it from our objects of sense, and then after that we still have to use words or images to think about that which we have abstracted. In other words, we need to see dogs in order to get the gist of what a dog is. But then even after that, we need to either audibly in

[09:51] the mind say the word "dog," or imagine a dog, in order to think about them presently, to think about that which we have learned. However, neither this way of learning nor thinking can assist a man in knowing and thinking about the essence of God. For God transcends not only all sensible things, but all created things. Therefore, for Paul to have had some taste of the essence of God, he would have had to partake in that vision as one withdrawn from external and interior sense, which was the question of this article. Article five, whether while in this

[10:29] state Paul's soul was wholly separated from his body. Separation of the soul from the body, from informing the body, is death; but it doesn't seem like St. Paul is asserting that he died and was resurrected in this experience of rapture as recorded in the second letter to the Corinthians. And we will discuss this question again in the next article. But Aquinas establishes here that, at the very least, separation of the soul entirely from the body was not requisite to have this experience. As he writes, "it was not necessary that his soul in rapture should be so separated from the body as to cease to be united thereto as its form. And yet it was necessary for his intellect to be

[11:04] withdrawn from phantasms and the perception of sensible objects." Article six, in our last article, did Paul know whether his soul was separated from his body? Augustine will then go on to say, "If the apostle doubted the matter, who of us will dare to be certain about it?" ## Notes

- **Denunciation vs. foreknowledge/predestination:** denunciation foretells a cause-effect relation that "is sometimes hindered by some other occurrence supervening" (Nineveh) — a *conditional forecast*, defeasible; foreknowledge/predestination reports what "is known in itself as already present to God," *unconditional*. A clean distinction between model-based prediction and view-from-outside-time.
- **The degree-ladder of prophecy is a ladder of mediation/abstraction:** highest = truth impressed directly into the intellect (no images), then image-mediated (God > angel > man as conveyor), waking > sleeping. The less the mediating interface, the higher the knowing — direct hook for interface theory and participatory vs. representational knowing.
- The teacher/student analogy: the "more perfect student" grasps the concept directly; the less capable need "particular and multiple examples." Few-shot vs. direct grasp — an abstraction/compression account of cognitive excellence.
- **Rapture = "uplifted contrary to nature," withdrawn from the senses, the intellect freed from phantasms** to (transiently) be informed by the divine essence. The central-theme limit-case: temporarily *removing* the perspective-limitation (the sensory interface) to glimpse more of the whole. Strong hook for Kastrup (de-clenching the dissociative boundary → mind-at-large) and Rohr (non-dual contemplative union).
- **Paul does not know whether he was in or out of the body** — even the paradigmatic rapture leaves its subject uncertain about his own condition. Perspective-limitation persists at the very threshold of the beatific vision.
