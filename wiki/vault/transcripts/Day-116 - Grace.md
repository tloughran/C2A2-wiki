### Q.110 — Of the Grace of God as Regards Its Essence

**Article 1 — Whether grace implies anything in the soul**

[00:57] Sometimes we use the word grace, such as when we say "he is in my good graces," to denote that a person is loved by another. Sometimes we use the term grace to mean something given gratuitously, or not on the basis of strict justice — like when we say "he graced us with his presence," meaning he didn't have to be here. In Aquinas's day, the word for grace in Latin was also used to mean grateful. [01:26] Our article asks if grace implies anything in the soul — and it does, because it denotes something given gratuitously from God to man, which according to St. Thomas is a special love whereby God draws the rational creature above the condition of its nature to a participation of the Divine good. And the following articles will serve to more deeply describe what that means, along with what grace is in itself.

**Article 2 — Whether grace is a quality of the soul**

[01:45] Remember that a quality is something in you; it is an aspect of you, or an accident, to use the philosophical category that it falls under. Your substance, or form-matter composition, is your intellectual soul informing the matter of your particular body, making you to be a human being — and if that sounds foggy, feel free to jump back to episode 34, on our discussion of form, matter, energy, and Dr. Werner Heisenberg. [02:13] Now, the reception or withdrawal of grace in us cannot be a substantial change for us, or it would necessarily entail the destruction of who we are at the most fundamental level — our substance — the removal of our substance, and then either the creation out of nothing, or out of prime matter, of another substance, the substance of some other thing. But Aquinas is certain that grace does not destroy nature but perfects it. That means that grace must be a modification of the same you, the same substance that persists across the change, across this reception or loss of grace. [02:53] And because grace is clearly not a quantitative change — it doesn't make you taller or wider — it's not a local or locomotive change — it doesn't alter your place in the world — and since, as we've just mentioned, it cannot be a substantial change, then we're only left with one option: it must be a qualitative change. This qualitative change which bestows the enduring disposition of grace is what we call sanctifying grace. [03:23] But there is also a graced movement of the soul, which can either come through that disposition or, from the outside, to a person without it, but in either case brings some particular graced action into effect, from what was potential to now actual — and that is what we mean by actual grace. And we will describe the quality of grace in greater detail as we move forward, but this article is mainly seeking to establish the kind of change in man grace entails. [03:56] And it is fitting for God to bestow the graced disposition, or sanctifying grace, and then the motion of grace — although he need not follow that order in all circumstances — because again, grace builds on nature, and that is how he moves us naturally: by reducing certain actions to act, but through our natural powers and habits. [04:16] Lastly, to quote Aquinas on the kind of change grace is in man, he writes: "Every substance is either the nature of the thing whereof it is the substance, or is a part of the nature, even as matter and form are called substance; and because grace is above human nature, it cannot be a substance or a substantial form, but is an accidental form of the soul."

**Article 3 — Whether grace is the same as virtue**

[04:50] And it is distinct. Aquinas says that it is analogous to the relationship between our natural light and the acquired virtues. So by our participation in the Divine light, we possess the light of the active intellect, by which we can then apprehend the essence of things, and then from that point acquire the habits of understanding, of wisdom, of science — and for more on the active intellect, see episode 41; for more on the intellectual virtue, see episode 89. [05:20] And just like we acquire natural virtues through a natural participation in the Divine light, so too the theological and infused virtues flow from, and are increased by, a supernatural light — the light of grace, which is a supernatural participation in the Divine nature. Therefore grace is not the same as a virtue, but it is the cause of, and the cause of increases in, the supernatural virtues. [05:57] And so, in order to distinguish between the quality of grace in the soul and that quality we call habits — habits in episodes 85 and 86 — St. Thomas writes: "Grace is not the same as virtue, but is a certain disposition which is presupposed to the infused virtues, as their principle and root."

**Article 4 — Whether grace is in the essence of the soul as in a subject, or in one of the powers**

[06:21] What is grace in? Not that the soul is in a place — unless one means that it is where it is acting. The article is asking, more precisely: what does grace modify? And we cannot say it modifies a particular power of man, because that is precisely what virtues and vices are — they're modifications of powers, of faculties. And as we mentioned in the article previous to this one, grace is instead the origin and the principle of the virtues; therefore it must modify the origin and the principle of the faculties, which is the essence of the soul. [06:53] In St. Thomas's words: "Grace, as it is prior to virtue, has a subject prior to the powers of the soul, so that it is in the essence of the soul. For as man in his intellective powers participates in the Divine knowledge through the virtue of faith, and in his power of will participates in the Divine love through the virtue of charity, so also in the nature of the soul does he participate in the Divine nature, after the manner of a likeness, through a certain regeneration or re-creation."

### Q.111 — Of the Divisions of Grace

**Article 1 — Whether grace is fittingly divided into sanctifying grace and gratuitous grace**

[07:34] And now on to question 111, of the division of grace. What are gratuitous graces? According to St. Thomas, he writes that they are graces bestowed on a man "not to justify him, but rather that he may cooperate in the justification of another," and so it is not called sanctifying grace. And it is of this that the Apostle says in 1 Corinthians 12:7, "And the manifestation of the Spirit is given to every man unto utility" — i.e., for others. [08:04] So, examples of that, as given here in 1 Corinthians chapter 12, it is written: "Now there are a variety of gifts, but the same Spirit; and there are varieties of service, but the same Lord; there are varieties of workings, but it is the same God who inspires them all, and every one. To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good. To one is given through the Spirit the utterance of wisdom, and to another the utterance of knowledge according to the same Spirit; to another faith, by the same Spirit; to another gifts of healing, by the one Spirit; to another the working of miracles; to another prophecy; to another the ability to distinguish between spirits; to another various kinds of tongues; to another the interpretation of tongues. All these are inspired by one and the same Spirit, who apportions to each one individually as he wills." [09:04] These gratuitous graces neither sanctify the man who receives them, nor presuppose that he is already sanctified — and that on account of them being intended precisely for others, for the common good, as St. Paul says. And thereby they are rightly distinguished against, differentiated from, sanctifying grace.

**Article 2 — Whether grace is fittingly divided into operating and cooperating grace**

[09:25] What do these terms mean? Beginning with a sentence from St. Thomas — it's very helpful — he says: "Operating and cooperating grace are the same grace, but are distinguished by their different effects." Okay. So in these terms we are talking about the one and very same grace; but when we consider that grace as coming from God, and related to the soul as that which is being moved by him, we call this grace operating grace. But from the perspective of the will, as not only being moved but moving — and precisely to some willed, specific, external action — then, from the perspective of the will itself being in play in this movement, we call that grace cooperating grace. [10:08] Hairsplitting? Or it may feel so. But to quote Aquinas, he writes: "Inasmuch as God moves the human mind to this act, we speak of operating grace. But there is another, exterior act, and since it is commanded by the will, the operation of this act is attributed to the will. Because God assists us in this act, both by strengthening our will interiorly so as to attain to the act, and by granting outwardly the capability of operating, it is with respect to this that we speak of cooperating grace."

**Article 3 — Whether grace is fittingly divided into prevenient and subsequent grace**

[10:55] As it is written in Psalm 59:11 of the Nova Vulgata, the official translation of the Church: "My God, his mercy goes before me" — *praeveniet* is the Latin here for "go before." Then in Psalm 23 it is written: "Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life." So there is mercy that goes before, mercy that comes after — but before and after what, precisely? It depends on what effect of grace you happen to be considering at that moment. [11:28] So, beginning with that list, St. Thomas gives us the five effects of grace. He writes: "There are five effects of grace in us: of these, the first is to heal the soul; the second, to desire good; the third, to carry into effect the good proposed; fourth, to persevere in good; and the fifth, to reach glory." [11:51] So within this order, one would say, for example, the healing of the soul is an effect of grace that comes before the effect to desire the good, and therefore the former grace would be called a prevenient grace, and the latter a subsequent grace — like we would call the number four, since it comes before five in counting, prevenient to it, and five subsequent. This is what prevenient and subsequent grace means across those five effects of grace in us: it means before and after. [12:25] In St. Augustine's words: "Grace is prevenient inasmuch as it heals, and subsequent inasmuch as, being healed, we are strengthened; it is prevenient inasmuch as we are called, and subsequent inasmuch as we are glorified."

**Article 4 — Whether gratuitous grace is rightly divided by the Apostle**

[12:47] These are the graces we listed from 1 Corinthians 12, such as the grace of prophecy and healing. In this article Aquinas defends the rationale behind each of them individually, and how each of them is truly useful to the salvation of another. [13:06] Now, men normally require both a message and some motive of credibility attendant to that message to believe it — along with, of course, when we're talking about the faith here, the interior workings of God in that man or woman. Therefore the gratuitous graces, which cannot bestow the interior workings of God, must be focused primarily on those other two pieces: teaching the faith, and providing evidence of its credibility. [13:26] For the sake of teaching is given the gift of supereminent certitude of the faith — that's Aquinas's phrase here — giving an eminent certitude to the teacher; that's helpful. Next, in order that the teacher may have a firm grasp of the faith's principles, is given the gift of wisdom, since wisdom entails knowledge of the highest, most principle truths in any science. [13:58] Then the gift of knowledge is given — the knowledge of particular effects of these causes — that the teacher might then be able to use those effects as examples and instances of the unseen truths which lie behind them, as St. Paul did, who wrote: "Ever since the creation of the world his invisible nature, namely his eternal power and deity, have been clearly perceived in the things that have been made." [14:18] Then, for the sake of bolstering credibility to the teacher's or preacher's message, is given the gifts of healing, working wonders, prophecy, and discerning hearts — which is God's gift of knowing the secrets of another's heart. [14:37] Lastly, the gift of kinds of tongues is given, which can mean speaking in another language by grace — like if an English speaker, only then in some graced moment, was able to speak Chinese or Arabic — as well as the gift of interpretation of tongues, which would be the graced understanding of the speech of another, which one naturally, normally, would not be able to do. [14:57] Then, in conclusion to our article, St. Thomas distinguishes the gratuitous graces of knowledge and wisdom against the gifts of the Holy Spirit, which also pertain to knowledge and wisdom. In his words, he writes: "Wisdom and knowledge are not numbered among the gratuitous graces in the same way as they are reckoned among the gifts of the Holy Ghost — i.e., inasmuch as man's mind is rendered easily movable by the Holy Ghost to the things of wisdom and knowledge; for thus they are gifts of the Holy Ghost. But they are numbered amongst the gratuitous graces, inasmuch as they imply such a fullness of knowledge and wisdom that a man may not merely think aright of Divine things, but may instruct others and overpower adversaries."

**Article 5 — Whether gratuitous grace is nobler than sanctifying grace**

[15:48] In our final article — absolutely not, because it is better to be holy than merely to do holy things. Now, of course, both graces may exist in the same one person, but there is an objective hierarchy between them, which we do well to remember. [16:07] I remember — and this may be a brief aside, so I ask for forgiveness in advance — when I was a seminarian, we were visited by a middle-aged lady who had just spent six years in prison for going into abortion clinics to hand roses to the mothers that were gathered in the waiting room there. And so here is this lady at the lunch table across from me at the seminary, and I begin telling a story about something — which I cannot remember — which had a moral to it, and I recall looking at her and seeing that she was learning from this story. Now, none of my professors would have learned from this story, but I could tell that she was, and it shocked me. [16:45] And I later realized why it shocked me — and it shocked me because, completely unknown to me, unconsciously as it were, I had equated holiness with knowledge. Here is this manifestly holy woman, and I was surprised to see her, in that moment, learning from this story. I had confused, and failed to distinguish, the greater grace — sanctifying grace — from the lesser graces. [17:10] Ultimately, what good is it to die with knowledge yet without that grace which makes us pleasing to God, which is sanctifying grace? And I pray that that is not my fate, nor the fate of anyone listening to this. But I hope the point is clear: holiness is the goal; sanctifying grace immediately unites us to that goal; and we should not pride ourselves in, or pursue as an end in itself, anything else — even if it truly be advantageous for others, like the gratuitous graces. [17:39] St. Thomas says: "The end is always greater than the means... but sanctifying grace ordains a man immediately to a union with his last end, whereas gratuitous grace ordains a man to what is preparatory to the end; i.e., by prophecy and miracles and so forth, men are induced to unite themselves to their last end. And hence sanctifying grace is nobler than gratuitous grace."

## Notes

- Aquinas uses hylomorphism explicitly: grace is an *accidental form* of the soul (not a substantial form, not a power/faculty, not the same as virtue). The argument turns on the rule that grace perfects nature rather than destroys it — so the change must be qualitative.
- The metaphysical category work here is unusually clean — grace is located "in the essence of the soul as in a subject," and from there it is the *principle* and *root* of the infused theological virtues. Good seam for Levin/hylomorphism bridge.
- Distinctions: *sanctifying* (habitual) grace vs *actual* grace (the divine motion); *operating* vs *cooperating* grace; grace *gratum faciens* (makes-pleasing) vs *gratis data* (given for others' benefit). Each is a different functional role of the same divine causal influx.
- Aquinas explicitly cross-references the episode on form/matter/energy and Heisenberg (episode 34) — the host already reads the medieval categories through a modern physics lens. The synthesis can pick that thread up without forcing.
- Central-theme hook: grace is described as God's "drawing the rational creature above the condition of its nature to a participation of the Divine good." This is exactly the perspective-limitation framing — grace expands the agent's perspective beyond what its natural resources can compute.
