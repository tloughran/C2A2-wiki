### Q.114 (I-II) — Of Merit

**Article 1 — Whether a man can merit anything from God**

[01:00] What is merit? St. Thomas tells us merit and reward refer to the same; for a reward means something given anyone in return for work or toil, as a price for it. Merit pertains to justice — it's the giving to another his or her due. So can a man be due a reward from God? [01:24] And our Lord tells us, on the one hand: "When you have done all that is commanded you, say, 'We are unworthy servants, for we have only done what was our duty'" — which seems to denote that man's actions cannot be due a reward. But on the other hand, if God were to choose, of his own free will, to reward certain things — like a parent might decide to reward a child for making his or her bed — then in this way one could be due a reward from God. [01:57] And it is in this way that eternal life is merited. As St. Paul says: "I have fought the good fight. I have finished the race. I have kept the faith. Henceforth, there is laid up for me the crown of justice, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will award to me on that day." And how that works out, we will spell out in the forthcoming articles.

**Article 2 — Whether anyone without grace can merit eternal life**

[02:20] Well, if he is in a state of mortal sin, he cannot merit anything, for he is an active enemy of God — similar to how a country does not reward those soldiers which are at war against it. Also, if man was never elevated to a supernatural state, but was at peace with God, even here he would not, strictly speaking, be due some reward from God for merely doing his duty — that was our Lord's words about an unprofitable servant. [03:00] However, man can be due a reward before God if God so wills to reward him for certain actions, and especially if he is elevated from simply being a creature to being a son or daughter of God by grace. And in this order of providence, in this actually existing universe, in which man has been called to a beatitude which is supernatural and beyond his natural powers either to know or to will — as St. Paul says, to what "no eye has seen, nor ear heard, nor the heart of man conceived, what God has prepared for those who love him" — in this order of providence, God has also determined that we are to reach this end via merit. As Jesus says, "Lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven," and in Matthew 25, "Come, inherit the kingdom prepared for you." [03:44] So then, merit is a possibility for us. And for man to know and will his correct end, and also to be able to be rewarded or inherit that end, then we must be divinized — and this is what grace does. It makes us likened to adopted sons and daughters of the divine, and therefore, from there, we can be rewarded like a son for making his bed, or like a son receiving the inheritance due to him from his father. And so, without grace, a man cannot, before God, merit eternal life; but with grace he can, and in the way described.

**Article 3 — Whether a man in grace can merit eternal life condignly**

[04:26] *Ex condigno* is the Latin here, meaning out of equality, or being of the same dignity. Can a man be due a reward because of his dignity as a son of God? And this is definitely the case, if God so chooses to elevate man to be a "partaker of the divine nature," as St. Peter calls it, and then determines to reward him as a son for actions done by grace. And this is commonly how one does inherit things — because of their dignity as a son or daughter, as sharing in the dignity of the family.

**Article 4 — Whether grace is the principle of merit through charity rather than the other virtues**

[04:59] And in a word from St. Thomas: "The merit of life everlasting pertains first to charity, and secondly to the other virtues, inasmuch as their acts are commanded by charity. So likewise it is manifest that what we do out of love we do most willingly. Hence, even inasmuch as merit depends on voluntariness, merit is chiefly attributed to charity."

**Article 5 — Whether a man may merit for himself the first grace**

[05:29] Which we answered extensively in the last couple episodes. Grace is a gift, as Scripture says, and gifts do not fall under strict justice — they are not wages or one's salary. Therefore the first grace, the grace of justification, cannot be merited. Although after that grace has been given, one can merit an increase in that grace; he can be supernaturally rewarded — but specifically because of his dignity as a son or daughter of God. [06:09] As St. Augustine taught: only grace makes every good merit of ours, and when God crowns our merits, he crowns nothing else but his own gifts.

**Article 6 — Whether a man can merit the first grace for another**

[06:23] And if man cannot merit the first grace for himself, we definitely cannot merit it for another either. Now Jesus Christ, the God-man, has merited the first grace, or the justification, for all those who will receive it — for he is the Son by nature, by the divine nature of his person. To this St. Thomas writes: "But Christ's soul is moved by God through grace, not only so as to reach the glory of life everlasting, but so as to lead others to it, inasmuch as he is the head of the Church and the author of human salvation," according to Hebrews chapter 2: "who hath brought many children into glory, the author of their salvation." [07:02] Now, we can merit the first grace for others *congruously* — that's from the Latin *congruo*, or based on a kind of fitness, fittingness. Intercessory prayer and penance is effective specifically because there is a kind of fittingness in that God, who desires that all men be saved, would hear the prayer — the earnest prayer — of those asking for that very thing, the salvation of another. [07:23] As St. Ambrose said to St. Monica, who was making ceaseless intercession for her wayward son: "Go your way. As sure as you live, it is impossible that the son of these tears should perish."

**Article 7 — Whether a man may merit restoration after a fall**

[07:43] No. If a man has fallen into mortal sin, because he has forsaken the principle of merit, which was his sonship through sanctifying grace. And a word from St. Thomas: "It is written in Ezekiel, 'If the just man turn himself away from his justice, and do iniquity, all his justices which he hath done shall not be remembered.' Therefore, his previous merits will no wise help him to rise again. Hence, no one can merit for himself restoration after a fall."

**Article 8 — Whether a man may merit the increase of grace or charity**

[08:15] If man can merit by grace eternal life, which consists in the vision of God — a vision which can be more or less in one than another, as we said in the last episode and in episode 7 — then it seems he must be able to merit an increase of that grace, so as to explain this diversity in glory. [08:37] Also, insofar as we are, for the remainder of this earthly life, acting either in accord with God's will or not, those acts of justice done by us specifically as divinized — or as adopted sons and daughters of God — do merit a reward, or an increase in our beatific reward which begins in this life, by a corresponding increase of grace. As our Lord said again: "Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust consume and where thieves break in and steal, but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven." [09:21] St. Thomas here writes: "But the term of the movement of grace is eternal life, and progress in this movement is by the increase of charity or grace, according to Proverbs 4: 'But the path of the just, as a shining light, goeth forward and increaseth even to perfect day,' which is the day of glory. And thus the increase of grace falls under condign merit."

**Article 9 — Whether a man may merit perseverance**

[09:43] Can a man earn the right to die in a state of grace — the right to persevere in grace all the way to the end? Here St. Thomas begins by pointing out that many attain to grace that do not persevere in it; as our Lord said, the way that leads to life is narrow, and those who find it are few. [10:04] He then explains how this can occur, and it is possible because the principle of grace is also the principle of perseverance in that grace — which in both cases is God. And in an analogy: imagine you are pushing a glass to the end of the table. In this motion, you are not only the beginning of that motion, but you are also the one who has to bring it to completion. If it is to come to completion — if the glass is going to get to the end of the table — you will have to be the one to get it there. [10:45] In St. Thomas's words here: "Perseverance of the wayfarer does not fall under merit, since it depends solely on the divine motion, which is the principle of all merit. Now God freely bestows the good of perseverance on whomsoever he bestows it."

**Article 10 — Whether temporal goods fall under merit**

[10:56] Can we earn the right to worldly goods from God? St. Thomas says: "What falls under merit does not come upon all alike, but temporal goods regard the good and the wicked alike, according to Ecclesiastes: 'All things equally happen to the just and the wicked, to the good and to the evil, to the clean and to the unclean, to him that offereth victims, and to him that despiseth sacrifices.' Therefore, temporal goods do not fall under merit." [11:28] However, we must say that "if temporal goods are considered as they are useful for virtuous works, whereby we are led to heaven, they fall directly and simply under merit, even as increase of grace, and everything whereby a man is helped to attain beatitude after the first grace." [11:50] So, in other words, the man who follows God should not expect to become rich or powerful, because these things are given and not given both to the good and the wicked alike. However, if there be some particular temporal good which God knows will assist a particular man in the attainment of that end for which God's providence is to bring him to, then he may merit, or be rewarded, that temporal good.

### Q.1 (II-II) — Of Faith

[12:19] And with that we move on to the third volume of the Summa, beginning with the question of faith. And as a prelude — for the sake of helping us see at the beginning where we will conclude when it comes to faith, and the components of that conclusion — here is the definition of faith as given by Saint and Cardinal John Henry Newman: What is faith? "It is the assenting to a doctrine as true, which we do not see, which we cannot prove, because God says it is true, who cannot lie. And further than this, since God says it is true, not with his own voice, but by the voice of his messengers, it is assenting to what man says, not simply viewed as a man, but to what he is commissioned to declare, as a messenger, prophet, or ambassador from God."

**Article 1 — Whether the object of faith is the First Truth**

[13:03] With any science which we possess in the mind, we can delineate first *what* is known, and then *how* it is known. When you understand that two things with one thing is three things — how do you know that? You know it because you pulled that truth from sense experience by the natural light of reason; you discovered that things have quantities and then logically worked out the precise number of these things in front of you. [13:45] So when it comes to theological faith, or saving faith: what is known, and how is it known? What is known is God — what this article calls the First Truth, the primary truth — as well as those things revealed by him as pertaining to the faith. Next, how is this known? It is known in a supernatural way, by God's own self-disclosure, his own revelation. So both from the aspect of what is known and how it is known, the object of faith is God, the First Truth.

**Article 2 — Whether the object of faith is something complex, by way of a proposition**

[14:17] And there's a way in which it's simple, and there's a way in which it's complex. In the sense that the object of faith is God — we believe God — it is something very simple. But insofar as this supernatural knowledge about God, albeit simple in itself, is understood by us in this life through propositions — like "I believe in God, the Father Almighty, creator of heaven and earth; I believe in Jesus Christ, his only Son, our Lord" — then it is something complex. [14:44] In St. Thomas's words: "The object of faith may be considered in two ways. First, as regards the thing itself which is believed; and thus the object of faith is something simple, namely the thing itself about which we have faith. Secondly, on the part of the believer; and in this respect the object of faith is something complex by way of a proposition."

**Article 3 — Whether anything false can come under faith**

[15:13] And this cannot be, because that which is known by faith is God, who is truth itself, and as revealed by God, who can neither deceive nor be deceived. And so neither from the aspect of what is known, nor how it is known, can anything false come under the object of faith. In a sentence from Aquinas, he writes: "The formal aspect of the object of faith is the First Truth; so that nothing can come under faith, save insofar as it stands under the First Truth, under which nothing false can stand."

**Article 4 — Whether the object of faith can be something seen**

[15:45] And Scripture answers this quite sufficiently. It is written in the letter to the Hebrews: "Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen." And we can add that this seeing is neither with the eyes nor the mind. Now, in heaven we will see God as he is, with the mind, in the beatific vision; but here we see God "by a mirror dimly," as St. Paul says — by faith, which is certain, based on the authority of God, but not seen like the conclusions of mathematics are seen.

**Article 5 — Whether those things that are of faith can be an object of science**

[16:23] Science here meaning certain conclusions which follow necessarily, or demonstrably, from true and known fundamental principles. Well, first of all, some of the conclusions of science are also contained within the faith. So, it is of faith that God is immutable — this is taught dogmatically and certainly by Scripture and the magisterium; St. James says, "Every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change" — but it is also known scientifically, as a certain conclusion within metaphysics. So, some objects of the faith are also objects of particular sciences. [17:09] When it comes to the science of theology, it is the case that it proceeds from the principles revealed by God to other certain conclusions — like the revelation of the incarnation leading to the dogma that Jesus Christ is one person with two natures, what we call the hypostatic union. [17:28] However, in regard to our article, on whether the things of faith are also an object of some other science, St. Thomas gives us this general rule: "All science is derived from self-evident, and therefore seen, principles; wherefore all objects of science must needs be, in a fashion, seen. Therefore there can be no faith about things which are an object of science."

**Article 6 — Whether those things that are of faith should be divided into certain articles**

[17:49] Which is a question kind of reminiscent of article 3. The truth revealed by God, and God himself, is something entirely simple in him; but to us, we do really need things spelled out and properly broken up into pieces for us to understand it. And that's why the Christian creed — the creed coming from the Latin word *credo*, or "I believe" — those things which we believe, is not just a word or a sentence long, but multiple articles long. [18:15] Aquinas explains: "Things which in God are one, are manifold in our intellect. Therefore, matters of faith should be divided into articles... Now, since the chief object of faith consists in those things which we hope to see — according to Hebrews, 'faith is the substance of things to be hoped for' — it follows that those things are in themselves of faith which order us directly to eternal life, such as the Trinity of Persons in Almighty God, the mysteries of Christ's incarnation, and the like. And these are distinct articles of faith."

**Article 7 — Whether the articles of faith have increased in the course of time**

[18:52] And this is a great question. The conclusion here is that, just like one could reduce all of our natural scientific principles to the first one on which they depend — and so they are in some way implicitly contained within it, that is, the principle of non-contradiction, that the same thing cannot be affirmed and denied at the same time — then so too one can reduce, and see implicitly contained in, the two articles of Hebrews 11:6 — where it is written, "Whoever would draw near to God must believe that he exists, and that he rewards those who seek him" — the rest of the articles of the faith. [19:29] So belief in God's existence implicitly contains all those dogmas related to God's identity, and that he rewards those who seek him contains all those ordinances pertaining to God's providence. [19:40] In an analogy: if I see a man two blocks from me, and you see that same man but two feet from you, you may also see that he's not just a man, but he's also your brother Jake, and he's also wearing a necklace, and also reciting a poem. But you and I are truly seeing the same man — your knowledge of him is just more detailed than mine is. [20:08] So we, who understand in a very detailed and specific way the Trinity and the incarnation, do not for that reason have a faith whose object is different than was Abraham's or Adam's. All of us have believed according to that dictate of Hebrews 11:6, and believed correctly — but, again, the explicitness of that faith has increased over time. [20:31] In St. Thomas's words, in response to our article: "Accordingly, we must conclude that, as regards the substance of the articles of faith, they have not received any increase as time went on; since whatever those lived later have believed, was contained, albeit implicitly, in the faith of those Fathers who preceded them." [20:49] Hence the Lord said to Moses: "I am the Lord. I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob as God Almighty, but my name the Lord I did not make myself known to them." David also said: "I have had understanding above the ancients." And the apostle — St. Paul — says that the mystery of Christ, "in other generations was not known, and it is now revealed to his holy apostles and prophets."

**Article 8 — Whether the articles of faith are suitably formulated**

[21:12] Article three, whether the articles of faith are suitably formulated — in the Apostles' Creed, the articles of the faith. They are suitably formulated, because they articulate both God's identity and those matters pertaining to his providence, our salvation. [21:21] To the first category, the creed delivers to us God's unity, when we recite "I believe in God"; then it articulates his Trinity, when we recite "the Father Almighty, creator of heaven and earth; I believe in Jesus Christ, his only Son, our Lord; he was conceived by the power of the Holy Spirit" — along with his works according to nature, which was creation, and then his works of grace and glory, along with the providential means of our salvation, which is Jesus Christ — which is articulated, or expounded, in the points which follow from the rest of the creed: [22:07] "He was born of the Virgin Mary; he was crucified under Pontius Pilate, died, and was buried; he descended to the dead; on the third day he rose again; he ascended into heaven and is seated at the right hand of the Father; he will come again to judge the living and the dead. I believe in the Holy Spirit, the Holy Catholic Church, the communion of saints, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body, and life everlasting. Amen." [22:31] So it's a kind of fluid and interlaced articulation of God's identity, his works, and his providential plan for our salvation, contained in seed form, from which all other dogmas can trace their foundation.

**Article 9 — Whether it is suitable for the articles of faith to be embodied in a symbol**

[22:40] In a creed, that is. To this St. Thomas responds: "A man cannot believe unless the truth be proposed to him that he may believe it. Hence the need for the truth of faith to be collected together, so that it might the more easily be proposed to all, lest anyone might stray from the truth through ignorance of the faith." [23:01] And he goes on: "The universal Church cannot err, since she is governed by the Holy Spirit, who is the Spirit of truth; for such was our Lord's promise to his disciples in John 16: 'When he, the Spirit of truth, has come, he will teach you all truth.' Now the symbol is published by the authority of the universal Church, and therefore it contains nothing defective." Well put, by a faithful Catholic.

**Article 10 — Whether it belongs to the Sovereign Pontiff to draw up a symbol of faith**

[23:28] Our last article — whether it belongs to the sovereign pontiff, so the pope, to draw up a symbol of faith. And this is important, because in regards to the Nicene Creed specifically, there is a grievance from our Orthodox brothers against the Catholic Church — the church founded by Christ — as to who has the right to formulate or reformulate a creed. And to this point, this article is really just saying again what was said in the previous article. [23:54] St. Thomas says: "A new edition of the symbol becomes necessary in order to set aside the errors that might arise. Consequently, to publish a new edition of the symbol belongs to that authority which is empowered to decide matters of faith finally, so that they may be held by all with unshaken faith. Now this belongs to the authority of the sovereign pontiff. Hence our Lord said to Peter, whom he made sovereign pontiff — in Luke 22 — 'I have prayed for thee, Peter, that thy faith fail not; and thou, being once converted, confirm thy brethren.' Consequently, it belongs to the sole authority of the sovereign pontiff to publish a new edition of the symbol, as do all other matters which concern the whole Church, such as to convoke a general council, and so forth."

[24:42] Which brings us to the close of another episode. We have just finished covering questions 114 and 115 — of the Prima Secundae — that is, of merit, and of faith. My name is Austin Habash with Think Catholic, and I cannot wait to see you tomorrow.

## Notes

- This is the volume-transition episode: 20 articles compressed, finishing Prima Secundae (Q.114, merit) and opening Secunda Secundae (Q.1, faith). Treat as bridge.
- Merit framework: there is no strict justice between creature and Creator; merit is possible only because God *wills* to make it so by elevating man to adopted sonship. Justice within the household of God, not justice between independent parties.
- Charity is the principle of merit — not the other virtues alone. "What we do out of love we do most willingly" — voluntariness is the qualifying condition. Direct Fredrickson hook: positivity resonance as biological substrate of the love that quickens merit.
- The first grace is not meritable; once given, increase in grace is. This sets up an asymmetry that maps cleanly onto active-inference learning curves where the prior cannot be earned by the agent but updates can be.
- Q.1 of II-II opens with the object of faith: faith's material object is varied (articles of the creed) but its formal object is *the First Truth*. The synthesis should mark this — faith's unity is in what it cleaves to, not what it inventories.
