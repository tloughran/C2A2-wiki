# Day 112 — Worship (Q.102-103)

Q.102 takes up the *causes* (literal and figurative) of the ceremonial precepts: why these particular sacrifices, sacred things, sacraments, and observances. Q.103 takes up the *duration* of those precepts — whether they pre-existed Moses, whether they justified, and whether they ceased at Christ's coming.

## Transcript

### Q.102 — The Causes of the Ceremonial Precepts

[00:01] **Article 1 — Whether there was any cause for the ceremonial precepts.** Yes — they were institutions of Divine Wisdom, ordained to an end (the worship of God), and so have reasonable causes that can be gathered from that end. "Consequently we must needs say that the ceremonial precepts were ordained to a certain end, wherefrom their reasonable causes can be gathered."

[00:02] **Article 2 — Whether the ceremonial precepts have a literal cause or merely a figurative cause.** Both. Aquinas: "Just as the ceremonial precepts foreshadowed Christ, so did the accounts of the Old Testament; for it is written 1 Cor 10:1 that 'all these things happened to them in figure.' Now in the accounts of the Old Testament, besides the mystical or figurative, there is the literal sense. Therefore the ceremonial precepts had also literal besides their figurative causes." The literal sense for the Passover instruction (Lev 23) is the historical mandate to Israel; the figurative sense is that the Passover foreshadows Christ — true paschal Lamb whose blood is shed, without blemish, no bone broken.

[00:04] **Article 3 — Whether a suitable cause can be assigned for the ceremonies pertaining to sacrifices.** Sacrifice expresses *recognition* that everything is from God and is directed to God. Aquinas: "In order to direct his mind to God, a right man must recognize that whatever he has is from God as from its first principle, and then direct it to God as its last end. This was denoted in the offerings and sacrifices by the fact that man offered some of his own belongings in honor of God, as though in recognition of his having received them from God, according to the saying of David (1 Chron 29): 'All things are thine, and we have given thee what we have received of thy hand.'" The chief sacrifice of all is Christ delivered to God "for an odor of sweetness" (Eph 5); the Old Law sacrifices foreshadow this one paramount sacrifice.

[00:06] **Article 4 — Whether sufficient reason can be assigned for the ceremonies pertaining to holy things.** Yes: the tabernacle and temple were both signs of Christ (the tabernacle contained the manna, Aaron's rod, the law — Christ is bread of life, eternal priest, eternal Word; the temple's divine presence foreshadows the Incarnation) and aids to worship by setting the worshipper's regard apart. Aquinas: "Man's tendency is to reverence less those things which are common and indistinct from other things, whereas he admires and reveres those things which are distinct from others in some point of excellence… for this reason it behooved special times, a special abode, special vessels, and special ministers to be appointed for the Divine worship, so that thereby the soul of man might be brought to greater reverence for God."

[00:08] **Article 5 — Whether there can be any suitable cause for the sacraments of the Old Law.** Yes — circumcision was a "seal of the righteousness which he had by faith" (Rom 4); Passover commemorated being led out of Egypt; purification rites set the people apart as belonging to God. All three are fulfilled in the sacraments of the New Covenant — baptism, Eucharist, penance, anointing — and pointed to Christ before his coming.

[00:09] **Article 6 — Whether there was any reasonable cause for the ceremonial observances.** Dress, dietary restrictions, manner of life. Aquinas: "The Jewish people were specially chosen for the worship of God… so too in that people's, and especially the priests', mode of life, there needed to be certain special things befitting the Divine worship, whether spiritual or corporeal. The reasons for these observances may be taken in two ways: first, according to their fittingness to the worship of God; and secondly, according as they foreshadowed something touching the Christian mode of life."

### Q.103 — The Duration of the Ceremonial Precepts

[00:11] **Article 1 — Whether the ceremonies of the law were in existence before the law.** Some ceremonial acts existed before Moses by personal devotion. Aquinas: "The patriarchs offered up these oblation, sacrifice and holocaust previously to the law, out of a certain devotion of their own will, according as it seemed proper to them to offer up in honor of God those things which they had received from him, and thus to testify that they worshiped God who is the beginning and end of all."

[00:13] **Article 2 — Whether at the time of the law the ceremonies of the Old Law had any power of justification.** No — citing Gal 2: "I do not nullify the grace of God, for if justification were through the law, then Christ died to no purpose." The ceremonies could cleanse corporeally/ritually but not spiritually. Hebrews 10: "for it is impossible that the blood of bulls and goats should take away sins." Aquinas: "It was possible at the time of the law for the minds of the faithful to be united by faith to Christ incarnate and crucified, so that they were justified by faith in Christ — of which faith the observance of these ceremonies was a sort of profession, inasmuch as they foreshadowed Christ."

[00:14] **Article 3 — Whether the ceremonies of the Old Law ceased at the coming of Christ.** Yes — the reality foreshadowed appeared in Christ. Col 2 again: "These are only a shadow of what is to come, but the substance belongs to Christ."

[00:16] **Article 4 — Whether since Christ's passion the legal ceremonies can be observed without committing mortal sin.** No — Paul (Gal 5): "If you receive circumcision, Christ will be of no advantage to you." Aquinas: "Just as it would be a mortal sin now for anyone in making a profession of faith to say that Christ is yet to be born, so too it would be a mortal sin now to observe those ceremonies which the fathers of old fulfilled with devotion and fidelity." To observe the legal ceremonies *as religious acts of worship* would be tantamount to asserting that Jesus was not the Messiah.

## Notes

- Q.102 a.2's two-sense reading of the ceremonial precepts (literal *and* figurative) is the Aquinas-meets-Origenism hermeneutic principle that anchors patristic-medieval biblical interpretation. The synthesis can connect this to contemporary semiotics: a sign can have *both* an indexical (causal/historical) reference and a *typological* (pattern-matching) reference, and the existence of the second does not negate the first.
- Q.102 a.3's "recognition that whatever he has is from God and direction of it back to God" is the deep structure of sacrifice across cultures (anthropologically). The bridge to Hoffman: if "the world" is an interface, then sacrifice is a *grammar* by which the agent acknowledges that the apparent goods are not self-grounded — and re-orders intentional structure toward the source.
- Q.102 a.4's "man reveres what is set apart" — the costly-signaling logic of liturgy — is now empirically supported in cultural-evolution studies (Henrich, Whitehouse): markedness and distinction are not arbitrary but track the cognitive economics of attention and commitment.
- Q.103 a.2's "faith in Christ incarnate and crucified, of which faith the observance of these ceremonies was a sort of profession" is Aquinas's careful position on how pre-Christian righteous figures were saved. It is Christologically integrationist. Wright's *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* makes a parallel move arguing that Israel's covenantal vocation was Messianic-directed.
- Q.103 a.4's "to observe the legal ceremonies as religious acts of worship now would be tantamount to denying Christ" is a strong claim with important hermeneutical history; the modern Catholic position (Nostra Aetate, 1965) significantly refines how to relate to ongoing Jewish observance. The synthesis should hold the careful theological logic without over-extrapolating into supersessionist polemic.
