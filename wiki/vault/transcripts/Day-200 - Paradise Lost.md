## Notes

- **Death as punishment** (Q.164 a.1): man was preternaturally incorruptible because grace gave the soul a supernatural sway over the body. Sin broke a nested subordination — will to God, passions to reason, body to soul, creation to man — so disorder, corruption, and death follow from the top failure cascading down.
- **The particular punishments are fittingly mapped to Genesis 3** (a.2): expulsion, the woman's pain in childbearing, the man's toil against thorns, strife between the sexes (Austin cites John Paul II's theology of the body: a shift from gift to use), and bodily death. 'Behold, the man has become like one of us' is read with Augustine as a deterrent-parable, not mockery.
- **Temptation by the devil was fitting** (Q.165 a.1): God tempts no one; only a fallen angel could; man in grace could have resisted 'sweetly'; and the permission served man's glorification — there is no praise in resisting if there is no real persuader to resist.
- **The order of the first temptation is the order of all temptation** (a.2): from sensuality (the serpent) to the lower reason via pleasure (the woman) to the higher reason via consent (the man) — a cascade up the cognitive hierarchy. 'In every kind of sin we find the same order as in the first temptation.'
- Synthesis hook: the fall as the rupture of a nested control hierarchy (Levin's scaled subordination / Friston's hierarchical generative model), and the temptation-order as a bottom-up cascade through it; Rohr's order-disorder-reorder frames the loss as the start of an upward path.
