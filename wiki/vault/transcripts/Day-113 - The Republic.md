# Day 113 — The Republic (Q.104-105)

Q.104 introduces and structures the judicial precepts (the third division of Old-Law content alongside moral and ceremonial). Q.105 examines their content: rulers, fellow-citizens, foreigners, household.

## Transcript

### Q.104 — The Judicial Precepts

[00:01] **Article 1 — Whether the judicial precepts were those which directed man in relation to his neighbor.** Judicial precepts are laws — civil or divine in origin — giving specific directions in civil matters and inter-personal interactions (contract law, the Cities of Refuge). They are distinct from ceremonial precepts (man's relation to God) and from moral precepts (which flow from the natural and revealed law). They are *judgments* by the correct authority for the sake of civil society.

[00:02] **Article 2 — Whether the judicial precepts were figurative.** Both historical and figurative. The Old Law judicial precepts came to a particular people in a particular time for a particular civil need; but like the rest of the Old Law they also pointed toward the coming Messiah. Example: if a slave was accidentally killed by an animal, the owner paid 30 shekels — and Christ, who "took the form of a slave" (Phil 2), is handed over for 30 pieces of silver.

[00:03] **Article 3 — Whether the judicial precepts of the Old Law bind forever.** Heb 7: "When there is a change in the priesthood, there is necessarily a change in the law as well." Aquinas: "The judicial precepts directed the people to justice and equity in keeping with the demands of that state; but after the coming of Christ there had to be a change in the state of that people, so that in Christ there was no distinction between Gentile and Jew as there had been before. For this reason the judicial precepts needed to be changed also." (What was perennially binding in the moral law remains binding.)

[00:04] **Article 4 — Whether it is possible to assign a distinct division of the judicial precepts.** Yes — fourfold: (i) precepts on rulers and subjects (Ex 22:8 — "you shall not revile God nor curse a ruler of your people"); (ii) precepts on subjects among themselves (buying and selling, judgments, penalties); (iii) precepts on the citizens' relation to foreigners (war, reception of travelers); (iv) precepts on household life (servants, wives, children).

### Q.105 — The Reason for the Judicial Precepts

[00:05] **Article 1 — Whether the Old Law enjoined fitting precepts concerning rulers.** The classical three forms are kingdom (one rules), aristocracy (few best rule), democracy (all rule). Aquinas in this article praises a *mixed* government — what Moses had — as best for preserving peace and durability: "All should take some share in the government, for this form of constitution ensures peace among the people, commends itself to all, and is most enduring. For Moses and his successors governed the people in such a way that each one of them was ruler over all, so that there was a kind of kingdom; moreover, seventy-two men were chosen who were elders in virtue — so that there was an element of aristocracy; but it was a democratic government insofar as the rulers were chosen from all the people." In *De regno* Aquinas argues for monarchy on natural-analogy grounds — heart, reason, queen bee, one God — but he qualifies: "A kingdom is the best form of government of the people so long as it is not corrupt. But since the power granted to a king is so great, it easily degenerates into tyranny unless he to whom this power is given be a very virtuous man… now perfect virtue is to be found in few." This Fall-grounded prudential reasoning is why the mixed form is preferred in practice.

[00:09] **Article 2 — Whether the judicial precepts were suitably framed as to the relations of one man with another.** Aquinas defends the Old Law's court system, regulations of private property, and exchange of goods. He addresses Hebrew slavery (Ex 21: six-year term, freedom in the seventh, families kept together; man-stealing punished by death) and notes the contrast with the slavery rightly reacted against today. Cicero: "A nation is a body of men united together by consent to the law and by community of welfare."

[00:11] **Article 3 — Whether the judicial precepts regarding foreigners were framed in a suitable manner.** Laws on warfare (offer terms of peace first per Deut 20; don't destroy fruit-bearing trees during siege); laws on resident foreigners ("you shall not wrong a stranger or oppress him, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt" — Ex 22). Citizenship was possible for certain peoples after some generations; some peoples were excluded on the grounds of a "proven and long-lasting history of being harmful to the people of Israel." On the *herem* (the command to utterly destroy certain peoples), the host offers an answer-from-providence: all men live under a death sentence; the God who has declared mortality has the right to determine when and how it is carried out.

[00:13] **Article 4 — Whether the Old Law set forth suitable precepts about the members of the household.** Servants: no overburdening in work; fair punishment; freedom in the seventh year with provision for the journey (Deut 5, 15; Ex 21). Wives: no slander without grave reason; one's hatred of a wife should not be detrimental to his son; bill of divorce rather than ill-use (a concession Christ later treats as such — "from the beginning it was not so"). Children: parents must instruct them in virtue and in the faith.

## Notes

- Q.105 a.1 is one of the most quoted Aquinas passages on political theology — the "best form is mixed" position. Note how Aquinas grounds the argument in (a) durability of peace, (b) commendation to all (= legitimacy from below, not just from above), and (c) the fact that perfect virtue is rare. This last point is the central pivot: the structural argument for diffused power is *anthropological* (the Fall has made perfect ruling-virtue rare), not metaphysical (which would favor monarchy by analogy with the cosmic order).
- The reconciliation of *Summa* I-II Q.105 a.1 with *De regno* is a serious scholarly question; the host's summary works. The deep point: Aquinas's natural-analogy argument is *defeasible* under conditions of moral corruption — exactly Wright's reading of the Old Testament narrative of Israel demanding a king "like the nations" against Samuel's warning.
- Q.104 a.3's "in Christ there was no distinction between Gentile and Jew" is Galatians 3:28 cashed out for civil-law theory. It is the foundation of Aquinas's view that the *judicial* precepts (along with ceremonial) cease at the New Covenant, while the moral precepts perdure. Wright's reading of Galatians and Romans is congruent.
- Q.105 a.3's offered harmonization of the *herem* commands deserves more nuance than the audio version offers. Modern Catholic biblical scholarship recognizes serious questions about genre (whether *herem* texts are descriptive of intent vs. polemic/typological), and Wright in *The Old Testament and the Cross* (and Goldingay, Boyd, Copan in their work) treats this as among the hardest theological problems. The synthesis should hold this with care.
- The household precepts (Q.105 a.4) on bill of divorce vs. ill-use show Aquinas treating the law as a *floor* that constrains worse behavior rather than a *ceiling* of moral aspiration — precisely Jesus's own framing in Matt 19.
