# Day 115 — Divine Assistance (Q.108-109)

Q.108 finishes the Treatise on Law by treating the *contents* of the New Law — what it prescribes and prohibits, including the evangelical counsels. Q.109 opens the Treatise on Grace with the necessity of grace.

## Transcript

### Q.108 — Things Contained in the New Law

[00:01] **Article 1 — Whether the new law ought to prescribe or prohibit any external acts.** Although the new law is "the law of liberty" (Jas 1:25), it does prescribe and prohibit certain external actions — because *act follows being*. The new law makes us sons in the Son through incorporation into his mystical body; certain acts are rightly expected of those who have received the Holy Spirit: keeping the commandments (Matt 19), confessing Christ (Matt 10), adherence to the sacraments (John 6: "unless you eat the Flesh of the Son of Man and drink his Blood, you have no life in you").

[00:03] **Article 2 — Whether the new law made sufficient ordinations about external acts.** Aquinas: "The new law had no other external works to determine, by prescribing or forbidding, except the sacraments and those moral precepts which have a necessary connection with virtue — for instance, that one must not kill or steal, and so forth." Other ordinances (civil law, ceremonial-ecclesiastical law) come through other proximate sources: "they are left to the decision of man — some relating to inferiors, as when a precept is given to an individual; others relating to superiors, temporal or spiritual, referring namely to the common good."

[00:05] **Article 3 — Whether the new law directed man sufficiently as regards interior actions.** Yes — directing *intention* (seek first the kingdom; "thy will be done") and *volition of the means* (away from lust, anger, covetousness; not judging rashly; not casting pearls before swine). Matt 6 prescribes prayer, fasting, alms — countering the three temptations of honor, pleasure, riches. Aquinas: "Whatever a man does in order to curb his desires comes under the head of fasting; whatever a man does for the love of his neighbor comes under the head of almsdeeds; and whatever a man does for the worship of God comes under the head of prayer."

[00:07] **Article 4 — Whether certain definite counsels are fittingly proposed in the new law.** Counsels are *recommendations* for those who wish to reach the beatific end more assuredly and expeditiously — *not* prescriptions incumbent on all. Matt 19:21 ("if thou wilt be perfect…"); Matt 19:12 ("he that can take let him take it"); 1 Cor 7:35 ("this I speak for your profit, not to lay any restraint upon you"). The three evangelical counsels — perpetual poverty, perpetual chastity, obedience (to a religious superior, as a vow) — and beyond these, any virtuous act above what is strictly required can be said to be done in accordance with the counsels.

### Q.109 — The Necessity of Grace

[00:11] **Article 1 — Whether without grace man can know any truth.** Without God man cannot act at all (the first, second, and fifth of the Five Ways). With God working through him naturally, he can naturally know many truths by abstraction from sense. But Aquinas: "Higher intelligible things the human intellect cannot know unless it be perfected by a stronger light — the light of faith or prophecy, which is called the light of grace inasmuch as it is added to nature."

[00:13] **Article 2 — Whether man can wish or do any good without grace.** Augustine: "without grace men do nothing good when they either think or wish or love or act." Distinguish: without *charity* (the supernatural habitual love of God as our due end), action is not directed to the right end, and so cannot be called good *simply*. But a man without grace can still build a house or feed his children — these are good in their species. The distinction is critical. Aquinas: "Man needs a gratuitous strength superadded to natural strength for two reasons: in the state of corrupt nature, in order to be healed; and furthermore in order to carry out works of supernatural virtue which are made meritorious."

[00:15] **Article 3 — Whether by his own natural powers and without grace man can love God above all things.** In *abstract* (state of perfect nature): yes — it is natural for the part to exist for the whole, for the hand to sacrifice itself for the head, for a soldier to die for his country. "Now to love God above all things is natural to man and to every nature." But in the state of *corrupt* nature, "man falls short of this in the appetite of his rational will, which unless it is cured by God's grace follows its private good on account of the corruption of nature." So now: cannot.

[00:17] **Article 4 — Whether man without grace can by his own natural powers fulfill the commandments of the law.** Trent: "God commands not impossibilities, but by commanding admonishes thee to do what thou art able and to pray for what thou art not able to do, and aids thee that thou mayest be able." Aquinas: man without sin in abstract could fulfill the commandments; in our actual post-Fall state, "the daily lived reality of every single one of us" is described in Rom 3 ("none is righteous, no not one") and Prov 24 ("a righteous man falls seven times"). But "what we can do with divine assistance is not altogether impossible to us… what we can do through our friends we can do in some sense by ourselves." Jerome: "our will is in such a way free that we must confess we still require God's help."

[00:19] **Article 5 — Whether man can merit everlasting life without grace.** No — Titus 3: "he saved us not because of deeds done by us in righteousness, but in virtue of his own mercy, by the washing of regeneration and renewal in the Holy Spirit." Aquinas: "Acts conducive to an end must be proportioned to that end. But no act exceeds the proportion of its active principle… Everlasting life is an end exceeding the proportion of human nature… hence man by his natural endowments cannot produce meritorious works proportionate to everlasting life, and for this a higher force is needed, the force of grace." Grace divinizes man so he may merit eternal life as a son of God.

[00:21] **Article 6 — Whether a man by himself and without the external aid of grace can prepare himself for grace.** John 6:44: "No man can come to me unless the Father who has sent me draw him." Aquinas: "To prepare oneself for grace is, as it were, to be turned to God; just as whoever has his eyes turned away from the light of the sun prepares himself to receive the sun's light by turning his eyes toward the sun. Hence it is clear that man cannot prepare himself to receive the light of grace except by the gratuitous help of God moving him inwardly." Jer 31: "Convert me and I shall be converted, for thou art the Lord my God"; Lam 5: "Convert us O Lord to thee and we shall be converted."

[00:23] **Article 7 — Whether man can rise from sin without the help of grace.** No — Gal 2: "I do not nullify the grace of God, for if righteousness were through the law, then Christ died for no purpose." Aquinas: "The order of nature can only be restored — i.e., man's will can only be subject to God — when God draws man's will to himself. So too the guilt of eternal punishment can be remitted by God alone, against whom the offense was committed and who is man's judge. And thus, in order that man rise from sin, there is required the help of grace, both as regards a habitual gift, and as regards the internal motion of God."

[00:26] **Article 8 — Whether man without grace can avoid sin.** Aquinas: "Man can avoid each act of sin only by grace. Nevertheless, since it is by his own shortcoming that he does not prepare himself to have grace, the fact that he cannot avoid sin without grace does not excuse him from sin." Trent again: God commands not impossibilities. Man without grace can keep from sin for a time, not for all time.

[00:27] **Article 9 — Whether one who has already obtained grace can of himself and without further help of grace do good and avoid sin.** No — a man with habitual knowledge of English still cannot think or speak English unless God as first mover reduces those actions to act. So with grace: even after one has the habit of sanctifying grace plus its infused virtues, he cannot act through those virtues without God moving him to act. Aquinas: "In order to live righteously a man needs a two-fold help of God: first, a habitual gift whereby corrupted human nature is healed, and after being healed is lifted up so as to work deeds meritoriously of everlasting life; secondly, man needs the help of grace in order to be moved by God to act."

[00:29] **Article 10 — Whether man possessed of grace needs the help of grace in order to persevere.** Yes — the *grace of final perseverance*. Aquinas: "Perseverance is called the abiding in good to the end of life; and in order to have this perseverance, man does not indeed need another habitual grace, but he needs the divine assistance guiding and guarding him against the attacks of the passions… To many, grace is given to whom perseverance in grace is not given."

## Notes

- Q.108 a.4's evangelical counsels (poverty / chastity / obedience) as recommended-not-required is the single clearest articulation in Aquinas of *supererogatory* moral structure. There is a *floor* (commandments — must) and a *ceiling* (counsels — may). The synthesis can connect this to philosophical work on supererogation (Urmson; Wolf) and to the practical recognition (in nearly every wisdom tradition) that some agents take on practices not required of all.
- Q.109 a.2's distinction between act-good-in-species and act-good-simply is critical. Aquinas does *not* hold that the non-Christian who feeds his children does no good — he holds that the act is good in its species but not ordered to the *due ultimate end*. This is far more careful than the popular caricature.
- Q.109 a.4 is the central question for understanding grace-nature relation: Aquinas explicitly endorses Trent's "God commands not impossibilities… and aids thee that thou mayest be able." The synthesis must hold both poles — the impossibility of fulfilling the law *of oneself* in the post-Fall state, *and* the genuine possibility of fulfilling it *with divine assistance*.
- Q.109 a.5's proportion-argument ("acts conducive to an end must be proportioned to that end") is the metaphysical hinge of the entire treatise on grace: a supernatural end requires a supernatural proportioning, which is what grace supplies. This is the move Wright reads as Paul's claim about "righteousness from God" (Rom 1:17) — not a forensic verdict but an *enabling* gift.
- Q.109 a.6's "you cannot turn yourself toward the sun without the sun illuminating your turning" image is precisely the structure of bootstrapping in *any* learning system: you need to already be in *some* relation to the target signal in order to update toward it. The synthesis with active inference is exact: priors must overlap with the truth distribution for learning to proceed at all.
- Q.109 a.10's "the grace of final perseverance" is the answer to one of the deepest practical-spiritual problems: even those who have received grace can fall away. The synthesis can hold this with Rohr's distinction between the "first half of life" (which has the rules) and the "second half" (which has the wisdom of falling and being restored — *Falling Upward*).
- This question is the natural pivot to *the central theme of the project*: the agent's perspective is limited; loving unity is the computational goal; "we work out our salvation in fear and trembling" because we cannot bootstrap into the right orientation without grace continually moving us. Synthesis must hit this firmly.
