# Day 111 — The Ten Commandments (Q.100-101)

A long episode (16 articles). Q.100 unpacks the moral precepts of the Old Law in detail through the Decalogue; Q.101 begins the treatment of ceremonial precepts. The discussion ranges from how the moral precepts relate to natural law through to the figurative/typological function of Old-Covenant worship.

## Transcript

### Q.100 — The Moral Precepts of the Old Law

[00:01] **Article 1 — Whether all the moral precepts of the Old Law belong to the law of nature.** Yes. Some natural-law precepts are grasped immediately by everyone ("honor your father and mother"); some only by the wise (e.g., "you shall rise up before the hoary head and honor the face of an old man" — Lev 19:32); some are judged morally right or wrong by our natural faculties *only after* revelation. In all three cases the mind is operating in its natural capacity, drawing morally obligatory conclusions, so the conclusions belong to the law of nature. Aquinas cites Rom 2:14 — "the Gentiles, who have not the law, do by nature those things that are of the law" — to ground the position.

[00:03] **Article 2 — Whether the moral precepts of the law are about all the acts of virtue.** Yes: the moral precepts direct "all those matters whereby human reason is well ordered" — which is effected by the acts of all the virtues, intellectual and moral. The Old Law directed the human person to conform passions and actions to reason, and then conform reason to revelation.

[00:04] **Article 3 — Whether all the moral precepts of the Old Law are reducible to the Decalogue's ten.** Yes, by reduction. Specific commands (e.g., Lev 18:21, "you shall not give any of your offspring to sacrifice them to Molech") are applications of a general Decalogue commandment (here, the first). What does *not* reduce: (a) primary intuitive principles preceding any positive law (the first principle of practical reason), and (b) conclusions that careful reflection of wise men shows to be in accord with reason. Aquinas: "Consequently two kinds of precepts are not reckoned among the precepts of the Decalogue — first, general principles… and again, those which the careful reflection of wise men shows to be in accord with reason, since the people receive these principles from God through being taught by wise men."

[00:06] **Article 4 — Whether the precepts of the Decalogue are suitably distinguished from one another.** Aquinas defends Augustine's enumeration over Jerome's (the standard Catholic numbering): the prohibition of strange gods and the prohibition of graven images are *one* commandment (since making images is forbidden only as objects of worship); the two coveting prohibitions are *two* (coveting wife = concupiscence of flesh; coveting goods = concupiscence of eyes). Three commandments referring to God, seven to neighbor.

[00:08] **Article 5 — Whether the precepts of the Decalogue are suitably set forth.** The first three follow from justice owed by a society to its ruler: fidelity (no other gods), reverence (no taking the name in vain), service/obedience (keep the Sabbath). The remaining seven concern justice within the community: give each his due, starting with parents (the first to whom we owe gratitude); then the duties of justice toward another's life (kill), conjoined-person (adultery), goods (steal), words (false witness), and finally inward desires (covet).

[00:10] **Article 6 — Whether the ten precepts of the Decalogue are set in proper order.** The things of God are well ordered. The order is from end (God-ward precepts) to means (neighbor-ward precepts), and within each block from greater to lesser gravity. Among the God-ward, treason (worshiping other gods) is graver than dishonor (taking the name in vain) than failure of service (Sabbath). Among the neighbor-ward, betraying parents (to whom we are most indebted) is gravest; the rest follow in descending order of wickedness.

[00:12] **Article 7 — Whether the precepts of the Decalogue are suitably formulated.** Mostly in the negative, because "negative precepts extend to more persons as a primary dictate of reason." Negations are broader than affirmations (Dionysius: "God is not corporeal" is true; "God is spiritual" is vague). Two precepts are affirmative — honor of parents and Sabbath observance — because those debts are *endless*; gratitude to God and to parents cannot be fully discharged by negation.

[00:14] **Article 8 — Whether the precepts of the Decalogue are dispensable.** No. "The precepts of the first table, which direct us to God, contain the very order to the common and final good which is God; while the precepts of the second table contain the order of justice to be observed among men, that nothing undue be done to anyone and that each one be given his due… consequently the precepts of the Decalogue admit of no dispensation whatsoever."

[00:16] **Article 9 — Whether the mode of virtue falls under the precept of the law.** No: the Old Law did not punish a man for lacking the stable disposition of temperance when he did not commit adultery — only for transgressing the law itself.

[00:17] **Article 10 — Whether the mode of charity falls under the precept of the Divine Law.** A nuanced question with conflicting historical opinions. Aquinas's position: (a) charity *does* fall under law as a precept, because God commanded us to love him with all heart, mind, soul, strength and our neighbor as ourselves — and supernaturally so, requiring infused habit; (b) charity is the formal mode of the acts of the other virtues — the right end informs the means: "the intention of the end is a formal mode of the act ordained to that end"; (c) but in another sense, charity does not fall under the precept of the Decalogue *as such* — one could honor one's parents in obedience to the letter without being a transgressor of the Old Law for lacking charity. Concluding statement: "Man cannot fulfill all the precepts of the law unless he fulfill the precept of charity, which is impossible without charity. Consequently it is not possible, as Pelagius maintained, for man to fulfill the law without grace."

[00:19] **Article 11 — Whether it is right to distinguish other moral precepts of the law besides the Decalogue.** Yes: to make explicit the conclusions following from the general principles. To "no false gods" was added the prohibition on burning children to Molech; to Sabbath were added ceremonial precepts; to "do not kill" was added prohibition of hatred and violence.

[00:21] **Article 12 — Whether the moral precepts of the Old Law justified man.** No — as the law cannot save today. Peter (Acts 15): "a yoke upon the neck of the disciples which neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear; but we believe that we shall be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will." Aquinas on Hebrews: "After the sin of our first parents, no one can be saved from the debt of original sin except by faith in the Mediator. But that faith varies as far as the mode of belief is concerned, according to the diversity of times and states." Those before the Incarnation believed implicitly (or, for the patriarchs, more explicitly) in the Mediator to come. Trent 1547 is cited at length confirming this.

### Q.101 — The Ceremonial Precepts in Themselves

[00:22] **Article 1 — Whether the nature of the ceremonial precepts consists in their pertaining to the worship of God.** Yes — including precepts on dress and food, which prepare and dispose the worshipper. Even today many religions use distinctive dress and dietary observance to assist contemplation.

[00:23] **Article 2 — Whether the ceremonial precepts are figurative.** Yes: they were a type or foreshadowing of what was to come. Col 2: "Let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and drink, or with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath. These are only a shadow of what is to come, but the substance belongs to Christ." Aquinas: "The things of God are not to be revealed to man except in proportion to his capacity, else he would be in danger of downfall, or he to despise what he cannot grasp. Hence it was more beneficial that the Divine mystery should be revealed to uncultured people under a veil of figures."

[00:24] **Article 3 — Whether there should have been many ceremonial precepts.** Yes: many among that people were prone to idolatry, so many ceremonial precepts were needed to recall them to true worship; and the mystery of Christ which the ceremonies foreshadowed required many signs.

[00:25] **Article 4 — Whether the ceremonies of the Old Law are suitably divided into sacrifices, sacred things, sacraments, and observances.** Yes. "The worship itself" = sacrifices. "Instruments of worship" = sacred things (tabernacle, vessels). "Worshippers" — their preparation = sacraments (consecration); their particular mode of life = observances (dietary, dress).

## Notes

- Q.100 a.10 is the centerpiece of the question and the bridge to the New Law in Q.106-108: charity is required to *truly* fulfill the law; charity is impossible without grace. Aquinas explicitly cites Pelagius as the foil. The synthesis can work with this; Wright reads Paul in nearby terms in *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*.
- Q.100 a.12 + Trent's clarification: Aquinas is clear that pre-Christic salvation depends on faith in Christ *as Mediator*, varyingly explicit. This is a Catholic position with a particular shape; Wright's reading of Pauline salvation history is congruent in pointing toward the universal scope of the Christ event.
- Q.101 a.2's "the things of God are not to be revealed to man except in proportion to his capacity" is a principle of *graded revelation* that connects deeply to how cognition and learning actually scale. It anticipates the developmental insight (Piaget; modern learning theory) that the optimal step size in instruction is set by the agent's current zone of capacity. Synthesis bridge to Friston/active inference: a generative model can only absorb information at its current update-rate; figures and ceremonies are exactly the kind of *intermediate representation* a learner can use.
- Q.100 a.5-6 build a tight rationale for the Decalogue's *order*. The argument is not "this is what tradition handed down" but "this is what reason would expect from a well-ordered law." Aquinas is doing rational reconstruction even for revealed material.
- The host's phrasing "spiritual infants" vs "spiritual maturity in Christ" is Pauline (1 Cor 3, Heb 5). It maps onto MacIntyre's "narrative of moral development" and onto Rohr's framing of the "two halves of life" — the first half oriented to law and tribal identity, the second to mystical/contemplative integration.
