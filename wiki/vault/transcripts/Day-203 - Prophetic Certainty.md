# Day 203 — Prophetic Certainty

Q.170 closes the treatise on temperance with its precepts; Q.171 opens the treatise on prophecy — what it is, whether it is a habit, what it can be about, and the certitude of its knowledge.

[00:05] Hello, my name is Austin Habash, the founder of Think Catholic, and you're listening to the Summa podcast, where we study St. Thomas Aquinas's Summa Theologiae in a way simple and insightful for anyone to understand. The Summa podcast is brought to you by Think Catholic. Taking two questions of the Summa a day will seek to summarize St. Thomas's responses, discovering the brilliance of Aquinas and his Catholic faith. This is day 203. So let's get started. We're covering today questions 170 and 171 of the Secunda Secundae, that is of the precepts of temperance and of prophecy. The translation that I

[00:42] always use in these podcasts is the one generously provided to me by Ave Maria Press, the five volume set translated by the fathers of the English Dominican province, which you can now get at a discounted price from Ave Maria Press's site by typing in the code Summa10. And so here we go. Article one, whether the precepts of temperance are suitably given in the divine law. As it is written in Exodus, "You shall not commit adultery. You shall not covet your neighbor's wife." So we see in the divine law precepts requiring not only temperance of the heart or the passions in declaring "thou shalt not covet," but

[01:17] also that external deeds be moderated by temperance, which is referred to in the phrase "thou shalt not commit adultery." However, in response to the objection that these precepts are too general and therefore not suitably given, we have to remember that the ten commandments were given to all, and in a way as to apply always, and so it had to be applicable to all. In other words, it had to be drafted broadly or generally. St. Thomas in responding to that objection writes the following: "The precepts of the Decalogue are universal principles of the divine law.

[01:53] Hence, they need to be common precepts. Now, it was not possible to give any common affirmative precepts of temperance, because the practice of temperance varies according to different times and according to different human laws and customs." Article two, whether the precepts of the virtues annexed to temperance are suitably given in the divine law. Does the Decalogue also give adequate credence to the annexed virtues of temperance, such as, let's say, meekness, which curbs anger, or studiousness, which curbs the desire for vain knowledge? And St. Thomas argues that although the Decalogue does not precisely explicitly mention these virtues like meekness or

[02:29] studiousness, it does make allowance for them in prohibiting their contrary vices as manifest and external action, such as in writing "thou shalt not kill" in regard to anger, or "thou shalt not have false gods" in regard to the vice of curiosity, which is sometimes superstitious. Or it also restrains pride by demanding deference to one's parents in the commandment "thou shalt honor thy father and mother." So although the virtues annexed to temperance are not mentioned specifically by the Decalogue, they are accounted for in prohibiting the external action which corresponds to their contrary vices. We move then on to

[02:57] question 171 of prophecy. Article one, whether prophecy pertains to knowledge. What is prophecy about? And beginning with a passage of scripture which we can then unpack. It is written in the book of Deuteronomy, "There arose no more a prophet in Israel like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face, and all the signs

[03:27] and wonders." Prophecy then is first of all about knowing that which is afar or far removed beyond the natural knowledge of man. This is why prophets have also been called seers. They have a depth of sight which is supernatural, as Moses knew what would befall the Egyptians and Pharaoh with the plagues. Secondarily, prophecy pertains to speaking or prophesying. As St. Paul said in First Corinthians, "the manifestation of the spirit is given unto profit." Meaning

[03:55] graces like prophecy are given for the church body. They're for others as well. And so for others to know, this word of prophecy must be conveyed to them by speaking or writing. Then lastly, prophecy pertains also to miraculous works, because the prophet must prove what they are saying is true, and they cannot prove it by appealing to natural reason, since what they know is above natural reason. Therefore, God himself must step in and testify to the truth of

[04:25] their words. And that is done ordinarily through the workings of miraculous deeds, as Jesus did when forgiving the sins of the paralytic and then doubted whether he had the authority to do so, then healed the paralytic as a divine witness to the words he had just spoken about forgiveness. This is also what occurred when the apostles went out and preached Christ crucified and then risen from the dead, which they then confirmed by the divine witness of miraculous works.

[04:54] Article two, whether prophecy is a habit. Is prophecy a stable quality in the man, and therefore something he can utilize at will? Similar to how you have the habitual knowledge of English and thereby can speak and write in English at will, or the man with the virtue of fortitude can face the fear of being killed at will. Is prophecy like this? And in response, St. Gregory the Great writes, "Sometimes the spirit of prophecy is lacking to the prophet, nor is it always within the call of his mind. Yet so that in its absence, he knows that its presence is due to a gift." And as a biblical example, even

[05:32] Elisha the prophet admitted that there were certain things he could not know, or he did not know, because God had not revealed it to him, in his own words. This is from 2 Kings chapter 4: "Let her alone, for she is in bitter distress, and the Lord has hidden it from me and has not told me." So it seems prophecy is not a habit. It is less like the sun's light, which the sun generates and thereby possesses, but more like the light illuminating the air, which is gained and

[06:02] lost at the whim of the source of light. In summary then, when God gives a supernatural light in the mind to the prophet, the prophet is able then by this light to discern truth which is above the natural. This is how prophecy works, and therefore cannot be considered a habit. Article three, whether prophecy is only about future contingencies. In a word from Aquinas, Gregory says that some prophecies are about the future. For instance, Isaiah chapter 7, "behold, a virgin shall conceive and bear a son." Some are about the past, as in Genesis 1:1, "In the beginning, God created heaven and

[06:38] earth." Some are about the present, as 1 Corinthians 14: "If all prophesy, and there come in one that believeth not, the secrets of his heart are made manifest." Therefore, prophecy is not about future contingencies alone. Article four, whether by divine revelation a prophet knows all that can be known prophetically. When the divine reveals something to the prophet, does the prophet then at the same time know all that can be known by that supernatural light? And we know from the scripture I already quoted, such as in the case of Elisha, that this is not the case. In general, if one knows the

[07:14] principle, then they can also see or discern from that principle other truths which flow from it. For example, from the principle of sufficient reason — that everything that exists must have an explanation for its existence and for having the attributes that it has — you can then deduce there must be a god, causeless and necessary. From there, you can deduce the act of creation. From there the fact that one is created and therefore has a debt of worship and religion towards the creator, and so on. Yet the principle of prophetic light is the divine light. It's the first truth, the essence of God, which no one can

[07:52] grasp in this life, because that is what heaven is: the deification of man in the vision of God, knowing God as he is, as is possible for man. And because man does not grasp this principle of the prophetic light, he also does not know at once everything that flows or can come from that principle, everything that can be known prophetically. Article five, whether the prophet always distinguishes what he says by his own spirit from what he says by the

[08:20] prophetic spirit. And our paradigmatic prophet, who serves as our answer to this question and evidence for what St. Thomas is going to argue for here, is the prophet Nathan, as recorded in 2 Samuel chapter 7, where he is asked by the king, "See now, I dwell in a house of cedar, but the ark of God dwells in the tent." And

[08:49] Nathan said to the king, "Go, do all that is in your heart, for the Lord is with you." But that same night the word of the Lord came to Nathan, "Go and tell my servant David, thus says the Lord, would you build me a house to dwell in?" So what happened here is that King David asked the prophet if it was God's will that he build the temple for the Lord, and Nathan says that it is, but he is wrong, for Solomon will build the temple of the Lord. Therefore the Lord had to come and correct the prophet Nathan. Therefore a prophet does not always certainly distinguish what he says of his own accord from that which he says as moved by the divine spirit. Now within this article St. Thomas distinguishes two ways in which the gift of prophecy affects the prophet: first

[09:23] by way of express revelation. And this revelation the prophet does know with certainty has come from God, such as the kind of certainty of the public revelation given the apostles, or to take an Old Testament example, when Abraham was certain that God had asked him to offer up Isaac — for no father on a whim or a guess that God has spoken is going to be willing to forfeit their son over it. Now opposed to this there is another manifestation of the spirit of prophecy

[09:53] which is through a kind of instinct, which can be murky to the prophet themselves. And this is what occurred with Nathan and King David. In St. Thomas's words, "And those things which we know by divine instinct are not all manifested with prophetic certitude. For this instinct is something imperfect in the genus of prophecy. Lest, however, this should lead to error, they are very soon set aright by the Holy Spirit, and from him they hear the truth, so that

[10:24] they reproach themselves for having said what was untrue." Article six, in our last article, whether things known or declared prophetically can be false. If a thing is truly known by express revelation, which we have spoken of already, in which by a supernatural light God bestows knowledge upon a man that is beyond his natural capacity to ascertain, and which comes with the certainty which the supernatural light provides him — kind of like our natural

[10:53] light of reason makes us certain that a part cannot be greater than the whole, or the number three greater than the number five — then this prophetic knowledge cannot be false, for it has come expressly directly from God, who can neither deceive nor be deceived. And so giving the last word in our episode to Aquinas, he writes here, "consequently the same truth must needs be in prophetic knowledge and utterances as in the divine knowledge, under which nothing

[11:23] false can possibly come. Therefore nothing false can come under prophecy." ## Notes

- **Prophecy is defined as knowing "beyond the natural knowledge of man"** — sight (seers), speech, and miraculous confirmation. The whole question is epistemological: a knowledge whose *source* lies outside the natural cognitive interface. Direct hook for interface theory and mind-at-large.
- **Prophecy is not a habit** — Aquinas's light-of-the-air vs. light-of-the-sun image: the prophet is *illuminated*, not self-luminous. Knowledge as transient participation in a source, not a stable possession. Flag for participatory vs. representational knowing (Kastrup/McGilchrist dialogue).
- The "principle" passage is striking: from a grasped principle one derives what flows from it (sufficient reason → a necessary God → creation → worship). But the prophet does *not* grasp the highest principle (the divine essence), so cannot derive all that follows. **Bounded inference from an ungraspable top** — central-theme keystone.
- **Nathan/David** (express revelation vs. murky "instinct") gives Aquinas a two-tier model: high-certitude direct revelation vs. low-confidence instinct corrected after the fact. A precision/confidence-tagging account of prophetic cognition.
- Closing claim: prophetic knowledge by express revelation "cannot be false," analogized to the self-evidence of "a part cannot be greater than the whole." The certitude is the certitude of the *source*, not the recipient — useful contrast with fallible model-based inference.
