# Day 188 — Temperance

*Aquinas closes fortitude with its precepts in the divine law (Q.140), then opens the treatise on temperance (Q.141). Temperance moderates the greatest bodily pleasures — those of touch, in food and procreation — bringing the concupiscible passions under reason. It is a cardinal virtue but not the greatest, since it regards the good of the individual rather than the many.*

## Notes

- Temperance moderates desire and pleasure of *touch* — the pleasures most natural to the animal, tied to preservation of self (food/drink) and species (procreation) (Q.141 a.4). The most resource-bound of the virtues: a precision-weighting / homeostatic-regulation problem.
- Its corresponding gift is *fear* — fear of offending God withholds man from the most seductive pleasures (a.1). Fear here is regulatory, not pathological.
- Temperance is cardinal (a.7) because it governs the greatest/most necessary pleasures, hardest to abstain from — but NOT the greatest virtue (a.8): justice and fortitude regard the good of the many (more 'godlike'); temperance regards only the individual's own passions.
- The rule of temperance is set by the *need of the present life and one's station* (a.6) — Augustine's 'moderation of a user, not the attainment of a lover'; Savonarola's medicine analogy. An explicitly homeostatic/resource criterion.
- Central-theme hook: temperance is the agent computing the good under the scarcity of its own bodily appetites — moderation as keeping the local model aligned to the whole.
