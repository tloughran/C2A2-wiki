### Q.58 — How the moral virtues differ from one another

[00:49] **Article 1 — Whether every virtue is a moral virtue.** We term those dispositions which affect the inclination of man moral virtues — for example, justice, temperance, courage, and the like — and this because, as Aristotle says, when we speak of a man's morals we do not say that he is wise or intelligent, but that he is gentle or sober. From St. Thomas: it is evident that inclination to an action belongs properly to the appetitive power, whose function it is to move all the powers to their acts. Therefore not every virtue is a moral virtue, but only those that are in the appetitive faculty.

[01:39] **Article 2 — Whether moral virtue differs from intellectual virtue.** We should expect the distinction between intellectual virtue and moral virtue, because, as Augustine puts it, sometimes we understand what is right while desire is slow to follow, or follows not at all. Therefore those habits which dispose us to understand rightly are fittingly distinguished from those which dispose us to appetite rightly — the first category pertains to the intellectual virtues (wisdom, science, understanding, etc.), and the second to the moral virtues (justice, temperance, fortitude). In St. Thomas's words: accordingly, for a man to do a good deed, it is requisite not only that his reason be well disposed by means of a habit of intellectual virtue, but also that his appetite be well disposed by means of a habit of moral virtue. And so moral differs from intellectual virtue even as the appetite differs from the reason.

[02:31] **Article 3 — Whether virtue is adequately divided into moral and intellectual.** In a word from Aquinas: in man there are but two principles of human actions, viz., the intellect or reason and the appetite. Consequently every human virtue must needs be a perfection of one of these principles: if it perfects man's speculative or practical intellect, in order that his deed may be good, it will be an intellectual virtue; whereas if it perfects his appetite, it will be a moral virtue. Therefore every human virtue is either intellectual or moral.

[03:07] **Article 4 — Whether there can be moral virtue without intellectual virtue.** Can a man possess good dispositions of temperance and fortitude without the habitual knowledge of art (which is right reason in those things to be made) or the habitual knowledge of science? We probably know intuitively that this is indeed the case — but one must possess prudence. In St. Thomas's words: there can be no moral virtue without prudence, and consequently neither can there be without understanding, for it is by the virtue of understanding that we know self-evident principles, both in speculative matters and in practical matters.

[03:44] **Article 5 — Whether there can be intellectual virtue without moral virtue.** This cannot be the case, at least when it comes to prudence, because when one's passions are unbridled or poorly disposed, judgment or choice is also skewed. As Aquinas says: prudence not only helps us to be of good counsel, but also to judge and command well; this is not possible unless the impediment of the passions, which destroys the judgment and command of prudence, be removed — and this is done by moral virtue. In order that he be rightly disposed with regard to the particular principles of action, man needs to be perfected by certain habits whereby it becomes connatural, as it were, to him to judge rightly of the end; this is done by moral virtue, for the virtuous man judges rightly of the end of virtue, because such as a man is, such does the end seem to him. So one cannot have prudence without the other moral virtues — yet one could still have intellectual virtues like science, wisdom, or art.

### Q.59 — Of moral virtue in relation to the passions

[05:09] **Article 1 — Whether moral virtue is a passion.** The definition of moral virtue given by Aristotle in this article is a habit of choosing the mean — the mean here meaning the middle, not in excess, against reason, appointed by reason as a prudent man would appoint it. Because virtues are habits appointed by reason, in Aristotle's words, they cannot be passions, because passions simply follow upon sensual apprehension, which is not an act of the reason. An important piece of the Aristotelian definition of moral virtue to keep in mind is the phrase "as a prudent man would appoint it," because judging the correct means to our intended end is not always cut and dry — it's not always abundantly clear what course we ought to take. So we should keep an eye on the virtuous man, on the means that he takes, on how he decides things, the path that he chooses — because if he is righteous, the righteous thing to do will bear a kind of connatural proportion to him, and therefore it will be attractive to him, this right course to take, in a way that is not to be found in the unrighteous. So in all things we have to put on the mind of Christ, as St. Paul says, and ask what path Jesus Christ would take in this particular situation.

[06:26] **Article 2 — Whether there can be moral virtue with passion.** Can one possess the virtue of justice and prudence even in the midst of passion? The succinct answer given by Aquinas is the following: if the passions be taken for inordinate emotions, they cannot be in a virtuous man such that he consents to them deliberately; but if the passions be taken for any movements of the sensitive appetite, they can be in a virtuous man in so far as they are subordinate to reason.

[06:53] **Article 3 — Whether sorrow is compatible with moral virtue.** Not only is sorrow compatible with moral virtue, it can even be a sign of moral virtue. As St. Thomas says, moderated sorrow for an object which ought to make us sorrowful is a mark of virtue. As an analogy: imagine a man who never felt pain when he placed his hands on a hot stovetop — we wouldn't consider that a good sign, that would be a bad sign. The virtuous man can and ought to sorrow for his past or present sins; he can sorrow for this fallen world around him; he can sorrow at the evils that are committed by others. Provided that this sorrow is moderate, or not contrary to reason, it can actually be a sign of virtue — a sign that this man perceives actual evils as evil.

[07:46] **Article 4 — Whether all the moral virtues are about the passions.** A sentence from Aquinas: reason directs not only the passions of the sensitive appetite, but also the operations of the intellectual appetite, which is the will, which is not the subject of a passion. Therefore not all the moral virtues are about passions — some are about passions, some about operations. In other words, temperance and courage are virtues about the passions, but justice is a habit of the will and prudence is a habit of the mind; therefore moral virtues are not only about the passions.

[08:25] **Article 5 — Whether there can be moral virtue without passion.** Since justice is a virtue of the will and not something modifying the passions, an act of justice — or an act of joy in the will — would not necessitate or essentially entail bodily passion. However, since we are a body-soul composite, it is fitting that our acts of the will should overflow into the passions, just as our acts of the will normally flow out into actual operation, which is carried out by the body. In St. Thomas's words: joy results from the act of justice, at least in the will, in which case it is not a passion; and if this joy be increased through the perfection of justice, it will overflow into the sensitive appetite, in so far as the lower powers follow the movement of the higher. Wherefore, by reason of this kind of overflow, the more perfect a virtue is, the more does it cause passion.
