# Day 147 — General Justice

*Questions 58 and 59 of the Secunda Secundae — the heart of the treatise on justice and its opposite. Q.58 defines justice as “the perpetual and constant will to render to each one his right” (a.1); it is always toward another, since equality is a relation between distinct parties (a.2); it is a virtue residing in the will (a.3–4); and it is in one sense general (legal justice orders every virtue to the common good) and in another particular (a.5–7), concerned with external operations under the aspect of one person’s relation to another (a.8). Its ‘mean’ is the real mean — an objective equality between thing and person, not the relative mean of the passions (a.9–10) — and so justice “stands foremost among all moral virtues” because it perfects the will and aims at the good of another (a.11–12). Q.59 treats injustice as the special vice opposed to particular justice (a.1), distinguishes doing an unjust thing from being unjust through the habit (a.2), holds that one can suffer injustice only involuntarily (a.3), and that doing injustice is mortal sin in its kind (a.4).*

## Notes

- The “real mean” vs the “relative mean” (a.10) is the synthesis-rich claim: justice’s standard is objective (the $10 for the $10 item), set by the relation between thing and person, not calibrated to the agent. Justice is the one moral virtue whose target is mind-independent in this way.
- Justice is general (legal) AND particular (a.5–7): the same virtue scales from ordering all virtue toward the common good down to one-to-one dealings. A nested part–whole structure — the same ordering at multiple scales.
- Justice resides in the will, not the passions (a.4); its act is “to consider the relation of one thing to another,” which “is proper to reason.” Justice is the will moved by a rational grasp of relation / otherness.
- Justice “stands foremost among the moral virtues” (a.12) because the will is higher than the sensitive appetite and because the other virtues serve the end of acting justly toward God and neighbor.
- One can suffer injustice only involuntarily (a.3, with the qualification that one may voluntarily accept less than one’s due) — Christ “willingly mistreated for the sake of others” is the host’s example; a hook for the kenotic / self-giving thread.
