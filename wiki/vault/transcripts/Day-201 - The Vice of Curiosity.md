Article two, whether studiousness is a part of temperance. Just as human beings have a natural desire for food and reproduction as animal, so too as rational we have a natural desire to know things. And just as a virtue is requisite to keep in check the desire for food or venereal pleasure, so too we stand in need of a virtue to moderate our desire for knowledge as well. So in response to our article, since temperance is about

[02:23] curbing desire, which is what studiousness is about, yet with an easier object of desire to resist than temperance, then studiousness is an annexed virtue to temperance. And although this virtue denotes primarily a curbing of inordinate desire to know, it also, by bestowing moderate desire for knowledge, assists a man to fight off the flesh when he may be tempted to study less than he ought to. As St. Thomas puts it, on the part of the soul, man is

[02:54] inclined to desire knowledge of things, and so it behooves him to exercise a praiseworthy restraint on this desire, lest he seek knowledge immoderately. Whereas on the part of his body, his bodily nature, man is inclined to avoid the trouble of seeking knowledge. Accordingly, as regards the first inclination, studiousness is a kind of restraint, and it is in this sense that it is reckoned a part of temperance. But as to the second inclination, this virtue derives its praise from a certain keenness of interest in seeking knowledge of things, and from this it takes its name. Moving then to the vice

[03:28] opposed to studiousness, we arrive at question 167, of curiosity. Article one, whether curiosity can be about intellective knowledge. Although knowledge, like food, like possessions, are not evil in themselves, they become evil if the desire for them and the use of them is not directed towards our true goal, which is heaven, or following the true means to that goal, which is imitation of Christ. The vice of curiosity then pertains to the desire for knowledge which is simply for the sake of vain glory, or really any other goal not ultimately set on God and neighbor. Or if the study be carried out

[04:03] on lesser things in avoidance of the greater things which one ought to be actually studying, or if one be studying something simply beyond them, futile for them to try and grasp at this time. There is also the vice of curiosity which would also be a kind of superstition, such as seeking knowledge through the demons, which we covered back on the virtues opposed to religion. In a word, summarizing how study can be vicious, Aquinas writes here: "Man's good consists in the knowledge of truth; yet man's sovereign good consists not in the knowledge of any truth, but in the perfect knowledge of the sovereign

[04:39] truth. Hence there may be sin in the knowledge of certain truths, in so far as the desire of such knowledge is not directed in due manner to the knowledge of the sovereign truth, wherein supreme happiness consists." Article two, in our last article, whether the vice of curiosity is about sensitive knowledge. ## Notes

- The whole treatment turns on **the end and direction of knowing**, not knowledge itself: knowledge is good, but becomes vicious when "not directed in due manner to the knowledge of the sovereign truth." This is a teleological/precision account of cognition — what to attend to, how much, and toward what end.
- **Studiousness as temperance** doubles in two directions: it restrains immoderate desire to know *and* it spurs the body's sloth toward the knowing it avoids. The virtue is calibration in both directions — flag for precision-weighting / active-inference resourcing.
- Curiosity's four failure modes (vain glory; lesser-over-greater; the futile/beyond-reach; superstitious knowing) are all **misallocations of finite epistemic resource** — a direct hook for the central theme's "computation under perceived resources."
- "Man's sovereign good consists... in the perfect knowledge of the sovereign truth" — the limit-case of the central-theme claim: the removal of all perspective-limitation is the vision of the simple whole.
- Austin frames sensible knowledge as licit when ordered to "sustaining nature or... the study of intelligible truth" — knowing-for-fitness vs. knowing-for-truth, the exact axis of interface theory.
