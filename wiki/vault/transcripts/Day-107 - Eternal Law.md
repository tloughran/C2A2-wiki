# Day 107 — Eternal Law (Q.92-93)

Yesterday's day-106 introduction covered the essence of law and gave the five-fold kinds. Today's two questions deepen the first effect (Q.92 — the making of men good) and the first kind (Q.93 — the eternal law).

## Transcript

### Q.92 — The Effects of Law

[00:01] **Article 1 — Whether an effect of law is to make men good.** Aristotle: "the intention of every lawgiver is to make good citizens." For a subordinate thing — passions ordered to intellect — it is good to be confirmed or aligned to that greater thing. Laws instruct a man to subordinate his particular actions and goals to his ultimate goal, happiness. They make men good by (a) instructing them in the path to that ultimate good and (b) giving them the opportunity to grow in virtue through obedience.

[00:02] **Article 2 — Whether the acts of law are suitably assigned.** "Acts" = functions. Law (i) permits, (ii) forbids, (iii) punishes, (iv) commands. Suitably enumerated because human acts are categorically evil (adultery — forbid/punish), categorically good (almsgiving — command), or categorically indifferent (choosing engineering as a profession — permit).

### Q.93 — The Eternal Law

[00:03] **Article 1 — What is the Eternal Law.** Augustine's definition: "the sovereign type by which we must always conform" — "type" meaning prototype/model. Just as a craftsman has the idea of the table in mind before building it, so God has in mind what each thing is, its nature, before bringing it into existence — including the perfection proper to it and the means to that perfection. This is the Eternal Law: the eternal rule concerning *what* a thing is, *what it is made for*, and *how it is to get there*. Aquinas: "Just as in every craftsman there pre-exists a type of the things that are made by his art, so too in every governor there must pre-exist the type of the order of those things that are to be done by those who are subject to his government… the type in him who governs bears the character of the law."

[00:05] **Article 2 — Whether the Eternal Law is known to all.** Augustine: knowledge of the eternal law is imprinted on us. Aquinas: "All men know the truth to a certain extent, at least as to the common principles of the natural law; and as to the others, they partake of the knowledge of truth, some more, some less." The eternal law as accessible by our natural powers is the natural law. Breaking the natural law (e.g., taking innocent life) thereby breaks the eternal law on which it founds.

[00:07] **Article 3 — Whether every law is derived from the Eternal Law.** Yes for just laws: "Human law has the nature of law in so far as it partakes of right reason; and it is clear that in this respect it is derived from the eternal law. But in so far as it deviates from reason, it is called an unjust law, and has the nature, not of law, but of violence." Even unjust laws, insofar as they retain some appearance of law through being framed by one in power, derive from the eternal law since "all power is from the Lord God" (Rom 13). Aquinas reproduces Romans 13:1-7 in support — every soul subject to governing authorities; rulers are God's servants for the good; pay taxes, render honor.

[00:09] **Article 4 — Whether necessary and eternal things are subject to the Eternal Law.** Aquinas: "All that is in things created by God, whether contingent or necessary, is subject to the eternal law; while things pertaining to the divine nature or essence are not subject to the eternal law, but are the eternal law itself." Affirmative for created necessary/eternal things.

[00:10] **Article 5 — Whether natural contingents are subject to the Eternal Law.** Irrational creatures are not subject in the sense of understanding the law and voluntarily submitting; they are subject by being moved by Divine Providence. Aquinas: "God imprints on the whole of nature the principles of its proper actions… consequently irrational creatures are subject to the eternal law through being moved by Divine Providence, but not as rational creatures are, through understanding the divine commandment."

[00:11] **Article 6 — Whether all human affairs are subject to the Eternal Law.** Yes: everything we do, we do for the sake of happiness, for the sake of some good — and that pursuit was placed in us by God. "In the wicked, the natural inclination to virtue is corrupted by vicious habits, and the natural knowledge of good is darkened by passions and habits of sin. But in the good, both are found more perfect, because in them, besides the natural knowledge of good, there is the added knowledge of faith and wisdom; and besides the natural inclination to good, there is the added motive of grace and virtue." The wicked fail to follow the eternal law perfectly through passion/vice/culpable ignorance; the righteous follow it supernaturally perfectly through grace and virtue.

## Notes

- The "type/exemplar/model" image is doing very heavy lifting in Q.93 a.1: it is simultaneously the divine plan of creation, the formal cause of each kind, and the normative pattern toward which the creature tends. This is exactly the territory Levin's morphogenetic-field program operates in scientifically — *form as a real, computationally active pattern that shapes development* — though Aquinas locates that form in the Divine Mind and Levin localizes it in bioelectric/anatomical attractor landscapes. The deep agreement is that "blueprint" and "process" are not separable.
- Q.93 a.6's contrast between perception-darkened-by-passion vs perception-enhanced-by-grace is a precise medieval anticipation of what Hoffman calls "the interface" being calibrated or miscalibrated, and what Friston frames as the agent's generative model being well-formed or poorly-formed against the ground truth.
- The Romans-13 articulation of derived authority is a bridge to Wright's political theology, which insists that "Jesus is Lord" is *not* a privatized confession but a public claim that all earthly authority is derivative and answerable.
- The eternal law is *not* itself God in essence but the conception in the divine mind by which God governs — this is the thread Rohr's "Universal Christ" picks up theologically: the eternal Logos as the pattern of the cosmos, which is now identified Christologically.
