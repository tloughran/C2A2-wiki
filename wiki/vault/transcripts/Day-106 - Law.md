# Day 106 — Law (Q.90-91)

Aquinas opens the long Treatise on Law (I-II Q.90-108) that anchors the rest of the Prima Secundae. With the "extrinsic principles of acts" introduced — the devil inclining to evil already covered, God inclining to good now to come — we begin with law (instruction) and will continue with grace (assistance).

## Q.90 — The Essence of Law

[00:01] **Article 1 — Whether law is something pertaining to reason.** Law stems from reason because it stems from our knowledge of (a) the first principle of practical reason ("good is to be done; evil avoided") and (b) particular judgments on what is in fact good or evil for us here and now. Aquinas's example: conscience is "knowledge applied to an individual case," a binding mandate stemming from the judgment of reason.

[00:03] **Article 2 — Whether law is always directed to the common good.** Particular goals must find their root in some ultimate goal. So too particular laws must direct us toward our ultimate good, happiness — and because man is naturally a social animal achieving happiness with and through community, law must direct the individual as a member of that community. The common good is defined (citing Fimister & Crean, *Integralism*) as a good shareable entirely by several persons without diminution — like truth or beauty. Aquinas: "Since every part is ordained to the whole as imperfect to perfect, and since one man is a part of the perfect community, the law must necessarily regard properly the relationship to universal happiness."

[00:05] **Article 3 — Whether the reason of any man is competent to make laws.** No: lawmaking belongs either to the whole people or to the public personage who has care of the whole people. Whoever is responsible for directing all to the end has the right to enact laws to that purpose.

[00:06] **Article 4 — Whether promulgation is essential to law.** Yes: a law obtains its binding force by being applied to those who are to be ruled by it, which application is made by promulgation.

Thus the formal definition closing Q.90: **law is an ordinance of reason, for the common good, made by him who has care of the community, and promulgated.**

## Q.91 — The Various Kinds of Law

[00:07] **Article 1 — Whether there is an eternal law.** Yes: law is a dictate of practical reason emanating from the ruler who governs a perfect community. The world is ruled by Divine Providence, and the Divine reason's conception of things is not subject to time but is eternal — hence this kind of law must be called eternal.

[00:08] **Article 2 — Whether there is in us a natural law.** Yes: the natural law is "the rational creature's participation of the eternal law" — an imprint on us of the Divine Light. It is our knowledge of the eternal law as accessible to us by the natural light of reason. The host's examples (sexual ethics, drunkenness, abortion) are presented as natural-law conclusions; the listener should note these are the host's tradition-specific applications rather than necessary corollaries of the bare definition.

[00:09] **Article 3 — Whether there is a human law.** Yes: from the precepts of the natural law as general and indemonstrable principles, human reason needs to proceed to the more particular determination of certain matters. These particular determinations devised by human reason are human laws. Civil law makes specific what would otherwise be murky or contested.

[00:11] **Article 4 — Whether there was any need for a Divine Law.** Yes: man is ordained to an end of eternal happiness disproportionate to his natural faculty, so he requires above-natural direction. Divine law also commands at the depth of interior thought and will (which human law cannot reach), and forbids interior sins (e.g., lust) that civil law could neither perceive nor enforce.

[00:13] **Article 5 — Whether there is but one Divine Law.** No: we have the Old Law (old covenant) and the New Law (new covenant in Christ). The Old Law promised temporal and sensible goods, directed righteousness in external actions, and was enforced by fear; the New Law promises eternal and immaterial goods, directs righteousness of the heart, and is motivated by love. As a father issues different commands to children and adults, so one God and one Kingdom gave one law to men while they were imperfect, and another more perfect law when they had been led to greater capacity for divine things.

[00:14] **Article 6 — Whether there is a law in the fomes (tinder) of sin.** Picking up Romans 7's "another law in my members fighting against the law of my mind": various creatures have various natural inclinations, so what is law for one is against the law for another. The law of man — by divine ordinance allotted to him according to his proper natural condition — is that he act in accordance with reason. This law was so effective in the primitive state that nothing beside or against reason could take man unawares. But when man turned his back on God, he fell under the influence of his sensual impulses. The "law of the members" is the sensual appetites or passions, which, insofar as they may incline to what reason forbids, are a law unto themselves at odds with the law of the mind.

## Notes

- The structure of the whole treatise on law is set here: essence (Q.90) and kinds (Q.91), with each subsequent question (Q.92-108) elaborating one effect or kind.
- The five-fold typology is now in place: eternal, natural, human, divine (Old + New), fomes.
- The "imprint of the Divine Light" image (Q.91 a.2) is the participation/illumination theme that links Aquinas to the broader Platonic-Augustinian tradition; it pairs naturally with Levin's "morphogenetic field" and Hoffman's "interface" — different vocabularies for "form/pattern that is more fundamental than the medium and gives rise to lawful behavior."
- The host's contemporary examples (sexual ethics, drug legalization in Denver) carry tradition-specific value judgments. The synthesis should keep them flagged as illustrations rather than as natural-law conclusions per se.
- Article 6 sets up the central problem that grace will eventually answer: a creature whose nature was perspicuously oriented by reason, but whose perspective is now darkened, divided against itself. The "fomes" articulation is the medieval anticipation of what active-inference frameworks call resource-bounded prediction-error under conflicting priors.
