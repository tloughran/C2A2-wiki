# Day 110 — The Old Law (Q.98-99)

Having treated law in general (Q.90-97), Aquinas turns to the divine law: the Old Law in Q.98-105 and the New Law in Q.106-108. Today's two questions are the opening overview of the Old Law (Q.98) and the structure of its precepts (Q.99).

## Transcript

### Q.98 — The Old Law

[00:01] **Article 1 — Whether the Old Law was good.** Paul: "the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and just and good" (Rom 7). Aquinas: "The Old Law was in accordance with reason, because it repressed concupiscence, which is in conflict with reason… moreover the same law forbade all kinds of sin, and these too are contrary to reason. Consequently it is evident that it was a good law." The Decalogue contains nothing repugnant to reason, which is why those laws remain in force.

[00:02] **Article 2 — Whether the Old Law was from God.** Yes — and given for two reasons: (a) to point prophetically to Christ, so that a prepared people might recognize the Messiah (Luke 24: "everything written about me in the law of Moses and the prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled"); (b) to form Israel interiorly by withdrawing them from idolatry and directing them to the one true God who is also three persons, preparing them for the fuller revelation in Christ.

[00:03] **Article 3 — Whether the Old Law was given through the angels.** Hebrews speaks of "the message declared by angels"; Exodus says Moses spoke with God face to face. Augustine resolves: "God spoke and appeared to him by means of a subordinate creature, i.e. an angel and a cloud. Again we may say that this vision face to face means some kind of sublime and familiar contemplation, inferior to the vision of the Divine Essence." The angelic mediation manifests God's glory as ruler of all; the contrast with the New Law (preached by Christ himself) shows the New Law's surpassing excellence.

[00:05] **Article 4 — Whether the Old Law should have been given to the Jews alone.** Aquinas: not because they were outstanding in themselves. He cites Deut 9: "Know therefore that the Lord thy God giveth thee not this excellent land in possession for thy justices, for thou art a very stiff-necked people," and Deut 8: "that the Lord might accomplish his word which he promised by oath to thy fathers Abraham, Isaac and Jacob." Election is gratuitous. "It is therefore evident that it was merely for the gratuitous election that the Patriarchs received the promise and that the people sprung from them received the law. And if again it is asked why he chose this people and not another, that Christ might be born thereof, a fitting answer is given by Augustine: 'Why he draweth one and draweth not another, seek not thou to judge if thou wish not to err.'"

[00:07] **Article 5 — Whether all men were bound to observe the Old Law in the same way.** No: only the Jews were bound to observe those parts beyond the natural law. Aquinas: "The Old Law showed forth the precepts of the natural law and added certain precepts of its own. Accordingly, as to those precepts of the natural law contained in the Old Law, all were bound to observe the Old Law; but as to those precepts which were added by the Old Law, they were not binding on any save the Jewish people alone."

[00:08] **Article 6 — Whether the Old Law was suitably given at the time of Moses.** Not only for advancing good men but especially for correcting evil men, who by Moses's time had grown prideful in what they thought they knew and could accomplish. The law exposed both — the standard of action men easily failed to reach. Peter in Acts: "Why do you make trial of God by putting a yoke upon the neck of the disciples which neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear?" For good men: "The law was given to them as a help… when the natural law began to be obscured on account of the exuberance of sin… it was becoming that the Old Law should be given between the law of nature and the law of grace."

### Q.99 — The Precepts of the Old Law

[00:10] **Article 1 — Whether the Old Law contains only one precept.** The Decalogue contains many commandments, but they are summed up at a higher level: Lev 19:18 — "You shall love your neighbor as yourself, I am the Lord"; and Matt 7 — "Whatever you wish that men would do to you, do so to them, for this is the law and the prophets." Aquinas: "The whole law is comprised in this one commandment, 'Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself,' as expressing the end of all the commandments — because love of one's neighbor includes the love of God when we love our neighbor for God's sake."

[00:11] **Article 2 — Whether the Old Law contains moral precepts.** Yes — "Do not kill, do not commit adultery, do not steal." The deeper insight: law's purpose is *friendship*. A friend is one to whom we will good as to an *alter ipse* — an extension of ourselves. To be a friend of God, we must become like him; the moral precepts assist this. Lev 19: "You shall be holy, for I the Lord your God am holy." Aquinas: "The principal intention of human law is to create friendship between man and man, so the chief intention of the Divine Law is to establish man in friendship with God. Now, since likeness is the reason of love… there cannot possibly be any friendship of man to God who is supremely good, unless man become good. Therefore it was necessary for the Old Law to include precepts about acts of virtue, and these are the moral precepts of the law."

[00:13] **Article 3 — Whether the Old Law comprises ceremonial besides moral precepts.** Yes — not only the Sabbath commandment but the very specific times and procedures of worship given through Moses. Aquinas: "Man is directed to God not only by the interior acts of the mind which are faith, hope and love, but also by certain external works whereby man makes profession of his subjection to God; and it is these works that are said to belong to the divine worship. This worship is called ceremony. Accordingly, those precepts of the law which refer to the divine worship are specially called ceremonial."

[00:14] **Article 4 — Whether besides the moral and ceremonial precepts there are also judicial precepts.** Judicial precepts are specific judgments given by legitimate authority applying the general principles of natural and revealed law to civil justice. The Old Law contains them — e.g., the Cities of Refuge for those who unintentionally killed (Num 35). Aquinas: "We must therefore distinguish three kinds of precept in the Old Law: moral precepts which are dictated by the natural law; ceremonial precepts which are determinations of the divine worship; and judicial precepts which are determinations of the justice to be observed among men."

[00:16] **Article 5 — Whether the Old Law contains any other besides the moral, judicial, and ceremonial precepts.** No: Deut 6 — "These are the precepts and ceremonies and judgments which the Lord your God commanded you" — placed at the beginning of the law, includes all precepts.

[00:17] **Article 6 — Whether the Old Law should have induced men to the observance of its precepts by means of temporal promises and threats.** Yes for the people of the old covenant — spiritual children compared to those in Christ. As a father uses temporal carrots and sticks with an adolescent child, so God acted with Israel. Christ expects more of those made more by his grace. "As in speculative sciences men are persuaded to assent to the conclusions by means of syllogistic arguments, so too in every law men are persuaded to observe its precepts by means of punishments and rewards… he who would persuade a man to the observance of any precepts needs to move him at first by things for which he has an affection… It was therefore fitting that the Old Law should conduct men to God by means of temporal goods for which the imperfect have an affection."

## Notes

- Q.98 a.6's "the Old Law was given between the law of nature and the law of grace" frames the whole treatise as a *developmental* sequence: nature → corrupted nature requiring written instruction → grace healing and elevating. This is exactly the framing Wright uses for biblical theology (the Pauline narrative of Israel's vocation), and exactly the framing Fredrickson uses developmentally for emotional and relational maturation (broaden-and-build operating across stages).
- Q.99 a.2's identification of *friendship* as the chief intention of the divine law — and "likeness is the reason of love" — is the single most important theological move in the question. It links the whole treatise to II-II Q.23 (charity = friendship with God). The synthesis should weight it.
- Q.99 a.6's "first move him by what he has affection for" is the precise pedagogical/developmental principle Fredrickson empirically formalizes: positive affect *broadens* future behavior; one cannot bootstrap right action without engaging the agent's current motivational state. The same principle is exhibited in active-inference frameworks: an agent only learns from prediction-error in a regime where its priors aren't too distant from observed reward.
- Q.98 a.4's gratuitous election is a hard passage that Wright treats at length: Israel is chosen not for merit but for *vocation* — to be the people through whom God blesses the nations. The synthesis can carry this carefully without flattening it.
- Aquinas's distinction between moral / ceremonial / judicial precepts will become important in Q.103-105 when he discusses the *cessation* of the ceremonial and judicial precepts with the New Law.
