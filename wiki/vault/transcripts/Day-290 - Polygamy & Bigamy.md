The operative sentence from Aquinas in this article is the following. Whatever renders an action in proportionate to the end which nature intends to obtain

[01:10] by certain work is said to be contrary to the natural law. By an action or but an action may be in proportionate either to the principle or

to the secondary end. All right, so is polygamy contrary to the primary end of marriage, which is the beginning of offspring? And the answer is no, a man

can have children with multiple wives quite easily. He can be assured that those children are truly his. However, polygamy does hinder the secondary goods

of marriage because man is a rational animal. He is made for friendship, which is hindered between a man and his wife when there are other wives present. What

[01:46] is lost here is what's called the good of fidelity. Polygamy also hinders man in so far as marriage in the new covenant is intended

to be a symbol of Christ's union with his one bride, the church. Therefore, since polygamy hinders these secondary ends which flow from man's

nature or flow from man's nature to worship God in the manner he has appointed, which means holding marriage as a sacrament today, for these reasons

polygamy is said to be contrary to the natural law. As Aquinas puts it, though polygamy does not wholly destroy the second end, it hinders it considerably.

[02:19] For there cannot easily be peace in a family where several wives are joined to one husband since one husband cannot suffice to satisfy the requisitions of

several wives. And again, because the sharing of several in one occupation is a cause of strife. Thus, potters quarrel with one another. And in like manner,

the several wives of one husband. The third end it removes altogether because as Christ is one, so also is the church one.

Article 2, whether it was ever lawful to have several wives. Polygamy was lawful in the Old Testament, not only in the case of Jacob

[02:51] and David, but also in Deuteronomy 21. It states, if a man have two wives, which presumes such is permitted in the old law. Now, since the secondary end of

marriage before the incarnation was not a sacrament, it wasn't Christ's union with the church, but it still contained the good of friendship among the

spouses, the due order of family life. And if polygamy in a certain age and among certain people was somehow expedient, though, unto these purposes,

then it could be permissible as not contrary to the goods of nature. Yet, such a determination being difficult to figure out on one's own should come from

[03:25] the ultimate legislator of all laws, which is God. This is what we see happening in the old law. On this point, it's good to quote St. Thomas at length.

He writes, "But because it's not easy to determine the above variations, it belongs exclusively to him from whose authority

he derives its binding force to permit the non-observance of the law in those cases to which the force of law ought not to extend. And this permission is

called a dispensation. Now, the law prescribing the one wife was framed not by man, but by God. Nor was it ever given by word or in writing,

[04:00] but was imprinted on the heart like other things belonging in any way to the natural law. Consequently, a dispensation in this matter could be

granted by God alone through an inward inspiration vouchsafed originally to the holy patriarchs and by their example continued to others at a time when it

behooved the aforesaid precept not to be observed in order to ensure the multiplication of the offspring to be brought up in the worship of God. For

the principal end is ever to be borne in mind before the secondary end. Wherefore, since the good of the offspring is the principal end of

[04:32] marriage, it behooved to disregard for a time the impediment that might arise to the secondary ends when it was necessary for offspring to be multiplied. Article

3, whether it is against the natural law to have a concubine. Concubinage is what we normally mean by living with a partner who is not your spouse, whom you

are having intercourse with. And it is contrary to natural law since the end or natural goal of intercourse is the beginning of children, which thereby

also entails the rearing and formation of those children, which needs both parents and both of those parents for a very long time. Such is the reason for

[05:09] why marriage is an office of nature. The person living in concubinage or committing fornication on the one hand either doesn't have this correct end in

mind or doesn't realize that the accomplishment of such an end entails marriage. Article 4, whether it is against the natural law to have a

concubine. In brief from our article, nothing but mortal sin excludes from the kingdom of God. But fornicators are excluded from

Solomon, "He had 700 wives, princesses, 300 concubines, and his wives turned away his heart." And Aquinas' response is that

fornication and adultery being contrary to the natural law essentially, namely according to the essential primary end of intercourse, cannot be permitted and

[06:19] has never been lawful. And thus, it seems that the concubines in the old law, at least in the places where it doesn't look like it is explicitly

condemned, actually entailed the concubines really living as additional wives. Yet, could still be distinguished from wives most likely because of their

distance from actual friendship and fellowship with the partner in question. And when it comes to the case of kings like David or Solomon, the book of

Deuteronomy explicitly states, "And he shall not multiply wives for himself lest his heart turn away." Therefore, we need not be terribly concerned trying to

[06:52] defend the actions of those who seemed to have already been become transgressors of the law in this regard. We move then on to question 66, of

bigamy and of the irregularity contracted thereby. Article 1, whether irregularity attaches to bigamy. By the term irregularity, what St. Thomas means

here is that we are dealing with what would prevent a licit ordination, someone being ordained. Bigamy is of a couple of different kinds

first of all, which Aquinas lists for us. He writes, "There are four kinds of bigamy. The first is when a man has several lawful wives successively. The

[07:26] second is when a man has several wives at once, one in law, the other in fact. The third, when he has several successively,

one in law, the other in fact. The fourth, when a man marries a widow. Accordingly, irregularity attaches to all of these.

Why these create an irregularity, making it unlawful to ordain such a man, is because by one's ordination, this man is made a sign of Christ's union with the

church, which is a union between one and another one. All of the above scenarios, however, are not an exclusive union between one and

[07:58] another, but in some way have implied implied someone else. Moreover, as an important aside, in both 1 Timothy and Titus, St. Paul stresses

that a bishop should be the husband of one wife, which does not mean one should not have multiple wives at one time, which would have been simply assumed as

forbidden and not needed to be mentioned, but that one should not have remarried after his spouse died if this man was to be ordained.

And this is really because continence was very important for the early Christians, and thus the man who was not satisfied with one wife, but after she

[08:30] dies is looking for another shows himself incapable to resist concupiscence, not likely to be able to live perfect continence, and definitely

not someone to be held up as a spiritual example, a leader for others. Article 2, whether irregularity results from bigamy when one husband has two wives, one in

law, the other in fact. If a man has a wife and a side mistress, then his bigamy will, of course, be a hindrance to his ordination to be another Christ,

an alter Christus, who is to symbolize Christ's union with the one bride, the church. Article 3, whether irregularity is contracted by marrying one who is not

[09:06] a virgin. St. Thomas says that irregularity is contracted regarding the man's eligibility to be ordained if he marry one who is not a virgin because,

again, of the signification of the sacrament of holy orders, the church as the bride of Christ is a virgin betrothed to him only. In a word from

St. Thomas, "There is, however, a difference because on the part of the husband, it is required that he should not have married another wife, but not
