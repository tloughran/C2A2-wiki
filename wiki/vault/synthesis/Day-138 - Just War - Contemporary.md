# Day 138 — Contemporary Parallel: Who Acts for the Whole

## Frame

Aquinas turns from the rupture of unity (schism) to the use of force across and within communities: war (Q.40) and strife (Q.41). His just-war conditions are famous and exact — legitimate authority, just cause ("the avenging of wrongs," the restoring of what was unjustly seized), and right intention ("the securing of peace," not "aggrandizement or cruelty") — and his reading of "all who take the sword shall perish by the sword" is that it condemns *unauthorized* violence (Peter's, in Matthew 26), not war as such. Strife, by contrast, is "a private war," declared "not by public authority but by an inordinate will," and is always sinful, mortal in the unjust aggressor. Under mind / conscious-realist monism — a nested hierarchy of conscious agents — these two questions turn out to be one act at two scales, and the whole weight falls on a single variable: *which agent is acting, and for whose good?*

## Problem

The shared inquiry-problem is the legitimacy of coercion. Force applied to another agent is, on its face, the opposite of the loving coupling charity seeks; yet Aquinas (with Augustine) holds that some force is not only permitted but ordered to peace. What distinguishes the two? His answer is structural: *authority*. War can be just because it is waged by "the head of a nation or whoever has responsibility for the well-being of that nation" — the agent who acts for the *whole's* common good. Strife is sinful because it is waged by "private persons" by an "inordinate will" — a part acting for its *own* good. The same physical act (violence) is just or sinful depending on which agent in the nested hierarchy performs it and for whose good. The problem, then, is to say what makes an agent the legitimate bearer of the whole's good — and to face honestly whether the loving-unity picture can license force at all.

A subsidiary problem appears in a.3: the "small treatise on deception." Ambushes are lawful; concealment is not lying; "even in sacred doctrine many things have to be concealed." When is withholding information licit, and when is it betrayal?

## Resource

The leading resource is the multi-scale account of collective agency — used carefully, as analogy. Tom's PRS-form record of Friston's work (PRS-06 in the Friston-tradition wiki) describes how individual agents "form coherent higher-order wholes without losing autonomy" by mutual model-building, becoming "a coherent collective agent." That higher-order agent has a good of its own — the maintenance of its order — and acts to preserve it. Aquinas's "proper authority" is, structurally, exactly this: the agent that genuinely *is* the locus of the whole's good, as against a part. Tom's PRS-form record of Levin's work sharpens the contrast. The organism is a collective of cells coordinated for one anatomical goal (PRS-08, cells as cognitive agents in morphospace); cancer (PRS-02) is a part that *defects* from that collective. Strife is the aggressive form of the same defection: a part using force not for the whole but against its neighbors, "by an inordinate will." So the war/strife distinction maps onto the collective/defector distinction — legitimate force is the whole acting through its proper organ; strife is a part usurping that role. The immune response is the natural image: the organism's coordinated force against a threat to its order is not "violence" in the pathological sense; the rogue clone's proliferation is.

The deception sub-problem yields cleanly to the Markov-blanket picture. In the active-inference frame (Tom's PRS-form record of Friston, PRS-04), an agent has internal states screened from the environment by its blanket; not every state is disclosed, and this is constitutive of being an agent at all, not a deception. Aquinas's distinction — concealing the plan of campaign is licit; bearing false witness is not — is the ethical correlate: an agent may keep hidden states without *asserting falsehoods*. Not-disclosing-all and lying are different operations.

But the resource must be applied with a flag the project should not soften. The collective-agency analogy explains the *structure* of Aquinas's distinction; it does not, by itself, justify any actual war, because the hardest fact under mind-monism is that the *adversary is also an agent within the whole*. The organism/cancer analogy works only if the "enemy" is genuinely a defector from the shared good — and across human communities that judgment is exactly what is contested. Tom's PRS-form record of Rohr's order-disorder-reorder (PRS-02) and the Universal Christ (PRS-03), with Wright's reading of the cross as the end of redemptive violence (*Jesus and the Victory of God*, by canonical work), pull the other way: in the loving whole, the adversary is not to be read out but reordered. The science here is a structural analogy; the moral weight is theological; and the two do not simply agree.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The proposed parallel reads the war/strife distinction as the distinction between *the higher-order agent acting for the whole's good* and *a part usurping force for its own*. With Aquinas: authority, just cause, and right intention are the marks of force ordered to peace; strife is always disordered because it is force by an inordinate will; and concealment differs from lying. Where it reframes: "proper authority" becomes the property of the agent that genuinely bears the whole's good (the collective field, the coherent collective agent), and strife becomes individual-scale defection turned aggressive — re-described in the same vocabulary as schism (Day 137), one scale down. Where it refuses to go further than the evidence: the reframe explains the *form* of legitimate force but raises the *bar* on its justification, because mind-monism insists the enemy is also a perspective within the loving whole. So the contemporary parallel keeps Aquinas's third condition — *right intention, the securing of peace* — as the binding one, and reads the whole treatise as hedging force toward its own supersession: force is licit only as a tragic, bounded remedy ordered to the peace that would make it unnecessary.

Evidence strength, named honestly. The collective-agent structure is empirical at the biological scale (Levin, Friston) but strictly *analogical* at the scale of nations — it warrants no actual war and should never be read as doing so. The Markov-blanket account of licit concealment is a clean formal analogy but ethically partial. The just-war triad and the gravity of strife are theological and high in field. And the tension between just-war reasoning and the loving-unity telos is real and is flagged, not resolved.

## The central-theme thread

The central claim says every limited perspective, when its limits are removed, is found *within* the simple whole — and that includes the adversary's. This is the deepest pressure the theme puts on Q.40-41. Aquinas can license force because, for him, justice in a fallen order sometimes requires it; the project does not contradict him, but it locates the only honest justification in his own third condition — the securing of *peace* — and reads everything else as constrained by the fact that the enemy is a fellow-member of the whole, computing badly from its own resources, never simply outside the loving unity. Strife is the antithesis: the part seizing, for private vengeance, a force that only the whole's good could ever license — perspective-limitation not merely defended (envy) or seceded into (schism) but weaponized. The working-out of salvation "in fear and trembling" includes the sober recognition that we are agents who can wield force, and that the only force the loving whole can finally own is the kind ordered to drawing every perspective back into peace.

## Where this leaves us

War and strife resolved into one question — who acts for the whole — with the loving-unity telos pressing hard on every justification of force and binding it to the securing of peace. Aquinas continues the catalogue of disorders against peace and charity into the collective and the relational: Q.42-43, sedition and scandal. Day 139 takes up the multitude that secedes from the common good, and the subtler harm of the agent who, by word or example, draws a weaker perspective into its own fall.
