# Day 136 — Contemporary Parallel: When Another's Good Reads as My Loss

## Frame

Having mapped charity and named the two ways its climb fails — sealing off (hatred) and giving up (sloth) on Day 135 — Aquinas turns to two further disorders of the social field: envy (Q.36) and discord (Q.37). Envy is sorrow at another's good, "in so far as it conduces to the lessening of one's own good name or excellence"; discord is contrariety of wills, contrary to the peace charity seeks. The contemporary question, under mind / conscious-realist monism, is sharp on both. If reality is a network of conscious agents whose flourishing is, at the deepest level, *shared*, why does another agent's good register in me as an evil? And if two agents both genuinely intend the good, how is real conflict between them even possible — and when is it nobody's fault?

## Problem

The shared inquiry-problem has two faces, one for each question. First, *why is another's good a sorrow at all?* Aquinas's answer is exact: another's good becomes "my own evil" only "in so far as it diminishes my own good in my own eyes." Envy presupposes a comparison and a frame in which the other's gain is read as my loss. He adds the telling observation that we envy not those far above us but those near us, "whom we strive to rival or surpass" (a.2). So envy is *indexed* — to a reference-class, to proximity, to rivalry — not to goods in the absolute.

Second, *how can agents who both intend the good still conflict?* Here Aquinas hands the project one of its cleanest medieval texts. The "sharp contention" of Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15) that split them was, he says, "accidental, not direct, because each intended some good, yet the one thought one thing good while the other thought something else, which was owing to human deficiency." Discord can be sin — when one "knowingly and intentionally" departs from the divine and the neighbor's good — but it need not be. Sometimes it is simply what finite, well-meaning agents do.

## Resource

For envy, the leading move is the non-zero-sum account of love and the formal account of mismodeling. Tom's PRS-form record of Fredrickson's work names positivity resonance as a coupling event that "scales from dyadic interaction to group resonance" without depletion (PRS-03 in the Fredrickson-tradition wiki): the capacity for love, and the goods love secures, are not subtractive — my sharing in another's flourishing does not diminish my own. The C2A2 cross-index makes the same point at the level of ontology: love/union is treated as a *fundamental, non-rivalrous category* across Fredrickson, Stump, and Kastrup (CROSS-005), and positivity resonance is the measurable signature of agents minimizing *joint* free energy (CROSS-010). Against that backdrop, envy is structurally a *sign error*. In the active-inference frame named in Tom's PRS-form record of Friston's multi-agent work (PRS-06 in the Friston-tradition wiki), agents couple by "reducing prediction error in each other's behavior," and love is the mechanism by which they "synchronize models and become a coherent collective agent." Envy carries the opposite sign on that coupling term: the other's predicted good enters my model as my predicted loss. And Aquinas's "we envy the near, not the far" is exactly a reference-frame effect — perception tracks fitness payoffs relative to a comparison-class, not truth in the absolute (the interface reading, by theme, in Tom's PRS-form record of Hoffman's PRS-03). Envy is what perspective-limitation produces when the local frame is mistaken for the whole.

This also preserves Aquinas's beautiful distinction between envy and *zeal* (a.2): sorrow at another's *greater spiritual* good, the sorrow that spurs self-reform, is praiseworthy. Same affect, same comparison — but the object is reordered toward the real good, and so the sign flips back. Zeal is envy with the coupling term corrected.

For discord, the resource is the same multi-agent picture read the other way. Friston's PRS-06 (in Tom's PRS-form record) asks "how do individual agents form coherent higher-order wholes without losing autonomy?" — and the answer is *mutual model-building*, never identical models. Two agents minimizing free energy against overlapping-but-distinct generative models will, in general, reach different policies even when both are oriented to the good. That is the formal shape of Aquinas's "accidental discord": Paul and Barnabas are not defecting; they are two bounded models of one good, diverging by the "human deficiency" that is simply finitude. The project should mark this carefully — it is the most load-bearing line in the episode.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The proposed parallel reads envy as the wrong-sign coupling of a perspective that has made temporal goods its end, and discord (in its non-culpable form) as the expected divergence of finite agents both reaching for the good. With Aquinas: envy is genuinely disordered, mortal in its genus because it is the contrary movement to charity, which *rejoices* in the neighbor's good; discord can be mortal when willful, but is sinless when it is mere finitude diverging. Where it reframes: Aquinas locates envy's root in a disordered love of temporal goods, and the parallel keeps that diagnosis while adding the formal mechanism — the sign error, the reference-frame distortion — that explains *why* the disordered love produces sorrow rather than mere desire. And it elevates the "accidental discord" remark from an aside to a principle: non-culpable disagreement is not a failure of charity but the native condition charity must work within.

Evidence strength, named honestly. The non-zero-sum character of love is empirically anchored (Fredrickson PRS-03, medium) and the wrong-sign coupling is formally clean but interpretive (Friston PRS-06, medium). The reference-frame reading of "we envy the near" is philosophical (Hoffman, by theme). The theological weight — envy as mortal in genus, discord as a daughter of vainglory — is high in field and untouched by the reframe.

## The central-theme thread

Envy and discord are the two faces of perspective-limitation in the social field, and the central theme reads them in opposite directions. Envy is the limit *lying*: it tells the agent that the whole is rivalrous, that the neighbor's good is carved out of a fixed stock and so is my loss. The loving whole the project is exploring refutes that lie directly — when all limitations are removed, every limited perspective is *contained* in the whole, and there my good and the neighbor's are one good seen from two places; envy is what it feels like to model that one good as two competing goods. Discord-without-fault is the limit telling the *truth*: even two agents both straining toward the good will diverge, because each computes the good from inside its own resources. That is the exact condition the central claim describes — agents working out loving unity "in fear and trembling," "aware of our limitations and our need for guidance from those who perceive more of the whole." Paul and Barnabas are the patron case: not a failure of love, but love operating under finitude, which is the only way love ever operates this side of the whole.

## Where this leaves us

Envy is the perspective insisting the whole is rivalrous; discord is the perspective discovering it is partial. The first is a disorder to be healed by seeing the non-zero-sum whole; the second is a fact to be worked within by the charitable correction Day 134 named. Aquinas now hardens both: discord of will becomes contention of *speech*, and then the rupture of the community itself — schism. Day 137 takes up Q.38-39, where the divergence of finite perspectives stops being accidental and becomes a willed secession from the unity that holds the many as one.
