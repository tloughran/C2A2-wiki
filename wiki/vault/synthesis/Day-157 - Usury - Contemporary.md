# Day 157 — Contemporary Parallel: Usury, Productive Capital, and the Discrimination That Matters

## Frame

Q.78 takes up *usury*: is it sinful to take money as price for money lent (a.1), may one charge for additional services (a.2), is one bound to restore the gain (a.3), may one borrow under conditions of usury (a.4)? Q.79 turns to the *quasi-integral parts of justice*: doing good (a.1) and declining from evil (a.2) as the two-sided structure of every just act. Q.78 is the medieval Church's bright line on lending; Q.79 supplies the formal scaffolding for the moral act it forbids. This is the question where the medieval frame meets its most substantial modern qualification — and where the central claim does its most careful discriminative work.

## Problem

The inquiry-problem, in both frames: *some charges in financial transactions answer to real underlying values (risk, time, productive capacity), and some do not — so on what basis can we tell the difference, and what is the moral structure of charges that don't?* Aquinas's medieval frame answers from a particular monetary ontology: money is a *measure* of value, its use is its consumption (you can't lend a coin without surrendering its use; once used it's gone), so charging for the use of money is selling something that doesn't exist — selling "the same thing twice." The contemporary problem is to preserve Aquinas's test (does the charge answer to real underlying value?) while updating the underlying ontology (modern money participates in productive capital, risk-bearing, and time-value in ways medieval *moneta* did not), so that the moral discrimination remains sharp where it matters most: against extractive rent-seeking on the needy.

## Resource

Tom's PRS-form record of Carroll's work supplies the right metaphysical resource. PRS-01 (poetic naturalism: particles, organisms, and persons are all legitimate levels of description of one substrate, so higher-level descriptions stay valid) makes *productive capital* a real higher-level pattern: a sum of money deployed in an enterprise that produces real goods is not the same as a coin sitting in a strongbox. The lender who advances productive capital is contributing to a real productive process (over time, under risk) that produces real value; the interest answers to that contribution. PRS-28 (moral facts as real at the level of description appropriate to agents and their wellbeing, without non-natural properties at the fundamental layer) supplies the discipline: the distinction between productive and extractive charges can be specified without sentimentality, by asking whether the charge answers to a real higher-level pattern (productive capacity, risk borne, time forgone) or to nothing (the borrower's need, with no underlying contribution by the lender).

Tom's PRS-form record of Hoffman's work names the historical gap clearly. PRS-03 (interface theory) is the formal claim that an interface is real (we act through it) but partial (the underlying reality is richer than the interface displays). Medieval *moneta* was an interface to a relatively flat underlying reality: stored value, exchangeable. Modern money is an interface to a richer reality: stored value plus productive capacity plus risk-allocation plus time-value plus information about the polity's distributed credit-worthiness. Aquinas's argument was right *for his interface*; the contemporary argument is right *for the interface money has become*. This is not a soft revision; it is the recognition that the underlying reality the interface tracks has changed (because productive enterprise at scale now requires capital deployment at scale).

Tom's PRS-form record of Levin's work continues the collective-cognition reading. PRS-17 (bioelectric pattern memory as *cognitive glue* — the mechanism constituting a collective knower above the level of its individual components) makes the polity's distributed allocation of capital — who lends to whom for what — a real apparatus of inference: each lending decision is the apparatus testing a hypothesis about productive deployment under uncertainty; aggregate outcomes update the apparatus's priors. PRS-02 (defection) sharpens the contemporary form of usury: extractive rent-seeking is the *defection* of capital-holders from the apparatus's allocation function — exploitation of price-taker status (those whose need leaves them no alternative) to extract value that answers to nothing in the apparatus's actual work.

Tom's PRS-form record of Friston's work formalizes the intention difference. PRS-04 (the organism as an active modeler that generates predictions and tests them against outcomes, rather than a passive responder): the *productive* lender's target is the joint return from a successful enterprise — risk-bearing and time-bearing are real costs the lender accepts in service of a shared productive outcome. The *extractive* lender's target is the borrower's incapacity to refuse — the model is rent-seeking on asymmetric need (the Q.77 cheating logic extended in time). PRS-28 (precision as inverse-variance weighting over selected channels, with aberrant precision as the signature of malfunction): the lender's responsibility includes accurate weighting of the borrower's actual position; loans to those with no realistic capacity to repay are predatory because they up-weight the lender's gain at the cost of the borrower's destruction.

Tom's PRS-form record of Wright's work supplies the scriptural ground for the bright line that *does* persist contemporarily. PRS-02 (faithful improvisation) is the form: Torah's prohibition of interest within the covenant community (Ex 22; Lev 25; Deut 23) is not an arbitrary cultic rule but a positive teleology — the covenant community must not allow capital-holders to extract value from members in distress. The Jubilee tradition (Lev 25) makes this systemic: periodic restoration of land and release of debts because *the apparatus must not let capital accumulation strangle the polity's poorest*. The prophets (Amos, Micah, Isaiah) target exactly this defection — the wealthy extracting from the needy through the apparatus of debt — as the wrong that calls down judgment on the community. The contemporary form of usury Wright would name is the predatory loan, the payday-lending pattern, the asymmetric extraction from need; productive capital deployment under shared risk is not the form the prophets condemn.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The proposed contemporary parallel preserves Q.78's underlying test while updating its application. *Preserved*: the *test* — does the charge answer to a real underlying value, or to nothing? *Preserved sharply against the contemporary form*: extractive usury (rent-seeking on the needy whose alternatives are negligible) remains exactly the wrong Aquinas names; modern payday lending, debt-trap structures, and predatory consumer credit are the unmistakable contemporary expressions of the prohibition the prophets and Aquinas both reach. *Updated*: where the medieval bright line condemned all interest because money was treated as pure measure, the contemporary frame distinguishes — productive interest (answering to risk-bearing, time-value, and contribution to real productive capacity) is *not* the wrong Aquinas named, because the underlying reality the modern money-interface tracks includes those real higher-level patterns. *Extended*: Q.79's two-sided structure of justice (doing good / declining evil) maps onto the lender's positive and negative responsibilities — positively, deploy capital to support the polity's productive cooperation; negatively, refuse extractive structures.

Measure against Aquinas: substantive convergence on the *purpose* of the prohibition (preventing capital-holders from exploiting asymmetric need), with the *application* updated to track a different monetary ontology. Where the contemporary frame extends Aquinas: it specifies more carefully *which* contemporary lending practices fall under the prohibition (predatory, asymmetric, rent-seeking on need) and which do not (productive, shared-risk, time-value-respecting). The bright line he draws becomes a *discriminating principle* — harder to apply mechanically, but no less morally serious; arguably more so, given the scale at which contemporary credit operates.

## The central-theme thread

Usury is the economic case where the central claim does its most careful discriminative work. The asymmetry of perspective and resource that drives every economic exchange is here intensified by time and need: the borrower has limited options and an urgent claim; the lender has capital and the freedom to choose. The central claim — that loving unity is what limited perspectives compute toward across the apparatus they cannot see whole — gives the criterion. A productive lending is the apparatus working: real risk is shared, real time is paid for, the polity's coordination across time is advanced. An extractive lending is the local-optimum-globally-defective move: the lender absolutizing their privileged position against the borrower whose need leaves them no alternative — the cheating-of-the-poor pattern the prophets target as the apparatus's most consequential defection. Working out salvation in fear and trembling, in finance, looks like the discipline of asking what the charge actually answers to — and refusing the charges that answer to nothing but the borrower's incapacity.
