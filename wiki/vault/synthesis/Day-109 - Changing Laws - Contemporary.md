# Day 109 — Changing Laws (Contemporary)

## Frame

Q.96 examines what human law can do (its power, scope, and limits) and how it binds conscience. Q.97 examines how human laws change, and why change itself is costly to the binding force of law. Three of Aquinas's claims here are remarkably modern:

1. Human law cannot rightly forbid all vices, because the overreach would either be ignored or provoke revolt (Q.96 a.2).
2. The *spirit* of law trumps the *letter* when their conjunction defeats the lawgiver's intent (Q.96 a.6).
3. Change-itself-has-costs: the binding force of law is partly a function of its perceived stability (Q.97 a.2).

The contemporary question: *what do contemporary computational, behavioral, and institutional theories say about (a) how law binds in conscience, (b) how law legitimately changes, and (c) the cost of change itself?*

## Problem

The shared problem: human laws are revisable conventions that nonetheless bind. How? When does revision restore alignment to the deeper natural-law signal, and when does revision *itself* erode the law's normative force? The medieval frame puts this as a question about the nature of just law; the contemporary frame puts it as a question about *institutional epistemics* and *coordination dynamics*.

## Resource: convention-as-Schelling-point, computational irreducibility of local determination, costly stability

**Wolfram's computational irreducibility** (PRS-03 in Tom's re-description of the Wolfram tradition): for many systems, the only way to know what they do is to run them. This is the strong claim relevant here: from the general natural-law priors, you cannot algorithmically generate the right human-law arrangements for a particular society at a particular time. Local determination is unavoidable. Q.95 a.4's "law of nations vs. civil law" already recognizes this; Q.96 a.6's "spirit-of-law" recognition is the same point at a different level.

**Friston's active inference** (FLAG-05): generative-model agents settle into convergent priors through repeated experience. A convention becomes binding partly because *every agent's prior expects every other agent's compliance*. This is precisely the mechanism behind Q.97 a.2's "the mere change of a law is of itself prejudicial to the common good": changing the law disturbs every agent's prior; the disturbance has measurable costs in coordination, and the *binding force* of law is partly this priors-convergence. Modern behavioral economics has independently arrived at this (Schelling-point coordination; status-quo bias).

**McGilchrist's hemispheric asymmetry** (FLAG-09): the spirit-of-law / letter-of-law distinction maps directly onto right-hemispheric (contextual, embedded, narrative) vs left-hemispheric (decontextualized, rule-following) cognition. Q.96 a.6's besieged-city example is precisely the case where left-hemispheric rule-following ("gates always closed") would defeat the right-hemispheric purpose ("preserve the city"). Aquinas's allowance for acting "beside the letter" in service of the lawgiver's intent is the *integrated* judgment that requires both hemispheres functioning together. The contemporary failure mode — rigid algorithmic enforcement that produces absurd outcomes — is left-hemispheric dominance unchecked by right-hemispheric context-sensitivity.

**CROSS-08 (emergent norms in agent systems)** in the master cross-program index: stable coordination schemes emerge from repeated local interaction under shared priors, and the schemes that persist exhibit specific structural features: stability across time (status-quo bias as feature), tolerance of edge-cases (allowing for spirit-over-letter), and selective change-paths that compensate stability-loss with substantial improvement (Q.97 a.2's exact condition).

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

Human law on this reading is the *Schelling-point coordination scheme* a community has settled into, anchored on natural-law deep priors but irreducibly local in its specific form. Its binding force in conscience derives from three sources:

1. **Alignment with the deep priors** (natural law). A law that contradicts the deep priors is recognized as "not a law" by Q.95 a.2's standard.
2. **Coordination value**. The law is what every agent expects every other agent to follow; this expectation is itself socially productive. Disturbing it disturbs cooperation.
3. **Pedagogical / developmental value**. The law trains the agent's recognition of the deep priors through repeated application — Rohr's first-half-of-life rule-following as scaffold for second-half-of-life integration.

### What this shares with Aquinas

- Just human law binds conscience (Q.96 a.4).
- Law cannot rightly forbid all vices; overreach defeats the function (Q.96 a.2).
- Spirit-over-letter when their conjunction defeats lawgiver intent (Q.96 a.6).
- Change-itself-has-costs (Q.97 a.2).
- Custom can obtain force of law (Q.97 a.3) — the convention-emergence mechanism.

### Where this reframes

- **The mixed-form-best argument (anticipated in Q.105 a.1) is now empirically defensible.** Diffused power tracks the institutional-design finding that distributed authority is more robust under perspective-limitation than concentrated authority. Aquinas's Fall-grounded prudential argument is exactly the modern public-choice insight that *officials are themselves limited agents with priors*.
- **The conscience-binding claim is sharper.** It is not merely that law binds because it is rational; it binds because *agents who coordinate around the law produce more good than agents who defect.* The natural-law alignment condition is necessary (defection from genuinely unjust laws is praiseworthy); coordination value is what makes alignment-with-the-deep-priors actually bind in conscience.
- **Law-change is *legitimate* when (a) it restores alignment to deeper priors that had been imperfectly tracked or (b) a community's actual moral state has shifted such that the prior law no longer coordinates well.** Aquinas's two cases in Q.97 a.1 (progress toward perfection; degeneration requiring loosened law) both fit. The contemporary point: revision *requires evidence* that the alignment has actually changed; revisionism on aspirational grounds without alignment-evidence is exactly the change-without-compensating-good Aquinas warns against in Q.97 a.2.

### Where the evidence is firm vs. speculative

*Empirical*: Schelling-point coordination, status-quo bias, institutional persistence findings, the empirical effects of rapid legal change on compliance and trust. All well-documented in behavioral economics and political science.

*Empirical*: McGilchrist's hemispheric data; right-hemispheric grasp of context-sensitive judgment.

*Formal*: Computational irreducibility.

*Speculative*: The full identification of "binding in conscience" with the combined alignment / coordination / pedagogical conditions is a philosophical reconstruction.

*Theological*: The covenant-political reading (Wright on biblical theology of law) and the developmental-stages reading (Rohr) are theological.

## The Central-Theme Thread

The central-theme thread is most visible in Q.96 a.6 and Q.97 a.1: *perspective-limitation cuts both for the rule and against the rule*.

Each individual agent's perspective is too limited to see the lawgiver's purpose in every case (so the agent ordinarily follows the letter). But the *collective* perspective — the ruler or judicious community — can sometimes see when applying the letter would defeat the purpose, and judiciously deviate. This is exactly the structure the central theme predicts: limited perspectives are individuated by their limits, but limited perspectives in coordinated community can *together* see more than any one of them can alone. The loving-unity telos requires both kinds of seeing.

The computational task is real. *We cannot bootstrap* the perfect law from natural-law priors alone (Wolfram irreducibility). We cannot rapidly revise law without coordination-cost (Friston priors-disturbance). We work out our salvation in fear and trembling because we know our specific local determinations might be miscalibrated, our priors might be drifting, and our changes might cost more than they buy. Aquinas's warning at Q.97 a.4 against the imprudent dispenser — the ruler who grants exceptions without alignment-reason — is the same warning a Friston-shaped institutional epistemics would issue.
