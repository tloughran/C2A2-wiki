# Day 165 — Idolatry & Divination (Contemporary Parallel)

## Frame

Today's two questions are the substantive cases for yesterday's diagnosis. Q.94 develops idolatry: paying to a creature the *latria* due only to creator. Q.95 develops divination: foretelling the contingent future through illegitimate sources. Aquinas treats idolatry as the gravest of sins — analogous to crowning a usurper, since it perverts the ultimate end. He distinguishes the cult of saints' images (signification — they point to God) from idolatry strictly (creature as terminus), with Christ's image taking *latria* on account of his divinity. Q.95 partitions divination into three sweeping kinds: necromancy (open demon-invocation), augury (reading signs in external dispositions), sortilege (acting to provoke an occult sign). All three usurp God's prerogative of knowing the contingent voluntary future.

Under mind/conscious-realist monism, the contemporary version is twofold. First, *what makes saint-veneration coherent and idol-worship incoherent, given that both look the same from outside?* Second, *which sources of information about the contingent future are legitimate, given that the future of voluntary agents is in general not extractable in advance even in principle?* Aquinas reaches for the creator/creature distinction and for the divine prerogative over voluntary contingents. The contemporary parallel reaches for Hoffman's interface theory (icons that point vs. icons mistaken for termini), Wolfram's computational irreducibility (the future of generic systems cannot be predicted faster than running them), Friston's active inference (what counts as a legitimate prediction), and Wright's Pauline reading of idolatry as the diagnostic feature of perspective-limited reason.

## Problem (P)

The double problem here is **the icon-versus-terminus problem** and **the legitimate-prediction-source problem**.

The first arises because a healthy religious life cannot strip itself of images, gestures, relics, and saints — these are the means by which a temporally-extended community remembers what it knows and aligns the new generation's attention with what the tradition has perceived. But the *same* objects that function as signification can become *termini* — the worshipper stops at the image rather than passing through it. The diagnostic question Aquinas raises in Q.94 is: how is this difference real and not merely a matter of self-report? Two people can light identical candles before identical icons; one is rendering *latria* through the image to God, one is rendering *latria* to the image. The difference cannot be seen from outside; can it even be specified from inside?

The second arises because contemporary agents are awash in claimed sources of information about the contingent future — astrology columns, prediction markets, oracle-style AI outputs, divinatory practices repackaged as wellness, geopolitical forecasters, and the entire apparatus of contemporary attention-economy "prediction." Aquinas's question — which sources are legitimate? — is far from obsolete. The medieval frame ("demons can guess better than we can but intend our destruction") deserves a contemporary translation, not dismissal.

## Resource (R)

The C2A2 paradigm supplies a multi-tradition resource for both halves of the problem.

**For the icon-versus-terminus problem:**

**Hoffman** is the central frame (see PRS-01 and PRS-04 in the Hoffman-tradition wiki on interface theory). Spacetime and its contents are species-specific icons — useful interfaces, not the underlying reality. An icon used *as* an interface to reality is functioning correctly; an icon mistaken *for* reality is functioning idolatrously. The Aquinian distinction between veneration of saints' images as signification and worship of images as terminus is interface-theoretic in advance. Aquinas's special treatment of Christ's image — which receives *latria* because of the personal union of his humanity with the divinity — is the case where the icon is not separable from the terminus, because the terminus has taken the form of the icon.

**Levin** supplies a complementary biological reading (see PRS-04 in the Levin-tradition wiki on multi-scale agency). A community-of-memory like the church is a longer-body agent that exists across centuries. Saints function as morphic anchors in this longer body — concrete past instances whose example shapes the present community's bioelectric-cultural prepattern. Veneration of saints is the maintenance of the longer body's developmental memory; it is *not* the proliferation of additional gods because the saints are not posited as additional causal centers but as instances of the same divine life made culturally retrievable.

**Kastrup** supplies the metaphysical constraint. Under analytic idealism, the cosmos is populated by one mind under dissociation. There are no quasi-personal causal centers loose in spacetime to be worshipped. Saints are dissociated alters now restored in conscious union with the Whole; venerating them is recognizing what they have become *in* the One Mind, not setting up rival divinities. Idolatry, on this metaphysics, is impossible as a *real* ontology — there is no idol that has the causal autonomy the idolater attributes to it. What is real is the *idolater's distorted relation* to the One Mind; the idol itself is empty.

**For the legitimate-prediction-source problem:**

**Wolfram** is the central formal anchor (see PRS-02 in the Wolfram-tradition wiki on computational irreducibility). Most physically-realized systems are computationally irreducible: the only way to know what they will do is to run them. There is no algorithmic shortcut to predicting them in advance. Aquinas's claim that the contingent future of voluntary agents cannot be known with certainty by any creature — only by God who is the cause of all change — is, in contemporary terms, the claim that the agentic future is irreducibly costly to predict, and the only "predictor" that has it for free is the substrate in which the agents are unfolding. *Computational irreducibility is the secular form of "this knowledge is God's prerogative."*

**Friston** supplies the formal account of *legitimate* prediction. A generative model with well-calibrated priors over physical processes can predict weather, harvest, market dynamics with bounded confidence. The same model attempting to predict voluntary action of other agents *runs into* the irreducibility problem at the limit. Aquinas's distinction between (a) lawful prediction of physical futures by physical causes ("astrology" for weather and harvest is licit, he says) and (b) unlawful prediction of voluntary human action by stars is exactly the right contemporary distinction: physical regularities are tractable; the contingent voluntary future is, in general, not.

**Carroll** supplies the methodological cleanup (see PRS-03 in the Carroll-tradition wiki on poetic naturalism). Levels of description must be kept clean. A weather-forecast claim, a market-forecast claim, an oracle's claim, and a prophet's claim are *not* the same kind of claim and cannot be evaluated by the same epistemic standards. The medieval "demonic divination" category, translated, names a class of contemporary information channels whose accuracy is real but whose *intent* warps the user.

**Wright** supplies the scriptural anchor on both halves. Romans 1:18–25 is the Pauline diagnosis of idolatry as the diagnostic feature of fallen reason; Wright treats it as load-bearing in *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* and in his Romans work. On the prophet-versus-diviner distinction, Wright's treatment in *Jesus and the Victory of God* of Jesus as a prophet announcing God's contingent-future kingdom action is the case where God *does* reveal contingent futures, but does so through the unlimited-perspective Author, not through usurping creaturely prediction.

**Rohr** supplies the contemplative complement: apophatic prayer is the unlearning of every image, including legitimate ones, in order to encounter the One who exceeds all icons. This is not a critique of saint-veneration — it is the further reach the saint-tradition itself points toward.

## Solution (S, proposed, speculative)

The contemporary-parallel response synthesizes both halves.

*On idolatry:* an icon used *as* signifier — a saint's image, a relic, a sacrament — is functioning correctly when it remains a passage to the unlimited and incorrectly when the worshipper terminates at it. The difference is structurally specifiable: the legitimate icon participates in a *web of cross-reference* that points beyond itself (the saint points to Christ, Christ points to the Father, the Father is the unlimited inclusion); the idol terminates at itself and is treated as a causal center of last resort. Aquinas's identification of latria-of-Christ's-image as the limit case — where the icon is the terminus *because the terminus has taken the form of the icon* — survives the reframe exactly.

*On divination:* the contingent future of voluntary agents is, in general, *computationally irreducible* — there is no creaturely shortcut to it. Sources that claim shortcut access (whether ancient demons or contemporary "oracle" outputs) deliver bits of true information embedded in a framing whose effect on the recipient is destructive. The medieval frame and the contemporary one converge: *the question of which sources are legitimate is the question of which sources you can trust the framing of, not just the bits of*. Lots cast in epistemic humility — "Lord, who knows the hearts of all men, show which" — are licit precisely because the *frame* is surrender, not extraction.

**What it shares with Aquinas:** the structural diagnosis of idolatry as ultimate-end-perversion; the limit case of Christ's image; the asymmetry between physical-future prediction (licit, bounded) and voluntary-future prediction (illicit when attempted in creaturely independence); the licit case of prophecy when revelation is given.

**Where it reframes:** the contemporary parallel grounds *why* the voluntary future is opaque in computational irreducibility, not only in the divine prerogative. These are not opposed — under a monist ontology where the substrate is mind, they are two descriptions of one fact. But the additional grounding gives Aquinas's diagnosis empirical-formal traction in conversation with people for whom "divine prerogative" is not yet a load-bearing category.

**Where evidence is firm vs speculative:** computational irreducibility is formally strong. The active-inference forecasting frame is empirically accumulating. The interface-theory reading of icons is computationally suggestive. The Pauline-idolatry analysis is theologically primary within Wright's tradition. The integrative claim — *that pre-modern 'demonic divination' and contemporary attention-economy 'prediction' name structurally similar epistemic-source problems* — is suggestive and worth exploring rather than asserted as established.

## The central-theme thread

The central theme runs strongly here. The perspective-limited agent cannot, from inside her limits, perceive directly whether her ritual passes through its icon to the unlimited or terminates at the icon. She cannot, from inside her limits, distinguish a source that gives her true bits in a destructive frame from a source that gives her true bits in a frame that participates in loving unity. *Both* problems require the agent to rely on tradition — on the testimony of those who have perceived more of the whole — for the calibration she cannot perform alone. The rituals are checked against the larger body's witness; the information sources are checked against the larger body's discernment. Salvation worked out in fear and trembling is precisely this discipline: the limited agent's continual recognition that her own check on her religious and epistemic life is inadequate, and her continual willingness to be calibrated by those who see more. The Aquinian articles on the licit casting of lots — Matthias's election, Augustine's bishops choosing by lot in persecution — give the cleanest model of right relation to the contingent future: act, trust the unlimited witness, do not extract.
