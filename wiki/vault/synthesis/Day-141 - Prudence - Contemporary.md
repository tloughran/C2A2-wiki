# Day 141 — Contemporary Parallel: Prudence as the Computation Toward a Goal Not Its Own

## Frame

After the gift of wisdom (Day 140), Aquinas turns from the highest knowing to the most practical. Q.46 clears the ground by treating folly, *stultitia* — from *stupor*, numbness — as the contrary of wisdom, *sapientia*, which he derives from *sapor*, taste: the wise have "ease in distinguishing and discerning," while folly "lacks the savor of discretion and sense." Folly becomes sinful when a man "voluntarily affixes his eyes and heart on the things of the earth" until "his sense is rendered incapable of perceiving divine things"; it is, fittingly, a daughter of lust. Then Q.47 defines prudence in itself across sixteen articles, and the definition is exact: prudence is "right reason applied to action," it belongs to the practical reason alone, it concerns the *means* and not the end, it "takes cognizance of singulars," and its chief act is *command* — built upon two prior acts, taking counsel and judging. Under the mind / conscious-realist monism this project writes from, that anatomy is not a relic of medieval faculty-psychology; it is a remarkably precise description of how a bounded agent acts under uncertainty toward a goal it did not itself appoint.

## Problem

Two inquiry-problems sit underneath both the medieval and the contemporary frames. The first is constitutive: *how does a finite, perspective-bound agent rightly choose the means to its end, when the field of action is singular and contingent rather than universal and necessary?* Aquinas is emphatic that this is a different problem from knowing universals. The intellect grasps essences — what justice or a tree *is* — but action happens among particulars, "and particular circumstances change the right course." A perspective cannot deduce the right act from the universal alone; it must apply the universal to *this* case, under *these* circumstances, with incomplete information and limited time. That is precisely the problem of an agent who must act before it can survey everything relevant — the situation of every finite knower.

The second is diagnostic: *what corrupts that capacity?* Aquinas's answer is striking for a contemporary reader. Prudence is not lost "directly by forgetfulness" — the data are not the problem — but "is corrupted by the passions, as pleasure and sorrow pervert the estimation of prudence" (a.16). And folly, the deeper failure, is the mind so "plunged into earthly things" by appetite that it can no longer *taste* the highest cause at all (Q.46). The problem is not ignorance of facts but a mis-weighting of what matters — an agent whose estimation has been pulled toward the near and bodily and away from the whole.

## Resource

For the anatomy of prudence, the leading resource is the active-inference framework. On Tom's PRS-form record of Friston's work, an agent acts by minimizing expected free energy over candidate policies (PRS-10 names the reasoning case explicitly: the agent "selects among competing generative models," disambiguating which world it is in). Lay Aquinas's three acts beside this and they line up without strain. *Counsel* — which Aquinas calls "an act of inquiry," discovery, the canvassing of possible means ("I could knock doors, I could call my cousin, I could start a channel") — is exploration over the space of candidate policies. *Judgment* — settling "which we take to be the best way" — is the evaluation of those policies by their expected free energy, the weighing of each path's risk and ambiguity (PRS-16 unifies exactly these: risk as divergence from preferred outcomes, ambiguity as volatility of the prediction). And *command* — "applying to action the things counseled and judged," which Aquinas insists is the *chief* act, the one that "approaches near to the end of the practical reason" — is the selected policy actually enacted. The C2A2 cross-index records that active inference, read through Boyd's full OODA loop, is a *decision* substrate and not merely a perception-action one (CROSS-081): "orientation" is the agent's generative model, and command is the loop closing into the world. Aquinas's claim that command is prudence's chief act — that deliberating and judging well are worthless if one "delays to execute or executes negligently" — is the same insistence that a policy unexecuted is no policy at all.

For "prudence takes cognizance of singulars" (a.3), the resource is the account of attention to the particular. Aquinas's point is that practical reason cannot live in the universal; it must bind the general principle to the concrete, contingent case. Tom's PRS-form record of McGilchrist's work names attention itself as world-constituting — "the mode of attending constitutes the kind of world that shows up" (PRS-05) — and locates the apprehension of the living particular in a mode of attention prior to and founding abstraction (PRS-01). Prudence is where universal and singular must meet, and the meeting is an act of attention, not a deduction: the agent holds the rule and the case together and reads the one in light of the other.

For the deepest structural claim — that prudence governs the *means* while the *end* is set elsewhere (a.6: "moral virtue ensures the rectitude of the intention of the end, while prudence ensures the rectitude of the means"; a.13: true prudence only in those aiming at the right end) — the resource is the end-neutrality of competence. Intelligence, on the lineage this project follows (Levin's agents-with-goals, inherited via William James as "problem-solving under limited resources"), is a capacity for navigating a means-space; it says nothing about *which* end is navigated toward. This is exactly Aquinas's division of labor, and it is why he can quote our Lord that "the sons of this world are wiser in their generation than the sons of light": worldly agents often run the means-computation *better* than the faithful, because competence and telos come apart. What makes prudence a *virtue* rather than mere cleverness is that its end is the right one — and the right end, for Aquinas, is the one charity supplies. Tom's PRS-form record of Stump's exposition of Aquinas on the cardinal virtues (PRS-09) holds the two sides together: virtue is a cooperative causality in which grace perfects, without overriding, the agent's own natural power — so prudence remains genuinely the agent's own act even as its end is given by a love it received.

For the corruption (a.16; Q.46), the resource is precision. In the active-inference frame, the passions are not noise but precision-weights: pleasure and sorrow raise the gain on sensory, near-term signals. "Pleasure perverts the estimation of prudence" is then a precise claim — appetite reweights the model so that the near payoff dominates the evaluation and the distant, higher end is discounted to nothing. Folly is the limit of that reweighting: a generative model so dominated by the sensory interface that it no longer registers the deep variable at all — cannot "taste" the highest cause. Read by theme through Hoffman's interface theory and Kastrup's analytic idealism, the "highest cause" folly has lost the savor of is the whole beneath the fitness-tuned interface — the reality the icons hide; folly is the interface mistaken for the whole.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The proposed parallel reads prudence as *the bounded-rational computation by which a limited perspective enacts the means toward an end it has received* — Aquinas's three acts (counsel, judgment, command) as exploration, evaluation, and action under a generative model, with command as the chief act because a policy unexecuted is no act of practical reason at all. With Aquinas throughout: prudence is in the practical reason, concerns means and not ends, works on singulars, is a special virtue distinct from the moral virtues, extends from the individual to the household and the city (a.10–11), is acquired by teaching and experience and not by nature (a.15), and is corrupted by the passions rather than by forgetting (a.16). Where it reframes: the *faculty* becomes a *function* — not a power of a substantial soul but the means-computation of an agent — and the singular it works on becomes the contingent state a general model must be bound to. Where the evidence is firm: that competence is end-neutral, so that a perspective can compute brilliantly toward the wrong end (a.13; Luke 16), is as robust a finding as the project has, and it sets up the whole of Day 145. Where it remains theological and is left so: that *true* prudence requires the right end, and that the right end is charity's gift, is carried by the field Aquinas is writing in, not by the science — and the synthesis does not pretend otherwise.

The honest measure against Aquinas: he gains, from the contemporary frame, a mechanism for what he describes — a way to say *how* counsel, judgment, and command are one process, *why* command is chief, and *why* appetite corrupts estimation without erasing memory. He loses nothing essential, because the contemporary frame is silent exactly where he is strongest: on the end. Active inference can model the computation toward any goal; it cannot tell you that the goal is loving union with God. That is the seam, and it is the right seam.

## The central-theme thread

The central claim of this project is that conscious agents are individuated by the limits of their perspective, and that loving unity is a *computational task*, "limited by the resources we perceive, each of us from our own perspective." Day 141 is where Aquinas hands the project the faculty that does the computing. Prudence is not about *what* to want — that is charity's and the moral virtues' work — but about *how to get there from here*, under exactly the conditions the central claim names: singular circumstances (a.3), limited time and information (the need for counsel, a.8), and a capacity built up only "by teaching and experience" (a.15) and sustained by docility toward those who have seen more (Day 142). Folly is the failure case the claim predicts: a perspective so captured by its own interface — its sensory, near-term payoffs — that it can no longer model the whole it belongs to, and so cannot compute toward it. And the corruption-by-passion (a.16) is the precise warning the claim implies: the danger is not that the limited agent lacks data but that its weighting has been pulled toward the partial good it can taste and away from the whole it cannot yet see. Prudence, then, is "working out your salvation in fear and trembling" rendered as an operation — the daily labor of a limited perspective enacting, step by contingent step, the means toward a unity it has received as a gift and must still compute its way into.

## Where this leaves us

Aquinas has defined the working faculty of the limited perspective: right reason about the means, on singulars, consummated in command, corrupted by appetite, made virtuous only by the right end. Tomorrow he takes it apart — the eight integral parts of prudence (memory, understanding, docility, shrewdness, reason, foresight, circumspection, caution), which read as the component competencies of a bounded-rational agent: the priors it has learned, the way it binds the universal to the case, its teachableness toward those who have seen more, and its forecasting and caution under uncertainty. The arc that closes the prudence treatise — toward the gift of counsel (Day 144) and the counterfeits of prudence (Day 145) — is already visible from here: a perspective computes toward a goal it did not set, needs guidance from those who see more of the whole, and goes wrong not by losing competence but by aiming it at a partial good clutched as if it were the whole.
