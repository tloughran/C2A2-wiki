# Day 164 — Superstition (Contemporary Parallel)

## Frame

Aquinas's diagnosis is unusually crisp. Superstition is a vice against religion *by excess* — not in amount of worship, but in misdirection. Either the worshipper aims worship at the wrong object (worshipping a creature as if it were creator), or aims it at the right object by the wrong means (treating a ritual gesture as if it had causal power independent of God's will), or — in Q.93 — worships the true God in a manner that *exteriorly signifies a falsehood* (post-Christ circumcision is Aquinas's example). The three species — idolatry, divination, observances — will be developed across the next two questions; today's articles set out the diagnosis itself.

Under mind/conscious-realist monism, the contemporary version of the question is: *what makes a ritual gesture superstitious as opposed to faithful, given that all ritual gestures use the same resonance-modulation machinery we described yesterday?* Aquinas's answer is that superstition is a failure of *proportion* — worship out of proportion to the ontology of its target. The contemporary parallel reaches for Hoffman's interface theory (the icon is not the world; mistaking icon for world is precisely the superstition shape), Friston's account of miscalibrated priors, and Wolfram's account of how humans detect spurious patterns in computationally irreducible systems.

## Problem (P)

The problem is the **ritual-ontology coherence problem**. Yesterday's account established that ritual gesture — adjuration, hymn, vow — is a real modulation mechanism, both inward on the agent's own state and outward on the collective field. But the *same* mechanism can be operated on any ontology. The mechanism does not check whether the ontology behind it is sound. A child who knocks-on-wood after voicing a hopeful sentence is using the resonance-modulation machinery faithfully; her ontology is wrong. A devout person who circumambulates a relic with rigorously orthodox doctrine is using the machinery faithfully; her ontology is sound. The mechanism is invariant; what varies is whether the ritual's *implied causal model* corresponds to reality.

This is the same problem operant-conditioning research has explored since Skinner's pigeons in the 1940s — birds whose food was randomly timed developed elaborate ritual behaviors because the reinforcement happened *after* whatever they were doing. The behavior is structurally identical to the ritual; the ontology behind it is empty. The contemporary version of Aquinas's question is: *how do we tell faithful ritual from operantly-conditioned superstition when both feel the same from the inside?*

## Resource (R)

The C2A2 paradigm supplies four converging moves.

**Hoffman** supplies the central frame (see PRS-01 and PRS-03 in the Hoffman-tradition wiki on interface theory). Spacetime and its contents are species-specific icons — useful interfaces that organisms evolved to navigate fitness payoffs. The icons are *not* the underlying reality. The superstition shape is precisely the error of treating an icon as terminus: worshipping the carved statue as if the carved statue were the locus of divine causal power. Aquinas's careful Q.94 distinction (still to come) between *latria* due to Christ's image because of his divinity, and *veneration* of saints' images as signification of what is real, is interface-theoretic in advance.

**Friston** supplies the formal account. A superstitious belief is a generative-model prior that is *miscalibrated* to the world — it places probability mass on causal links that do not exist. Operant-conditioning superstition is what happens when prior-update mechanisms over-fit to coincidence. Religious superstition is the same dynamic at the larger scale of a community's shared generative model. The Aquinian critique is structurally a critique of miscalibrated communal priors.

**Wolfram** supplies the deeper warning (see PRS-04 in the Wolfram-tradition wiki on computational irreducibility). Many real causal systems are irreducibly complex — there is no shortcut to predicting their outcomes from compact rules. Humans, faced with such systems, will detect spurious patterns *whether or not they exist*; the cognitive machinery does not gracefully handle the absence of compressible structure. Much of what gets called superstition is the human prediction system over-extending into computationally-irreducible terrain.

**Kastrup** supplies the metaphysical reframe. Under analytic idealism, the cosmos is populated only by mind — there are no quasi-personal causal powers loose in spacetime to be worshipped or appeased. The ontology of the target is constrained: worship is appropriately aimed at the One Mind under its varying personal manifestations (Christ, the Trinity, the saints as dissociated alters now restored in conscious union with the Whole), and aimed inappropriately at anything posited as having causal power *in addition to* the universal mind.

**Wright** supplies the scriptural anchor. Romans 1:18–25 is Paul's primary diagnosis of idolatrous reasoning, and Wright treats it as load-bearing for Pauline anthropology: *they exchanged the truth about God for a lie and worshipped and served the creature rather than the creator*. The text is structurally what Aquinas restates: idolatry is the worship-mechanism aimed by a bad ontology. Wright's reading is that Paul sees this not as a one-off error but as the diagnostic feature of fallen reason — the perspective-limited agent, lacking the infused supernatural knowledge Adam forfeited, mis-routes the worship-instinct.

**Rohr** supplies the contemplative complement: the contemplative tradition has always critiqued "magical" religion — the use of devotional gestures as causal instruments to compel divine response. Mature contemplative practice routes around the magical-causal frame entirely.

## Solution (S, proposed, speculative)

The contemporary-parallel response: *superstition is bad ontology made ritually visible*. The resonance-modulation machinery (yesterday) is invariant; the question is whether the ritual's implied causal model corresponds to what is in fact the case. Aquinas's three species map onto three distinct ontological errors: idolatry mis-locates the *terminus* of worship (creature for creator); divination mis-locates the *source* of supernatural information (demons or coincidence for God); observances mis-locate the *causal* structure of the world (lucky number, magic gesture).

**What it shares with Aquinas:** the structural diagnosis — superstition is excess of *proportion* and *direction*, not of amount. Religious life cannot be too intense; it can only be intense in the wrong direction. Aquinas's Q.93 insight that even worship of the *true* God can be perniciously wrong if its ritual exteriorly signifies a falsehood is exactly the contemporary reframe — the ritual *says something* about the world, and saying a falsehood is a real sin against faith.

**Where it reframes:** Aquinas grounds the diagnosis in the natural-law claim that worship is due to God alone. The contemporary parallel grounds it additionally in the empirical-formal fact that the human cognitive system over-detects patterns and over-fits priors. The reframe is *additive*, not corrosive — it gives Aquinas's diagnosis a second evidence base in observable cognitive bias.

**Where evidence is firm vs speculative:** operant-conditioning superstition is overwhelmingly well-evidenced. The pattern-detection-in-irreducible-systems point is formal-empirical (Wolfram, signal-detection literature). The interface-theory frame is computationally formal but ontologically contested. The integrative claim — *that medieval superstition diagnosis and modern cognitive-bias research are tracking one phenomenon* — is integrative-speculative.

## The central-theme thread

The central theme cuts cleanly here. The perspective-limited agent cannot, from inside her limits, verify the ontology that her ritual implies. She must trust some source about *what is in fact the case* in order to know whether her worship-mechanism is correctly aimed. The "tradition" that the central theme invokes — the testimony of those who have perceived more of the whole than the present agent can — *is the agent's protection against superstition*. Working out salvation in fear and trembling requires the limited agent to keep her ritual *checkable* against sources beyond her own immediate confidence. This is why Aquinas's example in Q.93 is so sharp: a person continuing the old-law ceremonial gestures post-Christ is, in his ritual life, asserting a historical claim (the Messiah has not come) that the larger tradition has confirmed is false. The body's confession can lie *even when the heart is sincere*.
