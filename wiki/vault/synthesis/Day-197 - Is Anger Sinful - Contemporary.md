# Day 197 — Is Anger Sinful? (Contemporary Parallel)

## Frame

Aquinas treats anger head-on (Q.158) and then its opposed vice, cruelty (Q.159). His answers are strikingly unsentimental. Anger is "the desire for revenge," and it is not as such evil: "if a man breaks into your house and you use anger to help fend him off, then this anger will be praiseworthy. You ought to be angry in this case." It becomes sinful when it goes "beyond what is right" — punishing the undeserving, exceeding deserts, defying the order of law, or aiming at something other than "the maintaining of justice and the correction of defaults." Most pointed is the eighth article: the *absence* of anger can itself be a sin. "He who is not angry, whereas he has cause to be, sins, for unreasonable patience is the hotbed of many vices." Anger is a capital vice (a.6) by the desirability of its end and its impetuosity, with six daughters; cruelty (Q.159) is the hardness of heart "ready to increase punishment," distinct from savagery, which inflicts pain "merely the pleasure they derive from a man's torture." The contemporary version: *is anger a malfunction to be minimized, or a functional signal whose virtue is calibration — and what distinguishes disordered correction from harm pursued for its own sake?*

## Problem

The inquiry-problem that runs from Aquinas to the present is the **status of a negative affect that is sometimes obligatory**. A purely suppressive model of the passions — anger as noise to be quieted — cannot accommodate Aquinas's claim that *failing* to be angry at injustice is a vice, that anger is "the passion given to man to assist him in pursuing or punishing the wicked as justice demands." If anger were simply a defect, less of it would always be better; but the treatise says there is a *right amount*, fired at the *right target*, for the *right end*. So the problem is to identify what kind of thing anger is such that it has an optimum rather than a minimum, and such that both overshoot (the quick-tempered, the rancorous) and undershoot (the apathetic bystander) are genuine failures. A second, sharper problem comes with cruelty: Aquinas separates the cruel man (who still has justice "in mind, albeit in a disordered way") from the savage or brutal man (who harms for "the pleasure they derive"), calling the latter "bestial." The problem is what distinguishes a *miscalibrated corrective drive* from a *reward drawn from the harm itself* — two things that look similar in the deed and are utterly different underneath.

## Resource

The leading resource is the active-inference treatment of affect as a functional, information-bearing signal rather than a disturbance to be damped (Tom's PRS-form record of the Friston program at PRS-10, where action and the appraisal driving it are expected-free-energy minimization). On that account anger is a high-precision error signal: it flags a state of the world (an injury to something the agent values) that demands a corrective policy, and it mobilizes resources for that policy. A signal of this kind has, by construction, an *optimum gain*. Too little gain (the bystander who "feels nothing" at an assault) and real injuries go uncorrected — Aquinas's defect-of-anger vice, exactly. Too much gain, or gain triggered by the wrong appraisal (a trifle read as an outrage, an injury brooded into rancour) and the system thrashes, "precipitates the mind into all kinds of inordinate action" — his capital-vice impetuosity. The Fredrickson-tradition record reinforces the point from the affective-science side: emotions are not contaminants but couplings tuned to the agent's situation (PRS-02), so the question is always fit, never mere quantity. And the McGilchrist-tradition record of attention as value-disclosing encounter (PRS-09) supplies the appraisal layer Aquinas leans on — that whether something *is* an injury worth anger is itself a matter of how it is attended to.

For the cruelty/savagery distinction the resource is the clinical bridge the master index records as CROSS-057 (the Friston × McGilchrist × Kastrup metastability/boundary work). Cruelty, on this reading, is the corrective system still aimed at (disordered) justice — the appraisal "this deserves punishment" is firing, but the brake that caps proportionate response has failed: hardness of heart "ready to increase punishment." Savagery is categorically different: the harm itself has become *rewarding*, so the behavior is driven not by an injustice-appraisal at all but by the reward drawn from another's suffering. That is why Aquinas files it under the "bestial" — it is not a human corrective gone wrong but a different motivational circuit entirely, and his phrase "resulting... either from evil custom or from a corrupt nature" tracks the learned-versus-constitutional split a contemporary account would also draw.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The contemporary-parallel response: anger is a functional error signal flagging injury to a valued order and mobilizing repair, so its virtue is *calibration* — fired at real injustice, sized to the injury, aimed at correction — and both excess (overshoot, wrong target, self-serving) and defect (no response to genuine injustice) are failures. Cruelty is that corrective signal still tracking justice but with the proportionality brake removed; savagery is a different drive, in which the harm itself is the reward.

*What it shares with Aquinas.* It keeps his core claims wholesale: anger is not evil per se; it is sinful only in its disorder (wrong target, excess, wrong end); its lack can be vicious; it is "capital" in its capacity to spawn other vices; and cruelty is distinct from savagery by whether justice is still (however disordered) in view. The both-vices structure — overshoot and undershoot — is his, not an import.

*Where it reframes.* "The desire for revenge" becomes a precision-weighted corrective signal with an optimum gain; "unreasonable patience is the hotbed of many vices" becomes a failure of necessary signal gain; the six daughters become the downstream pathologies of an over-gained or mis-appraised system. The cruelty/savagery line is reframed as brake-failure-on-a-justice-signal versus a reward-from-harm circuit — sharpening why one is corrigible disorder and the other "bestial." Aquinas's appeal to whether the avenger acts "out of hatred for some person" versus "for some good" maps onto the appraisal target: is the signal tracking the injury to the order, or has it bound onto the person?

*Where evidence is firm vs. speculative.* That negative affect carries function and has a fit-optimum rather than a minimum is well-supported in affective science and control theory (medium-high). The cruelty-vs-savagery split as brake-failure versus reward-from-harm is clinically plausible and matches the CROSS-057 material, but the precise mapping is a reconstruction (medium). The mortal/venial gradation — whether a given anger is "contrary to charity" — is moral-theological and presented as the tradition's, not as a finding.

## The central-theme thread

If the telos is loving unity among limited perspectives, anger turns out to be one of the instruments by which a limited perspective *serves* the whole rather than betraying it: it is the alarm that fires when the bound order is injured, and the mobilization that moves to repair it. The bystander vice is so striking precisely here — apathy in the face of an assault is not serenity but a failure to be coupled to the whole at all. The disorder of anger is not the signal but its *capture by the self*: anger "which wishes evil upon a man out of hatred for that man," anger for one's own vanity, anger sized to wounded pride rather than to real injury. That is the perspective absolutizing itself again — making its own slight the measure of justice. Wright's register names the rightly-tuned version: biblical *mishpat*, justice as the setting-right of what is broken in the community, anger in the service of repair rather than retaliation. Rohr's names the discipline: hating the evil without hating the person, the non-dual refusal to collapse the injury and the injurer into one target — exactly Aquinas's "not out of hatred for some person but for some good." Calibrated anger is a limited agent's love for the order it cannot see whole, defending it at the point where it is torn.

## Bridges

- **Anger ↔ precision-weighted corrective signal with an optimum** — the Friston-tradition record, PRS-10. *Why:* a functional error signal has a right gain, so both overshoot and undershoot are failures — Aquinas's excess and his defect-of-anger. Evidence: **mechanistic, medium-high**.
- **Defect of anger ↔ insufficient signal gain** — the Friston- and Fredrickson-tradition records (PRS-02). *Why:* "unreasonable patience" is a failure to be coupled to real injury. Evidence: **affective-science, medium-high**.
- **Appraisal of injury ↔ attention as value-disclosure** — the McGilchrist-tradition record, PRS-09. *Why:* whether something merits anger depends on how it is attended to. Evidence: **conceptual, medium**.
- **Cruelty vs. savagery ↔ brake-failure-on-justice vs. reward-from-harm** — master CROSS-057. *Why:* one is a corrective drive uncapped, the other a different, reward-driven circuit — Aquinas's "bestial." Evidence: **clinical-affective, medium**.
- **Anger for the good not the person ↔ mishpat; hating evil not the person** — the Wright- and Rohr-tradition resources. *Why:* rightly-tuned anger serves repair of the bound order, not retaliation. Evidence: **theological**.

## Where this leaves us

This completes the anger arc opened from the temperance side yesterday, and confirms the prediction carried in: anger has an optimum, not a minimum, and the defect article (a.8) is the cleanest evidence for it. The treatise now turns away from the irascible appetite's heat toward the quieter parts of temperance — modesty and humility (Q.160-161) — where the regulated good is no longer vengeance but one's own standing, and where the override-versus-alignment theme reappears as the right-sizing of the self.
