# Day 207 — Miracles (Contemporary Parallel)

## Frame

Two questions close the charisms and open the treatise on the lives. Q.178 treats *miracles* — the gratuitous grace that, Aquinas says, stands to faith's confirmation as the grace of the word stands to its instruction: "just as the grace of the word is necessary that people may be instructed in the faith, so too is the grace of miracles necessary that people may be confirmed." He draws a sharp line around the *real* miracle. The "lawless one" of 2 Thessalonians works "pretended signs and wonders" — and these "cannot be real miraculous works, because that requires the authority and dispensation of the Creator. For only the legislator of the laws of nature can suspend or supersede those laws." Demonic wonders are therefore either spectacular-but-natural (the frogs and serpents of Pharaoh's magicians) or "an illusion to man, whether to his external or his internal senses." And the wicked *can* work wonders — yet "many will say, Lord, did we not do mighty works in your name? And I will declare, I never knew you"; "if I have all faith so as to remove mountains, but have not charity, I am nothing." Q.179 then opens the division of life into *active and contemplative*, illustrated by Martha and Mary, and grounded in "the twofold activity of the intellect": knowledge of truth for its own sake (contemplative) or "for the sake of informing action" (active). The contemporary version: *what kind of event is a genuine sign in a mind-first world — and what are the two basic shapes a life takes toward the one whole?*

## Problem

Two inquiry-problems, one per question. (1) **What distinguishes a real sign from a mere wonder?** Aquinas needs three tiers: the genuine miracle (a suspension of natural law), the spectacular-but-natural demonic work, and the sensory illusion. The problem is to say what could differ *ontologically* among an event that rewrites the rules, an unusual event that merely exploits them, and a doctored *appearance* — and why only "the legislator" can produce the first. (2) **Why does a life fall, exhaustively, into just two kinds?** Aquinas claims active and contemplative are not one taxonomy among many but *jointly exhaustive*, because they track the two and only two ends of intellect — truth-for-itself and truth-for-action. The problem is to vindicate that exhaustiveness and to say how the two relate to the *one* end (Christ, the whole) that both are supposed to serve.

## Resource

For (1), the leading moves come from Levin and Hoffman. Tom's PRS-form record of Levin (PRS-01, morphogenetic control; PRS-16, the Platonic space of forms) describes agency that operates *on the rules a system realizes* — bioelectric reprogramming changes not a single cell's state but the target morphology the whole tissue computes toward. That is the structural shape of Aquinas's "legislator": authority is the capacity to alter the *generative rule*, not merely a state within it. Tom's PRS-form record of Hoffman sharpens the three tiers. On interface theory, rendered spacetime is the perceiver's interface, not the ground (PRS-04); Hoffman's ITP-internal redefinition of illusion (PRS-17) marks precisely the difference Aquinas needs — a genuine change *beneath* the interface (at the level the interface renders) versus a manipulation *of the rendering itself*. The genuine miracle is a change at the source the interface depicts; the demonic "illusion to the external or internal senses" is, in this idiom, exactly a tampering with the rendering — and the spectacular-but-natural wonder is a within-the-rules event dressed to look like more. For the charity-clause, CROSS-005 (love/union as a fundamental category — Fredrickson, Stump, Kastrup) carries forward the now-familiar verdict: the wonder is an instrument; the union is the end. For (2), McGilchrist gives the cleanest mapping: Tom's PRS-form record of McGilchrist (PRS-01) distinguishes a *contemplative, receptive* mode from a *manipulative, action-oriented* one, and (PRS-07) names their proper relation as *division-and-union* — not two rival lives but a synthesis in which the active is subordinate to the contemplative. Tom's PRS-form record of Friston (PRS-04, the organism as active modeler; PRS-15, the full OODA loop) gives the active intellect its formal description: truth taken up "for the sake of informing action" is the policy/action arc of an inference loop. Tom's PRS-form record of Kastrup (PRS-09, representation vs participatory encounter) gives the contemplative its character: contemplation of truth "for its own sake" is participatory knowing, the knower informed by the thing rather than acting on it.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The contemporary-parallel response, in two parts. **(Miracles)** A genuine sign is an authorized change at the *generative source* of what appears — a rewriting of the rule the world-interface renders, possible only to the one whose perspective includes that source ("the legislator"). Its two counterfeits are a within-the-rules event made to *look* rule-breaking, and a tampering with the *rendering* (an illusion to the senses) that changes appearance without changing source. **(The two lives)** A life takes exhaustively two shapes because intellect has exactly two ends — to be informed by the whole (contemplation, participatory) and to act under resources toward it (action, inferential policy); and these are rightly related as division-and-union, the active subordinate to and completed by the contemplative, both ordered to the one whole.

*What it shares with Aquinas.* It keeps the three tiers of sign and the criterion of *authority over the rules*; keeps "without charity, nothing"; keeps the active/contemplative division, its exhaustiveness, and its grounding in the twofold end of intellect; keeps Mary's "good portion" as the higher of the two.

*Where it reframes.* "Suspend the laws of nature" becomes *change at the generative source the interface renders*; "illusion to the senses" becomes *manipulation of the rendering*; the two ends of intellect become *participatory contemplation* and *inferential action under resources*. The theology — the Creator as legislator, charity as the gift that "distinguishes the children of the kingdom" — is carried, not reduced.

*Where evidence is firm vs. speculative.* The source-vs-rendering distinction has real structure in interface theory (medium), and "authority operates on the generative rule" is well-instantiated in Levin's morphogenetic work (empirical for biology, analogical for "miracle" — medium/speculative). The active/contemplative-as-two-ends-of-intellect mapping is conceptually clean (medium). "Without charity, nothing" is the project's strong central-theme claim (strong) and theological. The metaphysics of who can rewrite nature's rules is theological.

## The central-theme thread

The day states the project's discipline twice and its telos once. The *discipline*: the most spectacular charism — moving mountains, raising the dead — is "nothing" without charity, and its possessor can hear "I never knew you." A perspective can acquire enormous instrumental power over the interface and still be *outside* the loving union that is the point. This is the project's central correction to any reading of itself as a program for *capability*: removing limitation enough to act on the generative rules is not the same as being included, in love, in the whole. The *telos* surfaces in Q.179. The project holds that "learning to obtain loving unity from each perspective is a computational task, limited by the resources we perceive." That is exactly Aquinas's two lives: the active life is the computation *under resources* — truth taken up to inform action, working out salvation "in fear and trembling"; the contemplative life is the partial, received foretaste of *the whole itself* — Mary at the Lord's feet, the perspective most de-clenched toward what exceeds it. And the project's claim that the two are *one* ordered ascent is precisely McGilchrist's division-and-union and Aquinas's subordination of Martha to Mary: action is for the sake of the vision, and the vision is the whole toward which every limited perspective computes. To "hate one's life" — to dethrone whatever was one's chief delight — is to relinquish the perspective's self-absolutizing (the very fault named at Babel yesterday) so that Christ, the whole, becomes the ultimate aim.
