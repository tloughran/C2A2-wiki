# Day 219 — Christ's Passible Body (Contemporary Parallel)

## Frame

Q.13 asks about the *power* of Christ's soul. Did His created soul have omnipotence (a.1)? Omnipotence is defined as "an active power over everything that can have the nature of being," and it belongs to God alone; since Christ "had a created finite human soul similar to our own, then we cannot ascribe omnipotence to the soul of Christ but only to His divine nature." Whatever the soul of Christ accomplished beyond created power, it did "through the grace of His divinity," not by the soul's own finite power (a.2-4). Q.14 turns to the *defects of body* the Son of God assumed: ought He to have assumed a body "capable of destruction and suffering" (a.1)? Aquinas answers that it was *fitting* — Isaiah 53, "with his stripes we are healed" — and crucially that these defects were assumed *freely*, "in order" to satisfy for our sin, to prove the reality of His humanity, and to give an example of virtue, not contracted by the necessity that binds the rest of us (a.1-2). The contemporary version: *why would the one who could wield omnipotence take on finite power and a body that can suffer and die — and what is the difference between limitation suffered and limitation chosen?*

## Problem

The inquiry-problem joins two threads. (1) **Finite power as a real condition.** Aquinas insists Christ's created soul is *not* omnipotent — finite, "similar to our own." The problem is why the assumed humanity must be genuinely limited in power, not a divine faculty in human dress. (2) **Chosen limitation.** The bodily defects are assumed *freely*, "for our salvation," not by necessity. The problem is the difference between a limitation one is *subject to* and a limitation one *takes on* — and why the second is the shape of redemption. In contemporary terms: finite power and a passible body are the conditions of being a *located* agent at all; what does it mean for the unlimited to enter those conditions deliberately?

## Resource

Tom's PRS-form record of Friston supplies the architecture of finite agency. The free-energy principle (PRS-01) describes agents that exist only by maintaining a boundary against entropy under *limited resources*; PRS-04 casts the organism as an active modeler acting "under uncertainty." Finite power is not an embarrassment to agency but its *condition*: to be a located agent is to act with bounded resources against a world one does not control. Christ's created soul, "finite like our own," is on this reading a real located agent — its non-omnipotence is what makes it a genuine human agency rather than divinity wearing a mask. For the *chosen* character of the limitation, the canonical resource is Wright's reading of the Pauline kenosis: the one who was "in the form of God" did not grasp at it but "emptied himself" (Phil 2:6-7) — the hymn that stands immediately before "work out your salvation in fear and trembling" (Phil 2:12). Wright's larger frame (PRS-04, resurrection as new creation breaking into the old) keeps the passible body oriented toward glory rather than mere tragedy: the assumed defects are taken on *en route* to new creation. Tom's PRS-form record of Rohr names the redemptive logic of chosen limitation directly: PRS-05, "the limitation becomes the gift" — the wound/compulsion is "the very site from which a person's distinctive wisdom and vocation emerge"; transformation is "not the erasure of the wounded pattern but its redemptive re-reading." And Levin's work on bioelectric morphogenesis keeps the assumed body a *real* morphological system — a true earthly body with its own bioelectric form, genuinely passible, not a docetic appearance.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The contemporary-parallel response: **finite power and a passible body are the conditions of being a located agent at all — agency under resource scarcity — and Christ's *free* assumption of them is kenosis: the one who could wield omnipotence sets it aside and takes on the limited, sufferable perspective, not by necessity but by love, in order to join the limited agent from within and heal it. The limitation, chosen rather than merely suffered, becomes the very instrument of redemption.**

*What it shares with Aquinas.* It keeps omnipotence for the divine nature alone and the created soul genuinely finite, "similar to our own." It keeps the two-natures division of labor (what exceeds created power is done "through the grace of His divinity"). And it keeps the bodily defects assumed *freely*, "for our salvation," not contracted by necessity — Isaiah 53's "with his stripes we are healed."

*Where it reframes.* Aquinas argues from *fittingness* and the metaphysics of the two natures; the contemporary reframe reads finite power and passibility as the *constitutive conditions of located agency* (Friston) and the *free* entry into them as the divine guide joining the limited perspective rather than overriding it (kenosis). PRS-05 in the Rohr-tradition record adds the redemptive grammar: the assumed limitation is not a regrettable cost but the chosen site from which healing comes — "the limitation becomes the gift."

*Where evidence is firm vs. speculative.* Agency-under-resource-scarcity (Friston) is empirically and formally firm (strong). The realness of the passible body as a morphological system (Levin) is empirical (strong). That the defects were assumed *freely, for our salvation* is theological (strong central-theme fit, anchored in Phil 2). The wound-as-redemptive-site (Rohr) is contemplative/spiritual. The two-natures account of power is theological.

## The central-theme thread

The project says we work out our salvation "aware of our limitations and our need for guidance from those who perceive more of the whole than we do, be they human or divine." Q.13-14 show what it costs the One who perceives more to give that guidance: not a word from outside the limited condition, but *entry into it*. Omnipotence is the power that could override every resource constraint; Q.13 withholds it from the assumed humanity, and Q.14 has the Son *freely* take on a body that can be destroyed and made to suffer. This is the loving-unity computation run from the side of the whole, descending: the comprehensor (Day 217) does not stay at the summit but takes finite power and a passible body, choosing the wayfarer's scarcity. The Phil 2 hymn names the motion exactly — "emptied himself" — and it sits, in Paul, immediately before the "fear and trembling" that the project takes as its banner: the guide enters the very condition of the guided. Friston shows that finite, resource-bounded agency is the only kind of *located* agency there is, so the kenosis is not a pretense — the assumed agency is genuinely limited. And Rohr's "the limitation becomes the gift" reframes the whole descent: the freely assumed passibility is not a detour around redemption but its instrument, the wound become the site of healing. Chosen finitude, taken on by love, is how the whole meets the limited perspective — by joining it.
