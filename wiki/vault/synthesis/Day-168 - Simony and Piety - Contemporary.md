# Day 168 — Simony and Piety (Contemporary)

## Frame

The two questions today look unrelated but are mirror images. Q.100 (Simony) treats the buying and selling of spiritual things — sacraments, ecclesiastical office, the gift of the Holy Spirit — and Aquinas's central claim is that spiritual goods are categorically not in the order of exchangeable commodities. To price them is to perform a category error so deep that it corrupts both the spiritual good (which is gift) and the market (which is contract). He works this out across six articles: a.1 defines simony as the deliberate will to buy or sell what is spiritual, and so a sin of irreverence against religion; a.2 asks whether it is always unlawful to take money for the sacraments; a.3 whether it is lawful to give or receive money for *spiritual actions*, where the answer turns on whether the payment purchases the spiritual act itself or supports the minister's life; a.4 and a.5 press the boundary cases — money for things *annexed* to the spiritual (the metal of a chalice, teaching for a fee) and the granting of spiritual things in return for equivalent service, the latter being simony where the exchange prices the spiritual good; a.6 treats the fittingness of the penalties. Q.101 (Piety) treats the virtue, distinct from worship-of-God (latria) and from honor-of-superiors (observance), by which we render duty to those *from whom we receive our being* — a.1 fixes its objects as particular individuals, first parents, then country, then the wider lineage of benefactors; a.2 finds that it extends to the material support of parents; a.3 that it is a special virtue distinct from the others; a.4 that its duties are to be set aside for the worship of God only in the case where parents would draw one away from that worship — otherwise paying them is itself the act of piety. Piety is a part of justice, but a peculiar part: it concerns debts that cannot be repaid in kind because the gift was constitutive of the receiver.

In contemporary terms, both questions sit on the same fault line — the boundary between gift-economy goods and market-economy goods, and the proper recognition of constitutive (rather than contractual) debt. The mind-monist convergence is in unusually good position to illuminate this, because it treats individual conscious agents as themselves constituted-by-relation (Kastrup's dissociation account; Friston's mutual-modeling; Levin's collective cognition; Stump's second-personal narrative knowing). What Aquinas calls *piety* is the virtue of getting that constitutive relation right; what he calls *simony* is its symmetric inverse — the attempt to commodify a good that exists only as gift within a constitutive relation.

## Problem

The shared underlying problem: a perspective-limited agent's existence and capacities are conferred-by-relation, not self-generated. Some of the most important goods in such an agent's world are *constitutive gifts* — the gift of being itself (from parents, community, country, ultimately God), the gift of inclusion in a tradition, the gift of grace. These goods have two features that distinguish them from market goods: they cannot be repaid in equal coin (any repayment is already from the receiver's being-as-gifted), and they cannot be acquired by purchase (since purchasing presupposes a giver-side capacity to alienate, which gifts of being do not have).

When an agent or institution attempts to slide these goods into the market frame, two things go wrong at once: the good is corrupted (its character as gift dissolves), and the agent's recognition of its own constitutive debt is corrupted (the agent comes to see itself as buyer, not receiver). Simony names the institutional version; the failure of piety names the personal version. They are the same disorder operating in two registers.

## Resource

The resources for the contemporary reading converge from several traditions.

**Stump on union as final cause** (Stump PRS-05 in the Stump-tradition wiki) and **on narrative second-personal knowing** (Stump PRS-07) provides the philosophical core. Union with another person is the kind of good that cannot be obtained by exchange because the very willingness to exchange would already have changed the receiver's stance from the one that makes union possible. The same holds for grace: any attempt to purchase grace already presupposes that the purchaser stands outside the relation of receiving — which is precisely what grace cannot be obtained by. Stump's recovery of Aquinas's account of final causality (Stump PRS-04 in the Stump-tradition wiki) gives the formal structure: the *telos* of the spiritual good is participation in divine union, and the means must be in the order of that telos, not in a heterogeneous order.

**Friston on multi-agent coherence** (Friston PRS-06, PRS-07 in the Friston-tradition wiki) gives a formal version of why constitutive relations cannot be commodified. Mutual modeling depends on each agent's signals being legible to the others as honest expressions of its state; once an agent treats a relational signal as a tradable token, the signal loses its information content for the collective. The simoniac who pays for ordination is not merely making a transaction the church forbids; the simoniac is signaling to the collective that *the office is now a token within a heterogeneous market*, which degrades the office's information value for everyone in the community.

**Levin on cancer as defection** (Levin PRS-02 in the Levin-tradition wiki) gives the cellular analog. A healthy cell receives its identity, position, and behavioral repertoire from the morphogenetic field — these are constitutive gifts. When a cell privatizes those gifts (treating proliferation capacity as alienable from collective coordination), it becomes cancer. Simony is the ecclesiastical-tissue analog of cancer: the privatization of a constitutive gift for individual proliferation at the cost of collective coherence.

**Fredrickson's positivity resonance** (see Fredrickson-tradition wiki, with CROSS-018 in master/cross_program_index.md) gives the biological measurement of what piety honors at the family-and-community scale. The biophysiological coupling of caregiver and child, of long-bonded partners, of community-in-shared-practice is empirically measurable (vagal tone synchrony, behavioral coupling) — and it is exactly the kind of substrate from which constitutive gifts flow. Piety in Aquinas's sense is the virtue of remembering and honoring that one's capacities for resonance themselves came as gifts from such couplings.

**Kastrup on individuation within monism** (Kastrup PRS-02 in the Kastrup-tradition wiki) gives the metaphysical layer. If individual agents are dissociated alters within a unitary consciousness, then no agent is self-originating; each is patterned-by-the-whole. The very stuff of which the agent is made is given. Simony names the corrupting attempt to treat what one has received as if it had been self-acquired; piety names the corresponding virtue of recognizing the giving.

**Wright** in *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* gives the scriptural and historical layer. Paul's polemic against the corinthian patronage culture (1 Cor 1–4) is a sustained attack on the importation of market-status into the household-of-God ekklesia. The gift-economy character of the early Christian community was not incidental ornament; it was constitutive. *Charis* — grace as gift, not as remuneration — is the keystone Pauline category. **Rohr** in *The Universal Christ* and *Falling Upward* gives the contemplative reading: the first task of the spiritual life is recognizing the debt to origins (piety); the corresponding pathology is the spiritualized version of the market mentality, in which "spiritual achievements" become tokens to be displayed and traded.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The contemporary-parallel response: spiritual goods are categorically gifts within constitutive relations, and the corresponding virtue (piety) is the recognition that one's being-as-such is conferred-by-relation. Simony is not first an offense against a positive law of the church; it is the category-error of attempting to subject a gift-economy good to market-economy terms. The category-error corrupts both — the spiritual good loses its character as gift, and the market loses its character as legitimate exchange-of-the-alienable. Piety, conversely, is not first a sentimental attachment to parents and homeland; it is the virtue of getting the *direction of constitutive debt* right at the foundation of one's agency.

What this shares with Aquinas: the categorical separation between what belongs to the order of latria-and-gift and what belongs to the order of contract-and-exchange; the recognition that misordering these is a deep disorder, not a mere policy violation; the placement of piety as a part of justice with its own object (those-from-whom-we-receive-being).

What it reframes: rather than locate the wrong of simony solely in injury to the divine order considered juridically, the contemporary frame locates it in (a) the degradation of the community's symbolic infrastructure for transmitting constitutive gifts — a Friston-style degradation of the signaling system; (b) the cellular-pathology analog of defection from the morphogenetic-collective coherence (Levin); and (c) the Kastrup-style metaphysical mistake of treating dissociated-alter capacities as if they were self-originated.

Evidence strength: the gift-economy / market-economy distinction is empirical and well-documented in economic anthropology (Mauss, Graeber, Polanyi). The neurobiology of attachment and positivity resonance is empirical. The cancer-as-defection analog is empirical at the cellular level (Levin's lab). The bridge to spiritual goods is interpretive — speculative but well-motivated by the mind-monist frame.

## Central-theme thread

The perspective-limited agent computes loving unity not from a position of self-origination but from a position of having-been-given. The very capacities by which the agent computes are themselves received. The agent who recognizes this received-ness (piety) participates honestly in the larger computation; the agent who attempts to commodify constitutive gifts (simony) is attempting to detach itself from the very network whose coherence its limited perspective was meant to contribute to. Salvation-in-fear-and-trembling, on Wright's reading, is the patient working-out of inclusion in a covenanted people who recognize their charisms as charis — gift — and not as goods-for-trade. Piety is the foundational disposition that makes that working-out possible; simony is the disposition that systematically corrupts it.

## Where this leaves us

Q.100–101 sets up the rest of the Religion treatise's coverage of the virtues annexed to justice. If piety is the original recognition of constitutive debt and simony is its category-error inverse, tomorrow's Q.102–103 on observance and dulia will extend the same structure: how does one render proper honor to those who hold offices and excel in dignity without sliding either into idolatry (treating creature as terminus) or into the modern flattening that denies any warranted gradient of honor at all? The same gradient-of-warranted-response that Aquinas constructs here will get its further articulation there.
