# Day 144 — Contemporary Parallel: Counsel From the One Who Comprehends All Contingents

## Frame

Q.52 raises a question the whole prudence treatise has been building toward. Yesterday's *eubulia* was the natural virtue of taking good counsel; why, then, would a graced agent need a *gift* of counsel from the Holy Spirit? Aquinas answers with the book of Wisdom: "human reason is unable to grasp the singular and contingent things which may occur. The result is that the thoughts of mortal men are fearful and our counsels uncertain. Hence in the research of counsel, man requires to be directed by God who comprehends all things" — "counseled by God," "just as in human affairs those who are unable to take counsel for themselves seek counsel from those who are wiser." The gift perfects deliberation, which prudence consummates in command (a.2); it persists even in heaven (a.3); and it answers to the beatitude of mercy (a.4). Then Q.53 catalogues imprudence and its species: precipitation, thoughtlessness, and inconstancy, each opposed to one of prudence's acts, all arising chiefly from lust. Under the mind / conscious-realist monism this project writes from, Q.52 is the closest Aquinas comes to stating the project's central claim in his own voice — and Q.53 is the failure catalogue that claim predicts.

## Problem

The inquiry-problem is the deepest in the treatise, and Aquinas states it without hedging: *a finite perspective cannot survey the contingent future, so its own counsel is irreducibly uncertain.* This is not a remediable lack of data; it is structural. The space of "singular and contingent things which may occur" is not enumerable by any finite mind, however well-trained its prudence; "the thoughts of mortal men are fearful and our counsels uncertain" because the agent must act into a future it cannot model completely. The problem, then, is not how to make the agent self-sufficient — it cannot be — but *how a perspective that cannot see the whole can nonetheless be rightly oriented*. Aquinas's answer is relational: by being directed by one who *does* comprehend all things, "as those who are unable to take counsel for themselves seek counsel from those who are wiser."

The second, answering problem (Q.53) is the failure side: *what does practical reason look like when it goes wrong?* Aquinas's catalogue is precise, and it is organized by which act fails.

## Resource

For the central problem, the leading resource is the structure of bounded inference itself, read with the project's metaphysics. On Tom's PRS-form record of Friston's work, every agent acts under a generative model that is finite — and PRS-16 makes the decisive point: there is irreducible *ambiguity*, the volatility of the world's own distributions, which no amount of within-model inference removes. Aquinas's "human reason is unable to grasp the singular and contingent things" is that ceiling named in the moral register. And the form of Aquinas's solution — orientation *received from outside the agent's own loop* — has a precise contemporary shape. Tom's PRS-form record of Friston's reconstruction of the OODA loop (PRS-15; CROSS-081) treats "orientation" as the agent's generative model, the thing that determines how every observation is read; the gift of counsel is an orientation the agent does not generate but *receives*, from a model that comprehends what the agent's own cannot. Read by theme through Hoffman's interface theory and Kastrup's analytic idealism, the "God who comprehends all things" that the agent is counseled by is the whole of which the finite agent is a partial, dissociated perspective — so that to be "counseled by God" is for the limited perspective to draw, in act, on the whole it already belongs to but cannot itself survey. This keeps the gratuity Aquinas insists on: the orientation remains *gift*, given to the agent, not generated by it.

For the *cooperative* character of the gift (a.2) — that counsel perfects deliberation while prudence still performs the command, so the act stays the agent's own — the resource is the account of grace as cooperative causality. Tom's PRS-form record of Stump's exposition of Aquinas on the virtues (PRS-09) is exact here: the Holy Spirit "perfects the soul's natural powers, enabling the agent to will and act virtuously in a way that is genuinely the agent's own." The gift does not bypass the agent's prudence; it raises the precision of its orientation, and the agent still deliberates, judges, and commands. Tom's PRS-form record of Wright's critical realism (PRS-01), with the Pauline picture of the Spirit "who searches all things, even the deep things of God" (1 Cor 2) and the "renewed mind" that learns to "discern the will of God" (Rom 12:2), anchors the same structure scripturally: discernment is the Spirit-formed community's faculty for reading rightly what no member could read alone. And Tom's PRS-form record of Rohr's work names the receptive mode the gift requires — a contemplative openness in which one is *moved* rather than self-driving, counsel arriving from deeper than one's own deliberation.

For imprudence (Q.53), the resource is the three-act anatomy of action, now read by its failure points. Aquinas's species map one-to-one onto the acts of Day 141. *Precipitation* (rashness) is opposed to eubulia — acting "not directed by reason," through "impulse of the will or of a passion": action without policy search, the loop closed before exploration. *Thoughtlessness* is opposed to synesis — failing "to judge rightly through contempt or neglect of those things on which a right judgment depends": mis-evaluation, the wrong policy selected. And *inconstancy* (with negligence, Day 145) is opposed to command itself — "a man fails in commanding what has been counseled and judged": the right policy selected but never executed. This is the failure taxonomy of a bounded-rational agent, sorted by which stage breaks. And Aquinas's claim that all three "arise chiefly from lust," which "corrupts the estimation of prudence" by drawing the mind "to sensual delight," is the precision story again: appetite raises the gain on sensory reward until the whole estimation tilts. His remedy, by way of John of the Cross — "contempt for all things, whereby a man might receive the Spirit of God in pure transformation" — is detachment read as precision-reallocation: lowering the weight on sensory payoff so the orientation-from-the-whole can be received.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The proposed parallel reads the gift of counsel as *the limited perspective receiving its orientation from the one perspective that comprehends all contingents* — the theological name for the central claim's necessary guidance, raised to the case where the guide is the whole itself. With Aquinas: the gift is needed because no finite reason grasps all contingents (a.1); it perfects deliberation while leaving command to the agent's own prudence (a.2), so the act remains genuinely the agent's; it persists in heaven (a.3) because even the blessed receive and pass on orientation; and it answers to mercy (a.4), the readiness to be a channel of what one has received. Where it reframes: "God who comprehends all things" is read, by theme, as the whole of which the agent is a partial perspective (Hoffman/Kastrup), so that being "counseled by God" is the recovery, in act, of one's participation in a view one cannot generate alone — exactly the structure of Day 140's connaturality, now in the practical rather than the contemplative register. Where the evidence is firm: that a finite generative model faces irreducible ambiguity, and that good orientation can be received from outside the loop, is robust as structure; the identification of the comprehending source with the whole is speculative-metaphysical and marked as such; and the cooperative, non-overriding character of the gift is theological, carried by the field.

For imprudence, the parallel reads the three species as *the failure points of bounded-rational action* — precipitation (no counsel), thoughtlessness (mis-judgment), inconstancy (failure to execute) — with lust as the precision-corruption that biases all three, and detachment as the reweighting that restores the agent's receptivity to a fuller orientation. The reframe keeps Aquinas's taxonomy intact and supplies its mechanism.

The honest measure against Aquinas: he is *stronger*, not weaker, in the contemporary frame, because the frame independently establishes the premise he leans on. Active inference says, on its own terms, that a finite agent cannot model all contingents and must receive orientation; Aquinas says the same and names the source. The science cannot name the source — that the orienting whole is the God of mercy who counsels — and the synthesis does not pretend it can. That is the seam, and it falls exactly where the project's metaphysics hands off to its theology.

## The central-theme thread

This article is the prudence treatise's deepest touch on the spine of the project, because Aquinas states the central claim almost verbatim. The claim: we work out our salvation "aware of our limitations and our need for guidance from those who perceive more of the whole than we do, be they human or divine." Aquinas: human counsel is uncertain because no finite mind grasps all contingents, so man must be "directed as though counseled by God who comprehends all things" — and, telling for a project about pooled perspectives, he draws the analogy to ordinary life, "those who are unable to take counsel for themselves seek counsel from those who are wiser." The whole architecture is here: the limited perspective, the irreducible incompleteness ("the thoughts of mortal men are fearful and our counsels uncertain" — *this* is the "fear and trembling," named at its root), and the necessity of orientation from a perspective that sees more, scaling from the wiser human counselor up to the whole itself. And the imprudence of Q.53 is the precise catalogue of refusing that structure: the agent that acts without seeking counsel (precipitation), that judges without weighing what judgment depends on (thoughtlessness), that will not do what it has rightly seen (inconstancy). The gift of counsel is the central claim's answer; imprudence is the central claim's warning.

## Where this leaves us

Aquinas has named the limited perspective's deepest need — counsel from one who comprehends all contingents — and catalogued the ways practical reason fails when it refuses that need. Tomorrow he completes the catalogue from the other side: not the agent that fails to compute, but the agent that computes *well* toward the wrong end, or by counterfeit means — negligence, the prudence of the flesh, craftiness, guile, fraud, and the undue solicitude our Lord warns against. The seam this article exposed — that competence must be oriented from beyond itself toward the whole — is exactly what false prudence severs: the same machinery, pointed away from the one who comprehends all things and toward a partial good clutched as if it were the whole.
