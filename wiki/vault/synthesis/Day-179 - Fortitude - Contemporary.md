# Day 179 — Fortitude (Contemporary)

## Frame

Aquinas reaches the third cardinal virtue. Q.122 closes the treatise on justice by gathering its precepts — the Decalogue read as the commands of justice toward God and neighbor, the rendering-of-what-is-due codified. Q.123 opens fortitude (*fortitudo*): firmness of mind in the presence of difficulty and danger, supremely in the face of the fear of death. Aquinas makes a deliberately counterintuitive claim about its principal act. Fortitude's chief act is not to *attack* (*aggredi*) but to *endure* (*sustinere*) — to stand firm against a present and stronger evil over time. Endurance is harder than aggression, he argues, precisely because it forgoes the relief of action: the brave person holds position under a danger they cannot dispel, sustaining the fear rather than discharging it. Courage is thus less a surge than a steadiness.

Under mind / conscious-realist monism the contemporary version of the question is sharp and tractable: what is it for an agent to *hold a long-horizon good against an immediate threat-signal*? Fear is, computationally, a powerful and present gradient pulling the agent toward flight. Fortitude is the capacity to keep acting toward a more distant attractor — the good worth the danger — while that gradient pulls. The precepts of justice (Q.122) set what those distant goods *are*; fortitude (Q.123) is the capacity to stay with them when staying costs.

## Problem

Every embodied agent is built to flee threat. The threat-response is fast, somatic, and largely involuntary: it narrows attention, mobilizes the body, and biases action toward immediate self-preservation. This machinery is adaptive — agents that do not register danger do not last — but it is short-horizon by design. The recurring problem is that the goods most worth pursuing are often *long-horizon and other-regarding*: the friend defended, the truth maintained, the duty kept, the people protected. These goods require the agent to act against the strongest immediate signal in its own body. The problem of fortitude is therefore not the absence of fear — Aquinas is explicit that the brave still fear — but the maintenance of agency *through* fear toward a good the fear cannot see. And there is a failure on each side: the agent who cannot hold (cowardice, treated tomorrow) and the agent who feels no fear at all (a recklessness Aquinas counts a defect, not a virtue, because it misreads the real danger).

## Resource

**Fredrickson** supplies the empirically firmest resource, and it bears directly on Aquinas's "endurance" claim. In Tom's PRS-form record of her work, the broaden-and-build theory holds that negative emotions — fear above all — *narrow* the action repertoire (the fight-or-flight tunnel), while positive emotions *broaden* it, widening attention and the range of available responses and, over time, building durable physical, intellectual, and social resources. Crucially, her lab documented an *undo effect*: positive emotion measurably accelerates recovery from the cardiovascular aftermath of fear — the body's threat-arousal is quenched faster when positive affect is present. This gives Aquinas's prized act of *endurance* a mechanism. To endure under danger is to keep the action-repertoire from collapsing into the flight-tunnel; resilience, on Fredrickson's account, is exactly the capacity to retain breadth and recover arousal under threat, and it is trainable (loving-kindness practice raises vagal tone, the physiological signature of this capacity). The brave person is not the one without fear but the one whose system does not narrow to the single flight-response when fear arrives — and that is a measurable, cultivable state, not merely a moral posture.

**Friston** gives the formal account of what "holding a long-horizon good" *is*. In Tom's PRS-form record of his work, an agent under active inference acts to minimize expected free energy — prediction error integrated over its modeled future. Fear is a high-precision, short-horizon error signal: *this is dangerous, act now*. Fortitude is the agent's capacity to keep its policy anchored to a *deeper attractor* in its generative model — the good it most values, the outcome it is most committed to realizing — so that the immediate gradient does not capture the policy. Computationally, courage is a contest of horizons: the long-horizon expected-value term holding against the steep but short-horizon threat term. This reframes Aquinas's "endurance is harder than attack" precisely: attack discharges the gradient (act, and the tension resolves); endurance requires keeping the policy fixed on the distant attractor *while the steep local gradient persists unrelieved* — which is exactly the harder computation.

**McGilchrist** supplies the phenomenology. In Tom's PRS-form record of his work, the left hemisphere grasps, controls, and seeks to manipulate a danger into manageability; the right hemisphere can stand open to the whole situation — including what cannot be controlled — without fleeing into either denial or frantic mastery. Courage in his key is the right hemisphere's capacity to *remain present to the whole*, danger included, rather than collapsing into the left's grasp for control or the body's flight. The brave stand in the world as it is.

**Stump** enters as Thomistic interlocutor, and here she is more than a foil: her work on fortitude-in-suffering and on the second-personal texture of endurance illuminates why fortitude's principal act is to *sustain* — to remain in relation, and in fidelity, under a duress one cannot end.

**Wright** grounds the scriptural and metaphysical claim that makes endurance rational. In *The Resurrection of the Son of God* and *Surprised by Hope*, the resurrection reframes death itself: if new creation is real and bodily, then the worst the threat-signal can promise — death — is not the final horizon, and an agent may rationally hold to a good *beyond* it. Courage, on this account, is not the suppression of an accurate fear but the relocation of the agent's true long-horizon attractor past the point the fear can reach. **Rohr** gives the contemplative form: *necessary suffering* — the path of descent as the transition the modern West has learned to manufacture its way around — and, in *Immortal Diamond* and *Falling Upward*, "dying before you die": the willingness to let the False Self's survival-project be relativized, which is precisely what frees the agent to endure without being governed by the threat to that self. ## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The contemporary-parallel response: fortitude is the capacity of an embodied agent to keep its policy anchored to a long-horizon, often other-regarding good (Friston) while the fast, narrowing threat-response pulls toward flight — a capacity whose physiological substrate is the resilience that keeps the action-repertoire from collapsing and quenches fear-arousal under load (Fredrickson), and whose phenomenology is remaining present to the whole situation rather than fleeing into control or panic (McGilchrist). Its principal act is endurance because endurance is the harder computation: holding the distant attractor while the steep local gradient persists unrelieved.

What it shares with Aquinas: fortitude is firmness against danger, supremely the fear of death; its chief act is to endure, not to attack; it does not abolish fear but acts through it; and recklessness (no fear at all) is a defect, not the virtue — in the active-inference frame, the fearless agent has simply mis-estimated the threat precision, which is an error, not courage. Where it reframes: the medieval "firmness of mind" gains a mechanism (resilience, broadened repertoire, vagal tone) and a formal structure (long-horizon attractor versus short-horizon gradient). Evidence strength is unusually mixed and worth marking honestly: the broaden-and-build and undo-effect findings are empirical and replicated; the active-inference reading is formal and (in this application) interpretive; the hemispheric account is empirical-plus-interpretive; and the claim that resurrection relocates the agent's true horizon is theological — firm within Wright's program, but not an empirical result and not smuggled as one.

On Q.122, the precepts of justice: under this frame the Decalogue's commands of justice function as the *specification of the long-horizon goods* that fortitude then defends. Justice says what is due; fortitude is what lets an agent keep rendering it when rendering it is dangerous. The two questions are a single arc — the goods worth standing for, and the standing.

## Central-theme thread

This is where the project's central claim becomes almost literal. Conscious agents are individuated by the limits of their perspective, and each computes toward loving unity under the resources it perceives. Fear is the sharpest report of perceived resource-limitation: *you may not have what it takes; you may not survive this*. Fortitude is the agent continuing to compute toward the good *through* that report — and Aquinas's choice of "endurance" over "attack" as the principal act is, in this light, exactly right, because the central theme is not about conquering the limitation but about remaining faithful within it. The brave agent does not pretend the danger away (recklessness) and does not let the danger govern (cowardice); it holds the distant good while fully feeling the near threat. "Work out your salvation in fear and trembling" (Phil 2:12) is, almost word for word, a description of fortitude: salvation — inclusion in the people united with God — pursued by limited agents who tremble at the cost and continue anyway, guided by those (human and divine) who perceive more of the whole, and supremely by the resurrection's promise that the horizon the fear reports as final is not.
