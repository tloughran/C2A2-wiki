# Day 166 — Testing God (Contemporary)

## Frame

Aquinas's two questions today probe the same nerve from opposite sides, and Habash walks all eight articles. Q.96 asks what kinds of *observances* are licit, sorting them by what the practice is actually grounded in: the magical arts as such (a.1), observances directed at altering bodies — healing and the like (a.2), observances directed at fortune-telling (a.3), and the wearing of sacred words at the neck (a.4). His test across all four is whether the practice is grounded in (i) divine institution, (ii) the nature of the thing itself, or (iii) only in a tacit pact with the occult — the last two articles turning on the fact that a useless means, expected to work anyway, implies an appeal to a helper who is not God. Q.97 then asks when *testing God* counts as the vice of irreligion by deficiency: what temptation of God consists in — deeds whose expected result can come from God alone (a.1), whether it is a sin (a.2), whether it is opposed to the virtue of religion (a.3), and whether it is graver than superstition (a.4). Both questions are about how a finite agent under genuine uncertainty should *consult* and *test* its world-model without corrupting either the consulting or the testing.

In contemporary terms, the question is: how should an embodied predictive agent, whose only access to reality is mediated by an interface, calibrate its tests of that interface? When does a test reflect honest model-updating, and when does it reveal a refusal to update? The mind-monist convergence — Levin's substrate-independent cognition, Friston's active inference, Hoffman's interface theory — supplies a precise contemporary vocabulary for what Aquinas is doing with the medieval categories of *observance* and *temptation of God*.

## Problem

The shared underlying problem: an agent with limited perspective must act in a world it does not fully model. To act well, it must run tests. But not every test is honest. Some tests are designed to extract a confirmation the agent has already decided it wants; others are designed to substitute spectacle for the costlier work of updating one's priors on ordinary evidence. The medieval "tempter of God" demands a miracle in place of cheap, available evidence; the contemporary equivalent demands a clean experimental result in place of converging weak signals already in hand. In both cases, the structure is the same: the agent corrupts the testing apparatus to protect a prior it would rather not revise.

## Resource

Friston's active inference (Friston PRS-04 in the Friston-tradition wiki: organism as active modeler; Friston PRS-07 on substrate-agnostic free-energy minimization) gives the formal version: a healthy agent under uncertainty runs cheap, in-distribution tests whose expected information gain is high relative to their action cost. Demanding an out-of-distribution, high-cost confirmation — the equivalent of jumping off the temple — is symptomatic of a model whose priors are so badly miscalibrated that ordinary evidence no longer touches them. The agent is not testing reality; it is testing whether reality will rescue a model the agent already knows is failing.

Levin's basal-cognition program (Levin PRS-04, PRS-08 in the Levin-tradition wiki) extends the same logic below the neural: even single cells navigate anatomical morphospace by running cheap, local tests against bioelectric gradients. When the cellular collective demands a miraculous correction — as cancers do, refusing the ordinary signaling that maintains the morphogenetic field — the result is defection, not discovery.

McGilchrist's account of attention as ontological act (McGilchrist PRS-05 in the McGilchrist-tradition wiki) supplies the phenomenological layer. The mode of attention determines what kind of world shows up. Left-hemisphere demand-attention — "show me the sign on my terms" — encounters a world of objects under manipulation. Right-hemisphere receptive attention encounters a world that responds to honest consultation. The medieval prohibition on tempting God is a prohibition on the wrong mode of attending to the source of guidance.

Wright's canonical scholarship reads the temptation-narratives (Matt. 4 / Luke 4) the same way: Jesus refuses each test not because tests are forbidden but because *these* tests would have been demands rather than honest seeking. The Pauline pattern is consistent — *pistis* is trust-under-honest-consultation, not credulity, but also not the agent's attempt to extort confirmation. Rohr in *The Universal Christ* reads the same distinction as the move from grasping to receiving: the contemplative tradition prizes the disposition that asks honestly and is willing to be answered in unexpected forms (PRS-43 in the Rohr-tradition wiki, Medium: guidance is not extractable by lookup, and the underdetermination of specifics is a feature of the mode of knowing, not a deficiency of the source).

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

Aquinas's central response, framed in mind-monist terms: an agent's tests of the source of its guidance must be *constitutive* of the agent's ongoing relation to that source, not extractive from it. The tempter-of-God anti-pattern is the agent that would commodify divine guidance into a spectacle deliverable on demand; the contemporary version is the agent that would commodify any source of correction — a tradition, a teacher, an experiment — by making it perform on the agent's terms rather than entering into ongoing fidelity with it.

What this shares with Aquinas: the recognition that some demands on reality are themselves disordered, even when the *content* of the demand is "show me the truth." What it reframes: rather than locate the disorder in the breaking of a categorical rule against signs, the contemporary frame locates it in the corruption of the testing apparatus itself. The honest test is one whose result the agent will accept; the dishonest test is rigged in advance.

Evidence strength: the active-inference framing is mathematically formal. The prudential principle — that agents under uncertainty should not pay miracle-cost for confirmation they could obtain at ordinary cost — is empirical, well-established in decision theory and control theory. The theological framing of *why* one should refuse to commodify the source of guidance is interpretive: it depends on a prior commitment to relation-with-source as constitutive of the agent rather than instrumental to it.

## Central-theme thread

The perspective-limited agent who would be wholly delivered from limitation in a single revelatory moment is asking for individuation to be undone all at once — not for the slow, costly, in-fidelity computation of loving unity from within limitation. Aquinas's "do not tempt God" is, read this way, a constraint that *protects* the slow work. Salvation in fear and trembling, in Wright's reading, is the patient working-out of inclusion in a covenanted people; in the contemporary frame, it is the patient running of in-distribution tests whose accumulating results actually update one's priors. The tempter-of-God anti-pattern short-circuits that work; the licit-observance pattern in Q.96 carries it forward by encoding genuine corrections from the tradition into ordinary practice.

## Where this leaves us

The pair Q.96–97 opens, in the contemporary frame, the broader question of *what an agent owes the source of its corrections*. Tomorrow's Q.98–99 on perjury and sacrilege extends this from the consultative test to the costly signal: the oath as the agent's binding of itself to a future-honoring its present claim. If today's question is how to test the source without corrupting it, tomorrow's is how to bind oneself to one's own testimony without corrupting the binding.
