# Day 113 — The Republic (Contemporary)

## Frame

Q.104-105 cover the judicial precepts: laws on rulers and subjects, fellow-citizens, foreigners (including war), and household. The standout claims:

1. The judicial precepts ceased with the change in covenant; what was perennially binding in the moral law continues (Q.104 a.3).
2. The best form of government — as the Old Law shows under Moses — is a *mixed* form, combining elements of monarchy, aristocracy, and democracy, because this best preserves peace and is most enduring (Q.105 a.1).
3. Aquinas in *De regno* argues for monarchy from natural-analogy reasoning *but* qualifies that "the power granted to a king is so great, it easily degenerates into tyranny unless he to whom this power is given be a very virtuous man… now perfect virtue is to be found in few." The Fall-grounded prudential reasoning prefers the mixed form in practice.

The contemporary question: *what does the convergent paradigm say about institutional design under bounded rationality and perspective-limitation?*

## Problem

The shared problem: how to organize *coercive* authority within a community such that the coordination achieves the common good without producing tyranny or anarchy. Aquinas's prudential answer is the mixed form. The contemporary question is whether this prudential answer can be empirically and formally vindicated.

## Resource: distributed inference, hemispheric integration, computational irreducibility, mechanism-design under bounded rationality

**Friston's active inference at the multi-agent level** (FLAG-05): when many agents are doing inference about a shared world, *combining their inferences* — under appropriate weighting — produces a better estimate than any single agent's inference. This is the mathematical foundation of the wisdom-of-crowds insight that Aquinas's mixed-form argument independently arrives at. Distributed inference is *not* mere democracy; it requires structured aggregation (an *aristocracy of virtue* in Aquinas's frame) plus broad participation (the *democratic* element) plus executive coherence (the *kingdom* element).

**McGilchrist's hemispheric asymmetry** (FLAG-09): McGilchrist's work suggests cultures dominated by left-hemispheric cognition produce centralized, decontextualized, abstraction-loving institutional forms; cultures with healthier right-hemispheric integration produce more relational, embedded, distributed forms. Q.105 a.1's mixed form is the institutional analog of McGilchrist's "balanced asymmetry" — the right hemisphere as master (the integrating, relational orientation toward the common good) using the left hemisphere as servant (the rule-following, executive, propositional structure that makes the integration actionable).

**Wolfram's computational irreducibility** (PRS-03): no algorithm can derive the right institutional design for a particular community from first principles alone. Each community must run its own institutional experiment under its specific conditions. The general structural insight (mixed form is robust) is invariant; the specific cashing-out is irreducibly local.

**Hoffman's interface theory** (FLAG-02): legitimate authority is a *signal* in the political interface — it tracks something real (the community's coordination needs and its members' deep priors) but is not the same as the underlying reality. Communities can mis-recognize legitimacy; legitimacy can be transferred, lost, or restored. Q.105 a.5's "even foreigners are subject to higher powers" is the recognition that the legitimacy signal can extend across community boundaries.

**CROSS-14 (master wiki on institutional design under bounded rationality)**: public-choice theory, mechanism-design, and constitutional economics have independently arrived at structurally similar conclusions to Q.105 a.1. Concentrated power is high-variance under bounded-rationality conditions: when the concentrated ruler is virtuous, results are excellent; when not, results are catastrophic. Distributed power has lower variance and higher mean under realistic conditions where rulers are not reliably virtuous. Aquinas's prudential argument is the medieval formulation of this empirical-economic insight.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The mixed-form-best argument is a theorem about *institutional design under perspective-limitation*. Concentrated power presupposes a perspective-rich ruler; perspective-richness is rare; therefore distributed structures that integrate multiple limited perspectives produce more reliable common-good outcomes.

The judicial precepts of the Old Law were a *specific* such design — the design appropriate to Israel as a covenanted theocracy under direct revelation. With the covenant transition, the *specific* design ceased; the *structural insight* (perspective-distribution under coordination) continued and continues.

### What this shares with Aquinas

- Mixed government is the most enduring form, accommodating both the dignity of self-government and the prudential reality of unequal virtue.
- Authority is real and binding when oriented to the common good.
- The judicial precepts of the Old Law had their specific function for Israel; that function ceased with Christ, while the underlying natural-law principles continue.
- Conflict (war, criminal justice) is *not* outside the natural-law frame; just-war reasoning, fair-trial reasoning, and proportionate punishment all derive from natural-law principles.

### Where this reframes

- **The *De regno* monarchy argument and the *Summa* mixed-form argument are reconcilable** under perspective-limitation: pure-monarchy is the *abstract optimum* (analogous to the cosmic monarchy of God) but the *practical optimum* under realistic conditions of limited and unevenly-distributed virtue is the mixed form. Aquinas's natural-analogy reasoning sets the ceiling; his prudential reasoning sets the floor. Contemporary institutional design under realistic anthropology agrees.
- **The slavery in the Old Law** is structurally distant from chattel slavery in modern history. The synthesis must hold this carefully — Aquinas's defense of Old-Law slavery as a regulated, time-limited institution (Ex 21's six-year term, freedom in the seventh, the death penalty for man-stealing) is not a defense of slavery-as-such, and Aquinas himself elsewhere argues against slavery-by-conquest as unjust. The contemporary reading should hold the *natural-law constraints* on labor relations (no man-stealing, no perpetual bondage, family integrity preserved) without endorsing the institution per se.
- **The herem texts deserve more nuance than the host's harmonization provides.** Wright, Goldingay, Boyd, Copan, and others have done careful work on this — addressing the genre (whether *herem* is descriptive of intent or polemic/typological), the theological function (whether the *herem* command should be read in the rhetorical-typological mode of Ancient-Near-Eastern conquest accounts), and the question of how a God of love commands such things. The synthesis should not flatten this difficulty into a simple "all men live under a death sentence" answer. The honest theological position is that this is among the hardest problems in biblical theology and deserves sustained engagement.

### Where this is firm vs. speculative

*Empirical*: Public-choice theory and constitutional-economics findings on the variance properties of concentrated vs. distributed power. McGilchrist's hemispheric data. Cross-cultural institutional persistence findings.

*Formal*: Mechanism-design under bounded rationality; multi-agent active inference.

*Speculative*: Full reconciliation of *De regno* monarchy argument with *Summa* mixed-form argument.

*Theological/hard*: Old-Law slavery and *herem* texts deserve sustained theological work the synthesis cannot supply at this length.

## The Central-Theme Thread

The central theme attaches sharply here. *Perspective-limitation* is the empirical reality that institutional design must accommodate. No single ruler has the perspective to see the common good fully; concentrated power on a single perspective is therefore high-variance; distributed structures that integrate many limited perspectives reduce variance and increase mean alignment with the common good.

This is the central-theme claim in its political-theological form: *the loving-unity telos is approached, in the social-institutional space, by structures that allow many limited perspectives to inform one another*. The covenanted community Wright describes is precisely this structure at the theological level: many tribes, many gifts, many specific vocations, integrated under the one Lord. The Trinitarian formal structure (one God in three persons related by love) is the metaphysical archetype the mixed-form approximates institutionally.

"We work out our salvation in fear and trembling" attaches here to the recognition that *our institutional designs are themselves perspective-limited*. The community designing its mixed-form is doing so under the same conditions that make the mixed-form necessary. The work is iterative, error-prone, requires humility, and requires the import of perspective-broadening signals (revelation, mentors, diversity of voices) that the community cannot generate from within.

## Where this leaves us

Tomorrow (Day 114, Q.106-107) makes the central pivot of the entire treatise: from law-as-text to law-as-Spirit. The contemporary reading must hold the metaphysical claim that the *new law is principally not text but person* — the indwelling Spirit as the principle of fulfillment-in-charity. This is precisely the threshold the central-theme reading has been pointing toward: the import of perspective-broadening priors that the agent cannot generate from within. Open thread: how does the synthesis hold the radical interiority claim of Q.106 a.1 (new law as inscribed-on-heart by the Spirit) alongside the recognition that the New Law also has external/written/communal-practice dimensions (sacraments, scripture, ecclesial life)? The integrated reading is that the *interior* operation requires the *exterior* practices as its training ground.
