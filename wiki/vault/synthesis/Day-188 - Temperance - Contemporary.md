# Day 188 — Temperance (Contemporary Parallel)

## Frame

Aquinas first closes fortitude by asking whether the divine law rightly *commands* courage and its annexed virtues (Q.140), then opens the long treatise on temperance (Q.141). Temperance is the virtue that moderates the agent's strongest bodily pleasures — those of touch, in food, drink, and procreation — bringing the concupiscible passions under the rule of reason. Aquinas builds the treatise on a tight parallel: as fortitude is chiefly about the *greatest fear* (death in battle), temperance is chiefly about the *greatest pleasures* (the ones most necessary to the survival of the individual and the species). The contemporary version of the question, under mind / conscious-realist monism: *how does an embodied agent regulate the very appetites that keep it alive, and what does it mean that this self-regulation is, by Aquinas's own ranking, a lesser good than the virtues that serve the many?*

## Problem

Two inquiry-problems sit inside this article, and both bridge the medieval and contemporary frames.

The first is **homeostatic self-regulation under necessity**. Temperance is uniquely hard because its objects are *necessary*: the agent cannot simply renounce food or sex the way it might renounce a luxury, because these are the operations "which preserve the nature of the individual... and the nature of the species" (Q.141 a.4). The agent must regulate exactly the drives it cannot abolish. This is the control problem at the heart of any persisting biological system: maintain the set-points that keep you alive without letting the error-correcting machinery run away into excess or, on the other side, into the life-denying defect.

The second is a **scale problem**, and it is the more provocative. Aquinas ranks temperance *below* justice and fortitude (a.8) on an explicit principle from Aristotle: "the good of the many is more godlike than the good of the individual." Temperance loses rank because "the glutton, an unchaste man, may only sin against himself," whereas justice and fortitude touch others and even "the entire nation." The contemporary question this opens: is self-regulation really only about the individual? Or does the agent's internal homeostasis turn out to be the substrate on which any larger, multi-agent good is built?

## Resource

For the first problem, the leading resource is the active-inference framework recorded in the Friston-tradition wiki: *precision-weighting as temperance*. An embodied agent maintains itself by minimizing the long-run surprise of its interoceptive states; appetite is the signal that a homeostatic set-point (caloric, reproductive) is being predicted to drift. Temperance, in this register, is well-calibrated precision on those interoceptive priors: the agent neither under-weights the appetite into the life-denying defect Aquinas will name tomorrow as *insensibility*, nor over-weights it into the runaway excess of intemperance. The strength of the parallel is that Aquinas already frames temperance as *moderation set by need* — "the rule of temperance... is that a man not take any more or any less of sensible pleasurable goods... than is required by reason for his life and for his station" (a.6). "No more or any less, set by need" is, almost verbatim, a homeostatic-regulation criterion. Augustine's image of using the goods of this life "with the moderation of a user, not the attainment of a lover," and Savonarola's image of the Christian who "takes only as much medicine as is necessary to sustain health," are both statements of optimal set-point tracking rather than maximization.

The Fredrickson-tradition wiki adds the empirical biology. Its program treats *vagal tone* as a biomarker of self-regulatory capacity and of the ability to return to baseline after arousal (an active research question in that wiki asks whether vagal tone can index community health). Temperance has a measurable physiological signature: the capacity to down-regulate appetitive arousal and recover equilibrium is exactly what high vagal tone tracks. This keeps the synthesis honest — there is real, present-day evidence that self-regulation of bodily drive is a trainable, embodied capacity, not merely a moral abstraction.

For the second problem — scale — the resource is the Levin-tradition program, where agency scales continuously from cell to tissue to organism to community, each level a homeostatic agent nested in the next. This is where the synthesis can press Aquinas's ranking. The cross-program index's CROSS-003 (the attention cluster: Friston precision-weighting, McGilchrist hemispheric attention, Fredrickson broadened scope, Hoffman interface) and CROSS-005 (love as a fundamental category across Fredrickson, Stump, Kastrup) together suggest that the individual's internal regulation is not sealed off from the good of the many — it is the precondition for it. An agent that cannot regulate its own appetites cannot enter the positivity-resonance couplings (Fredrickson's Love 2.0) by which communities form.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The contemporary-parallel response: temperance is well-calibrated interoceptive precision — the embodied agent tracking its necessary appetitive set-points by need rather than by maximization — and Aquinas's ranking of it below justice and fortitude is *half* right.

*What it shares with Aquinas.* The synthesis keeps his core architecture: temperance is real self-mastery, cardinal because it governs the most necessary and most difficult-to-abstain-from pleasures; its rule is set by need and station, not by suppression; and its corresponding gift is *fear* — the fear of God that "withholds man from the pleasures of the flesh" (a.1), which the synthesis can read without embarrassment as a high-level prior that re-weights local appetite against an ultimate good. Aquinas's refusal to treat temperance as mere abstinence — "no more *or any less* than is required" — is exactly the point a modern self-regulation account would insist on, and the synthesis should foreground it against any ascetic caricature.

*Where it reframes.* "Concupiscible passion" becomes interoceptive prediction-error; "the rule of reason" becomes the agent's higher-order generative model that sets the appetitive set-points; "moderation by need and station" becomes optimal homeostatic tracking under a social and embodied context. The substantive reframe is on the *scale ranking*. Aquinas demotes temperance because it "regards only the individual." Under a paradigm where agency scales continuously and where loving union is itself realized through embodied, physiologically-mediated coupling (Fredrickson's positivity resonance, the biological substrate that CROSS-005 links to Stump's union and Kastrup's re-integration), the boundary between "good of the individual" and "good of the many" is porous. The agent's internal temperance is the *condition* of its capacity to participate in the larger good — an intemperate agent is a destabilizing node in any community it joins. So the synthesis proposes: temperance is correctly ranked below justice and fortitude *as a direct contribution* to the common good, but it is wrongly seen as *merely* private. It is the homeostatic floor on which collective intelligence stands.

*Evidence strength, honestly.* The precision-weighting / homeostatic reading of temperance is formally well-motivated but interpretive (medium). Vagal tone as a self-regulation biomarker is empirically supported (medium-high), though its identification with the *virtue* of temperance is an extrapolation. The scale argument is philosophical, leaning on Levin's empirically-grounded scaling of agency but reaching past what is strictly demonstrated. The body-as-temple / station reading is theological.

## The central-theme thread

Temperance is the agent computing the good under the scarcity of its *own body* — the most intimate resource-limitation there is. The central claim of this project is that conscious agents are individuated by the limits of their perspective, and there is no limit more constitutive than embodiment: hunger, fatigue, desire are the perspective. Temperance is what it looks like to work out one's salvation in fear and trembling at the level of the flesh — not by escaping the body's limits but by ordering them toward the whole. Wright's register makes this concrete and anti-dualist: the body is not a prison to be transcended but, in the Pauline frame, a temple — and salvation is new-creation embodiment, not disembodiment. Temperance is therefore not contempt for the body but its right ordering toward inclusion in the covenanted people. Rohr's non-dual register guards the same point from the other side: the temperate person neither grasps the goods of this life as a lover nor despises them as an enemy, but uses them — holding the contradiction (good but not ultimate) without collapsing it either way.
