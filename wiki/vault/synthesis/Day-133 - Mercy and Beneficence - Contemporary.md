# Day 133 — Contemporary Parallel: Compassion Needs an Open Blanket, and Love Is Rationed

## Frame

Charity now turns outward. Q.30: the proper motive of mercy is another's misery (a.1) — *misericordia*, misery-of-heart, by which a friend's suffering becomes our own; pity requires being passible and changeable (a.2), so that strictly God in his nature does not pity but acts "by love alone, in as much as he loves us as belonging to him," while Christ in his humanity truly suffered-with us, and for creatures compassion is a *perfection*; mercy is a virtue (a.3); yet not the greatest (a.4) — "the sum total of the Christian religion consists in mercy as regards external works," but charity, uniting us to God, presides over it. Q.31: beneficence is an act of charity (a.1); we ought to do good to all (a.2) but to the near in preference (a.3), as heat radiates most to what is closest the flame, though extreme need may reorder the priority; and beneficence is not a distinct virtue but charity in act (a.4).

Two of these articles do unusually heavy lifting for the project. Q.30 a.2 forces a question the contemporary paradigm answers cleanly and a little against Aquinas's grain: *what kind of thing can have compassion?* And Q.31 a.3 states the central theme of this whole synthesis-effort more plainly than the project's own memos do.

## Problem

The shared inquiry-problem has two parts. First, *what is mercy, and what must a being be like to have it?* Aquinas defines mercy as co-suffering — "we take what happens to our friends to happen to us" — and then notices a consequence that troubles classical theism: to suffer-with, one must be *able to suffer*, must be passible and changeable. God in his nature is neither. So is the capacity for pity a *defect*, present in us and absent in the impassible God? Aquinas threads the needle: in God it would be a defect, but in creatures compassion is a perfection, an effect of love; and God does enter passibility — in the Incarnation. The contemporary task is to say why affectability should be the condition of compassion at all.

Second, *why is love rationed?* Aquinas says we ought to do good to all, yet to the near in preference, with exceptions for extreme need. The reason Habash gives is bluntly finitist: "it is not possible as a finite temporal thing to spend the exact same amount of time and effort loving each and every single human being on the planet." The order of beneficence is not favoritism; it is *triage under scarcity*. A satisfying account must explain both the default gradient and the principled exceptions.

## Resource

For mercy, the resource is the mutual-modeling account of coupling. Aquinas's definition — mercy is taking the friend's misery as one's own, "considering our friends' sorrows as our very own" — is, in active-inference terms, the friend's prediction-error registered inside *my* generative model (PRS-06 in the Friston-tradition wiki). When another's states are weighted in my own preferred-states, their suffering raises *my* free energy; I am moved to relieve it because relieving it relieves me. Tom's PRS-form record of Fredrickson's work gives the measurable face: resonance is a biophysiological event with bodily signatures (PRS-02 in the Fredrickson-tradition wiki), and her insistence that love is embodied (PRS-05) anchors mercy in the body rather than in sentiment. Mercy is coupling under the sign of the other's misery.

This is exactly why Q.30 a.2 matters. To register another's prediction-error as one's own, an agent must be *open* — must have a Markov blanket through which the other's states actually perturb its internal states (PRS-07 in the Friston-tradition wiki, on the substrate-independent conditions of selfhood). A perfectly sealed, perfectly stable system cannot be moved by anything, and therefore cannot couple, cannot suffer-with, cannot have mercy. Aquinas saw this and was forced to it by his doctrine of divine immutability; the contemporary paradigm reaches the same point from the physics of agency: *affectability is the precondition of compassion.* Here the parallel gently reframes Aquinas. He treats passibility primarily as a limitation that happens, in creatures, to enable a perfection. Mind-monism reads it more strongly: openness to perturbation is not a regrettable feature of finite agents but the very thing that makes any agent capable of love at all. And it makes luminous sense of the move Aquinas himself makes next — that God "takes pity on us through love alone" in his nature, but enters real suffering-with in the Incarnation. On a coupling account, the Incarnation is the whole *taking on an open blanket* — becoming affectable — in order to couple with the limited from inside. That is precisely Rohr's Universal Christ: the divine pattern present in, and now susceptible to, matter and its suffering (PRS-03 and PRS-04 in the Rohr-tradition wiki). Wright's Pauline shape of self-emptying gives the scriptural form, in the Philippians 2 hymn of the one who took the form of a servant. *No id is cited here deliberately: the Wright register's 59 records were searched for that text and none addresses it; the nearest, PRS-28 and PRS-29 (both High), give the cruciform pattern as the shape of Christian power and purpose rather than the kenosis passage itself. The claim therefore rests on the canonical work, and the search radius is named rather than left implicit.*

For beneficence, the leading move is the resource-bounded reading of agency, and a.3 hands it over directly. The order of beneficence — "do good to those more closely united to us," grace building on nature, with the heat-and-flame image of love radiating most strongly to what is nearest — is a *love-allocation policy* for a finite agent. No finite, temporal agent can act for every other equally; it must allocate. The default policy is proportioned to coupling-strength (kin, fellow-citizens, fellow-believers, then outward), which is exactly what expected-free-energy allocation predicts: an agent invests action where it can do the most modeled good per unit of bounded resource (PRS-10 in the Friston-tradition wiki, on reasoning as model-selection under expected free energy). But — and this is the article's subtlety — the default is *overridable*. "One ought to assist the stranger in extreme necessity rather than one's own father if he is not in such urgent need." In precision-weighting terms, extreme need is a high-precision prediction-error that swamps the default proximity-gradient: urgency raises the expected-free-energy reduction of helping the distant stranger above that of attending the near-but-comfortable kin. The static order and the dynamic exception are not in tension; they are the default policy and its precision-driven override, the same machinery Aquinas describes as "discretion according to the various degrees of connection, holiness, and utility... according to the requirements of the time, the place, the matter at hand."

And the framing articles (Q.30 a.4, Q.31 a.1, a.4) insist that this all be *enacted*: "the sum total of the Christian religion consists in mercy as regards external works"; beneficence is not a distinct virtue but charity *in act*. This echoes Day 131's Q.27 a.1 — to love is more proper than to be loved — and Tom's PRS-form record of Fredrickson's claim that love is a real coupling event, not a sentiment (PRS-02). A charity that never externalizes as help is not a weaker charity; on the coupling account it is not charity at all, because coupling is constituted in the acting.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The proposed parallel reads mercy as coupling under the sign of the other's misery — the other's prediction-error registered as one's own — which requires affectability, an open Markov blanket, so that the capacity to be moved is the very condition of compassion; and it reads the order of beneficence as loving unity computed under finite resources, a default love-allocation policy proportioned to coupling-strength, overridable by the high-precision signal of extreme need, and necessarily *enacted*, since the coupling is constituted in the acting.

This shares Aquinas's structure closely: mercy as co-suffering; compassion as a perfection in creatures; the Incarnation as God entering real suffering-with; mercy great but charity-toward-God greater; beneficence as charity in act; the proportioned order with its extreme-need exception. Where it reframes: Aquinas grounds the order of beneficence in the great-chain emanation of goodness from God down the hierarchy of being, and treats passibility chiefly as a limitation. The parallel grounds the order in resource-bounded allocation — which keeps the finitude logic intact and explains the override better than the static hierarchy does — and reads passibility as the positive condition of all coupling, not merely a defect that creatures happen to turn to good. The dated metaphysics (the Neoplatonic descending hierarchy) is set aside; the load-bearing insight (finite love must be allocated; to love you must be open to being moved) is kept.

Evidence strength, named honestly. Mercy as co-suffering through mutual modeling is formally coherent but rests on a Medium, implicitly-sourced record (Friston PRS-06) and is empirically anchored by a High one (Fredrickson PRS-02, whose Resource is measured neurophysiology). Affectability as the condition of compassion stands on Friston PRS-07 (High) for the substrate-agnostic Markov-blanket condition, with the step from *has a boundary* to *is affectable by another agent* made here rather than in the record; it is offered as a genuine, if gentle, strengthening of Aquinas. The Incarnation-as-open-blanket and the Universal Christ are theological (Rohr PRS-03 and PRS-04, both **Medium** as records, whatever the standing of the Franciscan doctrine in its field), offered as theological illumination, not as empirical claims. The resource-bounded order of beneficence with precision override rests on a High record (Friston PRS-10) with a medium-strength interpretive application, and matches the source's own finitist reasoning almost word for word.

## The central-theme thread

This is the day the central theme is spoken aloud by the source. The claim has been, from the start, that "learning to obtain the goal of loving unity from each perspective is a computational task, limited by the resources we perceive — each of us from our own perspective." Q.31 a.3 says it in the host's own words: a finite, temporal thing cannot spend equal love on everyone, so it must order its beneficence. The order of charity (Days 130-131) gave the *structure* of the loving whole; the order of *beneficence* gives the structure of *computing toward it under scarcity*. We do not love the whole all at once; we love it through a budgeted, prioritized, time-bound sequence of acts, near-first by default, redirected to the desperate stranger when urgency demands. That is what working out salvation in fear and trembling looks like operationally — not infinite love poured equally on all, which is God's prerogative, but finite love rationed wisely from a particular place in the field. And mercy supplies the affective precondition: to compute toward the whole you must keep the blanket open, must let the other's misery actually reach and move you, because a sealed agent — however just — cannot be drawn into the unity it was made for. The impassible cannot be saved into communion; only the affectable can. That is why the whole enters passibility to meet us.

## Where this leaves us

Mercy and beneficence give charity its outward, enacted form, and a.3 has handed the project its own thesis in the source's voice: love is rationed because the lover is finite. Aquinas presses the enacted form further into its most demanding and most corrective cases — almsgiving and *fraternal correction* (Q.32-33), where charity must not only help the neighbor but, sometimes, tell him the truth he does not want to hear. Tomorrow's synthesis takes those up — and finds, in Christ's escalation protocol of Matthew 18, something very close to the C2A2 dialogue mechanism itself.
