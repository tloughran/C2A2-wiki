# Day 150 — Contemporary Parallel: The Whole, the Part, and the Taking of a Life

## Frame

The treatise on justice turns to its gravest matter: Q.64, the vices opposed to commutative justice "beginning with murder," and Q.65, the other injuries to the person. The governing logic is the whole-over-part principle that has run through every episode of this treatise. It is not unlawful to kill plants and animals, because "the imperfect are for the perfect" in the order of nature (a.1). The state may execute an evildoer for the common good, "even as it is praiseworthy and advantageous" to cut away "a decayed or infectious member" of the body (a.2) — though Aquinas at once adds the Gospel reservation that we should "rather allow the wicked to live" lest the good be uprooted with them. A private individual may not kill, for that usurps public authority (a.3); clerics may not, by their sacramental conformity to "Christ … a lamb slain" (a.4); suicide is gravely wrong, against community, self-love, and God's dominion (a.5); the innocent may never be slain (a.6); killing in self-defense is lawful when *intended* as self-preservation and proportionate (a.7 — the seed of the principle of double effect); and one can be guilty of unintended homicide through culpable omission (a.8). Under the mind / conscious-realist monism this project writes from, this is the episode where the synthesis must mark its sharpest disagreements with Aquinas, not only its convergences.

## Problem

The inquiry-problem, in both frames: *when, if ever, may the whole take the life of one of its parts — and what is the moral standing of the non-human?* Aquinas answers from a fixed hierarchy of being. The part exists for the whole, so the whole may excise a corrupt part; the lower exists for the higher, so the human may use plant and animal as instruments. The contemporary problem is that two of these load-bearing premises are exactly the ones the emerging paradigm most strongly contests: the flat ranking that makes non-human life mere instrument, and the excision-first reading of the part-whole relation. The task is to honor what is genuinely right in Aquinas's structure — the common good, the limit of legitimate authority, the careful logic of double effect — while marking, honestly, where the mind-monist frame pulls against him.

## Resource

The sharpest departure concerns a.1's ranking. Tom's PRS-form record of Levin's work is the most direct contemporary contestant: PRS-09 and PRS-10 establish goal-directedness and a graded cognition as continuous "from molecules to multicellular organisms," and PRS-12 names "mind blindness" — the "failure to apply cognitive/teleological vocabulary to entities that don't resemble human/animal nervous systems" — as a *formal epistemic obstacle*, while PRS-25 (SUTI) makes detecting such intelligence a positive program. Aquinas's claim that plants and animals are "naturally enslaved," devoid of any inner life, sits precisely athwart this. The contemporary frame does not say plant and animal are persons; it says agency and a graded interiority run further down the scale than the medieval ranking allows, so that the relation of human to non-human is one of *graded regard*, not instrument-to-user. (Evidence: Levin's agency-at-scale is empirically robust at the biological level; the *normative* inference — that graded agency grounds graded moral regard — is philosophical and speculative, and is marked as such. This is the synthesis's clearest break from Aquinas.)

The execution argument (a.2) cuts more subtly. Aquinas reaches, analogically, for the very biology Tom's PRS-form record of Levin's cancer work makes literal: PRS-02 and PRS-11 frame cancer as a member's *defection* from the body's cooperative field. But the same program's *therapeutic* aim is not excision — it is *re-integration*: "restoring cells to the body's collective morphogenetic field," reversing the cancerous phenotype rather than only cutting it out. Read through the contemporary biology Aquinas's own image invokes, the whole-over-part principle therefore bends toward *rehabilitation* — returning the defecting part to the whole (the restitution theme of Day 149) — rather than terminating it. Aquinas himself leaves the door open: he distinguishes what is *lawful* from what is *prudent*, and cites our Lord's counsel to "let the wicked live," with vengeance "delayed to the last judgment" — which Tom's PRS-form record of Wright's inaugurated eschatology (PRS-03) frames as the deferral of final judgment to the end, leaving the present as the time for restoration. Tom's PRS-form record of Wright's resurrection scholarship (PRS-04) and of Rohr's Universal Christ (PRS-03) push hardest here: the cross is victory through *non-retaliation* — "a lamb slain" who "went silent … to the slaughter," exactly the figure Aquinas invokes to bar clerics from killing (a.4) — and resurrection, not execution, is the Christian answer to death. The synthesis takes these as openly normative theological resources, strong in field, that weight the whole-part logic toward absorbing rather than excising the violence of the part.

Double effect (a.7) is where the contemporary frame *confirms and formalizes* Aquinas. His distinction — the act is licensed by the *intention* of self-preservation, the aggressor's death following *praeter intentionem* and bounded by proportionality — maps onto Tom's PRS-form record of Friston's work: PRS-04 and PRS-16 make *intention* the agent's generative-model target and prior preference, distinct from the side-effects the model tolerates. Double effect is then the difference between what the policy *aims at* (self-preservation) and what it *foresees but does not target* (the aggressor's death). Tom's PRS-form record of Carroll's compatibilism (PRS-04) secures the realism this requires: intention is "a useful high-level description of physical processes," a real agentive pattern, so the aim/side-effect distinction tracks something real and can bear moral weight. The same resources handle culpable omission (a.8): if action is policy-selection under active inference, then *failing to remove* a foreseeable hazard is itself a policy with consequences — "he who does not remove something whence homicide results … is in a sense guilty of voluntary homicide." Omission is causation when the agent's policy could have prevented the outcome. And suicide (a.5) follows from corporate substance: Tom's PRS-form record of Stump's work (PRS-14, High — a hylomorphic metaphysics of corporate substance; PRS-15 is the narrower body-of-Christ record and is not cited for this) makes the person a real part of a real whole, so self-destruction is injury to the whole — and, on the central claim, the refusal of a perspective that belongs to the whole rather than to itself alone.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The proposed parallel preserves Aquinas's deep structure while marking two departures. *Preserved*: the common good is real, the authority to take life is strictly limited to the public whole (never the private individual, a.3), double effect is sound (a.7), omission can be culpable (a.8), and suicide is a wrong against the whole (a.5). *Reframed, sharply*: a.1's flat ranking of beings gives way to *graded regard* tracking graded agency (Levin) — the clearest break from Aquinas in the treatise. *Reframed, by his own lights*: the whole-over-part logic of a.2, read through the biology Aquinas's "diseased member" image invokes, bends toward *re-integration over excision* (Levin's cancer therapy; Day 149's restitution), and the theological resources (Wright's cross and resurrection; Rohr's Universal Christ) weight it decisively toward non-retaliation — pushing Aquinas's own "let the wicked live" reservation from the margin to the center. Where the evidence is firm: agency-at-scale (empirical at the biological level); double effect as intention-vs-side-effect (formally clean). Where it is speculative: the normative leap from graded agency to graded moral regard (philosophical, marked); the analogy between cellular re-integration and the rehabilitation of offenders (interpretive). Where it is openly theological: that non-retaliation and resurrection, not excision, are the final word — carried on Wright and Rohr, not smuggled as empirical.

The measure against Aquinas: this is the episode where the synthesis most diverges. It judges that a.1's instrumental ranking depended on a flat hierarchy the emerging paradigm overturns, and that a.2's execution conclusion — already qualified by Aquinas — is further pressured toward rehabilitation by the very biology and theology that frame the rest of the treatise. It judges that double effect and the limits on private killing are firmer than ever. The conclusion is not that Aquinas is wrong about the common good, but that the common good, rightly understood through agency-at-scale and the cross, seeks to *restore* the part wherever it can rather than to cut it away.

## The central-theme thread

This is the hardest case for the central claim, and it belongs at the close of the treatise's gravest question. If conscious agents are individuated by the limits of their perspective, and the telos of all is the loving unity in which "every limited perspective is contained in the whole," then to end a perspective's life is the gravest possible defection from that telos — the permanent removal of one of the very perspectives the whole is meant to include. The whole-over-part logic that runs through the justice treatise reaches its limit here: a body may defend its members against a part that is destroying them, but the goal is never excision for its own sake; it is the restoration of the part to the whole wherever restoration is possible. The cross is where this becomes explicit — the figure Aquinas himself invokes to forbid the cleric to kill: the whole absorbing the violence of the part rather than excising the part, "vengeance delayed to the last judgment" so that the present remains the time of re-integration. "Working out salvation in fear and trembling" reaches, at the edge of life and death, its most demanding form: the refusal to write any perspective permanently out of the whole that is meant to hold them all.

## Where this leaves us

The treatise's gravest question — the taking of life — has been read with Aquinas where his structure holds (the common good, the limits on killing, double effect, culpable omission) and against him where the emerging paradigm pulls hardest (the flat ranking of beings; excision over re-integration). Tomorrow (Day 151, Q.65 closing into Q.66) Aquinas turns to *theft and robbery* — injustice in external possessions, where the question of private property opened on Day 146 (the "right of nations" discerned by reason) returns as the matter of commutative justice. The restitution-and-re-integration theme that has run from Day 149 through today carries forward: the right ordering of possessions, and its repair when violated, is the next face of rendering to each what is the whole's to apportion and the part's to hold.
