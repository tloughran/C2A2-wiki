# Day 307 — You Made It (Contemporary Parallel)

## Frame

The last episode of the *Summa in a Year* gathers the remaining eschatology into one survey: Q.99 on God's mercy and justice toward the damned, Appendix 1 on the souls who depart this life *with original sin only* (the limbo of the unbaptized — natural happiness without the beatific vision) and on the souls *expiating actual sin or its punishment in purgatory*, and Appendix 2's two articles on purgatory itself. Aquinas, who began the whole *Summa* with God as the principle from whom all things proceed, ends it by mapping the states in which rational creatures finally come to rest: the fixed loss of the damned, the natural rest of those in limbo, the temporary purifying fire of purgatory ordered toward the vision, and behind them all the glory of the blessed already treated. The closing words of the podcast match the closing words of the work — a hope to meet "by the grace of God... in heaven."

The contemporary version of the question is the question this whole project has been circling: *what is the structure of the return?* If agents are individuated by the limits of their perspective, what does it mean for a limited perspective to come, finally, to rest — and are there genuinely different *kinds* of rest?

## Problem

Three linked inquiry-problems surface in the appendices and persist into the contemporary frame.

First, **the gradation problem**: Aquinas refuses a binary. Between the fixed loss of the damned and the full vision of the blessed he inserts *limbo* (a complete natural happiness that nonetheless lacks the supernatural vision) and *purgatory* (an incomplete state still in motion toward the vision). The contemporary question is whether final states are really graded — whether there is a real difference between a perspective *complete on its own terms* and a perspective *united to the whole*.

Second, **the purgatory problem**: what is purgatory *for*? Aquinas's dominant frame is *satispassion* — the paying-off of a remaining debt of temporal punishment. But the same data can be read as *formation*: the perspective being made fit to bear a vision it could not yet sustain. Which reading is right matters for the whole shape of the return.

Third, **the completion problem** — the one the finale forces. If the telos is union with the whole, does reaching it *erase* the individual perspective (so that "you" do not, in the end, make it) or *fulfill* it (so that "you made it" is literally true of you)? This is the question on which the project's entire account of individuation stands or falls.

## Resource

On the completion problem, the lead resource is the convergence the network has already registered as CROSS-005: love/union as a *fundamental category* rather than a derived feeling, drawn together across Fredrickson (positivity resonance as a measurable between-persons event), Stump (union as the highest good), and Kastrup (individuation as dissociation within one consciousness). Read against the finale, CROSS-005 lets us state the telos precisely: the end is not the dissolution of the alter back into undifferentiated consciousness but the *re-integration of a specifically-patterned* perspective — Kastrup's Daimon (P8 in the Kastrup-tradition wiki) names exactly the claim that the individual is not generic, so that what is united to the whole is *this* perspective, with its signature intact. That is the resource that answers "does 'you' make it?" with *yes*: union fulfils rather than erases.

On the purgatory problem, Wright supplies the decisive reframe. The God's-homecoming salvation frame (Wright-tradition wiki, PROP-2026-05-31-002) reads salvation as *incorporation into the people among whom God comes to dwell*, and the new-creation reframe (PROP-2026-05-31-001) reads the resurrection not as a law-violating miracle but as the firstfruits of new creation breaking into the old. Both push purgatory away from a penal ledger and toward *formation*: what is being completed is fitness for life in a renewed creation, the capacity to bear a vision and a community one could not yet sustain. Rohr's relation-is-basal claim and his non-dual practice (Rohr-tradition wiki) press the same direction — "how do I get to heaven" is "a question of the ego, not of the Trinity"; purgation is the un-clenching of the boundary so that the prior belonging can be received with open hands.

On the gradation problem, Kastrup's dissociative-boundary granularity (P14 in the Kastrup-tradition wiki) and Carroll's emergence taxonomy (P11 in the Carroll-tradition wiki) together give us the vocabulary: final states differ in the *porosity* of the boundary — sealed (the damned, treated yesterday), intact-but-natural (limbo: a perspective whose boundary is healthy and complete on its own terms, but not opened to the whole), and *being-made-porous* (purgatory: the boundary in process of opening). Carroll's discipline reminds us these are meaning-register stories, not measured states — but the gradation is structurally coherent, not a muddle.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The contemporary-parallel response: **the return is graded, and purgatory is formation, not payment — the perspective that keeps computing past death, being progressively widened until it can bear the vision of the whole; and the union it reaches fulfils the individual perspective rather than erasing it.** Limbo, on this reading, is the genuinely intelligible category of a perspective complete in its *natural* good — at rest on its own terms — yet not opened to the supernatural whole; not a failure, but a completeness of a lesser scope. Heaven is the limit-case where all limitation is removed and, as the project's central claim has it, "the simple whole is seen and every limited perspective is contained in it."

What this shares with Aquinas: the refusal of a binary, the reality of an intermediate purification, and above all the conviction that the end is *vision and union*, not annihilation. What it reframes: purgatory's grammar shifts from debt to formation — from *satispassion* to becoming-fit — which is more continuous with the "fear and trembling" of the whole earthly computation (we are being formed now; the formation simply is not finished at death for most). Where the evidence is *firm*: that love/union is a real category with measurable embodied correlates (Fredrickson), and that perspectives are individuated and can be more or less porous, is well-supported. Where it is *speculative*: that the computation *continues* past biological death, and that its terminus is a vision containing all perspectives, is theological — Wright's resurrection argument is strong *as history* but its metaphysics is contested, and the cosmic scope of the union is Rohr's faith-claim, not a result. The synthesis should hold the hope without smuggling the confidence.

## The central-theme thread

For a year this project has been writing one synthesis-in-progress: conscious agents are individuated by the limitations of their perspective; loving unity is the goal toward which each limited perspective computes, under scarce resources, "in fear and trembling." The finale is where the *reditus* — the return — is named outright. The damned (yesterday) are the perspective that stopped computing. Limbo is the perspective that completed a smaller computation and rested there. Purgatory is the perspective still computing past death, being purified until it can hold the vision. And heaven is convergence: the point at which limitation is removed and every partial view is contained in the simple whole, *without ceasing to be itself*. "You made it" is therefore not sentimental. It is the literal completion of the computation the whole series has described — the limited perspective arriving, by grace and by formation and by the guidance of those who saw more of the whole, at the loving unity that was its end all along. That the individual perspective is *fulfilled* and not erased in that arrival is the load-bearing wager of the project, and it is the note on which Aquinas, and the podcast, choose to end.

## Where this leaves us

This is the end of the *Summa* and the end of the year. The work began with God as the principle of all things and ends with the creature's return to God; the contemporary parallel has tried, across 307 days, to show that the *exitus–reditus* arc maps onto a defensible mind-first claim about individuation and the computation toward loving unity — speculative where it must be, empirical where it can be, theological where the *Summa* itself is theological. The open thread that outlasts the series is the one the central theme always carried: if the return fulfils rather than erases the perspective, then the work of *informing one another* — of widening each limited view toward the whole — is not preparation for the end but a participation in it already begun. You made it; and the making was the point.
