# Day 268 — Excommunication (Contemporary Parallel)

## Frame

Aquinas (Suppl. Q.21-22) defines *excommunication* as separation from the communion of the Church — chiefly from the sacraments and the general suffrages of the faithful — defends its *congruity* as medicinal rather than merely punitive, names its *cause* (grave, contumacious sin), and asks *who* can impose it (those holding jurisdiction, by the power of the keys). The major excommunication cuts the offending member from the visible body so that shame may bring amendment; the model text is Paul's in 1 Corinthians 5, "deliver this man to Satan… that his spirit may be saved." The contemporary version under mind / conscious-realist monism: what is it for a collective agent to *cut off* one of its own members, and how can severance be ordered to the member's restoration rather than his ruin?

## Problem

The shared inquiry-problem: **how does a whole respond to a part that has set its own goal against the whole's — and can the response be drawn so that exclusion serves reintegration?** Aquinas's answer is the medicinal cut. The contemporary question is whether there is a principled, non-arbitrary account of when a whole should sever a defecting member, and of how severance can be healing rather than destructive.

## Resource

Levin's program supplies the leading move — and it is unusually exact here. In Tom's PRS-form record of Levin's work, PRS-02 frames *cancer as defection*: a cell that drops out of the body's collective morphogenetic goals and reverts to its own unicellular agenda, no longer answering to the whole's bioelectric field. PRS-11 sharpens this as the body's *dissociative* pathology — a sub-region whose identity has split off from the organism's. Excommunication, read through this lens, is the collective agent's recognition and ratification of a defection that has *already occurred*: the contumacious member has decoupled from the body's shared goals; the Church's act names the decoupling and marks the boundary it has crossed. Crucially, Levin's clinical interest is not in destroying defected cells but in *normalizing* them — restoring the field's signaling so the rogue cell re-couples. That is precisely the medicinal reading: the cut is drawn toward the member's return.

Friston frames the medicinal/punitive distinction. In Tom's PRS-form record of Friston's work, PRS-18 (ZBS band-width) treats a community's standing options for out-of-model evidence as a tunable parameter: a whole can *assimilate* a deviation (update toward it) or *repel* it (act to exclude). Healthy excommunication is the bounded, reversible repulsion that holds the door open — a boundary drawn to provoke a belief-update in the member — as against a brittle expulsion that simply discards him. PRS-22 keeps the boundary real: a collective that sustains a group-level Markov blanket — a statistical boundary held by the whole, distinct from the blankets of its members — is one whose membership line is a real dividing surface, so severance genuinely alters the member's coupling to the whole rather than merely relabelling him.

Wright grounds the medicinal aim in the founding text: 1 Corinthians 5 hands the offender over precisely "so that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord" — Pauline excommunication is corrective discipline within the covenant community, not erasure from it. Rohr guards the limit: in Tom's PRS-form record of Rohr's work, PRS-03 states the Universal Christ as the divine pattern present in all of creation and *not confined to any single institutional expression* — so no ecclesial act can sever a person from the Christ present in all reality; excommunication is discipline-within-belonging, a boundary inside love, never exclusion from it. That the cut removes the member from the Body's *suffrages and sacraments* — a real spiritual privation — is theological, as is the claim that an unjust excommunication does not bind the soul.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The contemporary-parallel response: **excommunication is a collective agent's medicinal response to a member that has decoupled from the whole's shared goals — the body's ratification of a defection (Levin PRS-02) or dissociation (PRS-11), drawn so that the severance provokes re-coupling rather than loss (Friston PRS-18).** This *shares* with Aquinas the definition as separation from communion, the medicinal-not-punitive congruity, contumacious sin as cause, and jurisdiction as the condition of imposing it. It *reframes* the act as naming an already-present decoupling and as a bounded, reversible repulsion ordered to the member's return — continuous with how a body responds to a defecting cell. It *firms up* that severance can be genuinely healing, with a clinical analogue (normalization, not ablation), and that the medicinal intent is the founding Pauline intent. It *marks* that the *spiritual* privation of the sacraments and suffrages, and the non-effect of an unjust sentence, are theological; the resource explains the *form* of medicinal exclusion, not the grace withheld or restored.

## The central-theme thread

Excommunication is the dark side of the central claim. Perspective-limitation individuates agents; but a limited perspective can *harden* — a part can set its own goal against the whole and refuse the guidance that would orient it toward loving unity. The cancer analogy is sobering and precise: defection is a cell perceiving only its own local resources and computing for itself alone, against the body. The whole's response, when rightly drawn, is not destruction but a painful boundary meant to break the isolation — to make the decoupled member feel the loss of communion and re-couple. "Salvation in fear and trembling" includes this: the community's hardest discipline is still ordered to inclusion, the cut still aimed at the return.
