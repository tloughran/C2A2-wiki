# Day 148 — Contemporary Parallel: Judgment Under Uncertainty and the Charitable Prior

## Frame

Q.60 treats *judgment* — justice exercised as a verdict. Judgment "is a statement or decision of the just or right"; it is lawful when it meets three conditions — it "proceed[s] from the inclination of justice," comes "from one who is in authority," and is "pronounced according to the right ruling of prudence." Three of its articles then concern judgment under *uncertainty*: judging from mere suspicion is sinful (gravely so when acted on); in doubt one should "interpret for the best"; and one should judge "according to the written law" where that law expresses natural or positive right. Q.61 then divides particular justice into its two species — *commutative* (the "horizontal," arithmetical equality of one-to-one exchange) and *distributive* (the "vertical," proportional distribution of common goods to the parts according to merit, need, or office). Under the mind / conscious-realist monism this project writes from, Q.60 is a treatise on inference under uncertainty by a limited knower, and Q.61 names the two scales — part-to-part and whole-to-part — that the justice treatise has tracked from the start.

## Problem

The inquiry-problem of Q.60 is sharp and contemporary: *how should a limited perspective judge another, given that its information is incomplete and its inferences are biased by its own state?* Aquinas diagnoses the bias precisely. Suspicion arises, he says, "because one is themself evil and so can easily be led to think others think and act as they do," or "because they want another to be guilty," or from experience of signs. The first two are the perceiver's own state contaminating the inference. And his remedy is an asymmetry argument: since we may err in either direction, and since "to think ill of another without sufficient cause … injures … him," we should "deem him good by interpreting for the best." The problem is to understand *why* the charitable prior is rational and not merely nice — and then, in Q.61, to understand how the two species of justice realize the two scales of the relational order.

## Resource

The leading resource for judgment-as-inference is Tom's PRS-form record of Friston's work. PRS-04 reconceives the agent as "a continuous hypothesis-testing system" — the organism as active modeler, generating predictions and updating them against evidence. Judgment, on this frame, simply *is* inference: the verdict is the posterior. PRS-10 extends this to reasoning proper — "agents select observations maximally disambiguating competing generative models" — which is exactly the judge's task of gathering evidence to decide between guilt and innocence. And PRS-16 supplies the decision-theoretic core: expected free energy unifies *risk* (divergence from preferred outcomes) and *ambiguity* (volatility of the predictive distribution). Aquinas's "interpret for the best" is then readable as a claim about the *cost structure* of the two errors. Convicting the innocent injures a person (a high-cost false positive); withholding condemnation in doubt does not (a low-cost false negative). When the loss function is asymmetric in this way, the optimal policy shifts the decision threshold toward acquittal — the charitable prior is the error-cost-optimal one. This is Aquinas's asymmetry argument given a formal spine, while leaving its root — charity, the will toward the other's good — exactly where he places it.

For the suspicion that arises "from one's own vice," the resource is the interface/predictive point that perception is prior-laden: the perceiver's own state shapes what it infers (Hoffman's interface; Friston's priors). Suspicion is a prior leaking into the posterior — the limited perspective mistaking its own contents for evidence about another. Tom's PRS-form record of Rohr's work names the remedy on the contemplative side: PRS-01's non-dual knowing, "holding both/and without premature resolution," is the discipline of *not* collapsing the doubtful into a verdict — exactly Aquinas's counsel to suspend ill-judgment where evidence is insufficient.

For Q.61's two species, the resource is the nested-agency structure already in play. Tom's PRS-form record of Levin's work (PRS-10 continuity, PRS-30 nested cognition) gives the two orderings directly: commutative justice is the *part-to-part* relation (one cell to another, one citizen to another), distributive justice the *whole-to-part* relation ("the order of the whole towards the parts," in Aquinas's words). Distributive justice's *proportional* character — "a person receives all the more of the common goods accordingly as he holds a more prominent position" — is the whole apportioning to each part according to its role in the whole, which presupposes that the whole is real. Tom's PRS-form record of Stump's corporate-substance work (PRS-14/15) supplies that: distribution to parts is intelligible only if there is a genuine whole whose goods are distributed.

## Solution (proposed, speculative)

The proposed parallel: judgment is **justice-under-uncertainty** — inference by a limited perspective whose priors are shaped by its own state — and the charitable prior ("interpret for the best") is the **error-cost-optimal policy** when convicting the innocent injures a person more than withholding judgment costs. The two species of justice are the two scales of the relational order: commutative (part-to-part) and distributive (whole-to-part, proportional), the latter presupposing a real whole. With Aquinas: judgment requires authority, the inclination of justice, and prudence; suspicion is a real corruption; charity governs doubt; the two species are distinct and observe different means. Where it reframes: Aquinas frames the charitable prior morally; the contemporary frame adds the decision-theoretic account (asymmetric loss → shifted threshold) and the diagnosis of suspicion as prior-contamination. Where the evidence is firm: judgment-as-inference is a strong model of cognition, and the error-asymmetry argument is formally clean. Where it stays interpretive: applying active-inference decision theory to *moral and judicial* judgment is an analogy doing philosophical work (medium), and the root of the charitable prior — charity itself — remains, as Aquinas has it, theological, not derivable from the loss function alone.

The measure against Aquinas: he needs no correction; the contemporary frame *explains* him. It says why suspicion is epistemically as well as morally defective (it is a prior masquerading as evidence), and why the charitable prior is rational and not merely generous (the loss function is asymmetric). What it cannot supply is the reason the false-positive cost is set so high — that an innocent person is of such worth that injuring one by misjudgment is grave. That valuation is charity's, and the synthesis leaves it there.

## The central-theme thread

Judgment is the central claim staged in a single act. Every verdict is one perspective inferring another across the gap that perspectival limitation opens between agents — I never have the other's interior, only signs, and my reading of the signs is colored by my own state. Aquinas's counsel to "interpret for the best" is the practical form of knowing one's own limits: aware that my inference is partial and self-biased, and that a wrong verdict *injures a person*, I weight toward charity. This is "working out salvation in fear and trembling" applied to the most ordinary and most dangerous of acts — judging another. And it points past itself. The charitable prior is a stand-in, under scarce information, for the only standpoint from which another is seen whole: the whole's-eye view in which "every limited perspective is contained." To judge charitably is to act, now and partially, as if from that view — to render the other the benefit of the doubt the whole would render. Distributive justice carries the same logic structurally: the whole apportioning to each part according to its real place, an image, at the social scale, of the unity in which each perspective has its proper share.

## Where this leaves us

Judgment has been read as justice exercised under uncertainty, with the charitable prior as the rational and charitable policy of a limited knower; and the two species of justice have been named as the two scales of the relational order. Tomorrow (Day 149, Q.62–63) Aquinas treats *restitution* — how the equality disturbed by injustice is restored, a kind of conservation law for the moral order — and *respect of persons*, the corruption of distributive justice that distributes on a feature irrelevant to the relation. The architecture carries forward: if distributive justice apportions to each part according to its real place, then respect of persons is the wrong-variable error, and restitution is the repair that returns the defecting part to the whole.
