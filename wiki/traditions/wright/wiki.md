## Research Program Overview
**Thinker:** N.T. Wright (University of St Andrews, emeritus)
**Core claim:** The biblical narrative is a five-act drama (creation, fall, Israel, Jesus, church) in which the present community is called to improvise faithfully within the unfinished final act; critical realism provides an epistemology that avoids both naive positivism and postmodern relativism; the resurrection of Jesus is a historical event with cosmological implications
**Paradigm:** Critical realism, narrative theology, new perspective on Paul, inaugurated eschatology, faithful improvisation

**Summary (from RC Pilot document):**

N.T. Wright is the tradition's narrative theologian and historian — the thinker who gives the project a model of how a community inhabits and extends a story it did not originate. His critical realism stakes out a precise epistemological position between Carroll's poetic naturalism and Kastrup's idealism: reality exists independently of our knowing it, but all knowing is perspectival, tradition-embedded, and revisable. That places him close to MacIntyre's tradition-constituted rationality while adding an explicitly historical and narrative dimension that MacIntyre's more formal account leaves implicit.

His most distinctive contribution is the five-act hermeneutic. If the biblical story is an unfinished drama — creation, fall, Israel, Jesus, church, with the final act still open — then the task of the present community is not to repeat the past but to *improvise faithfully*: to act consistently with the story's trajectory while responding to circumstances the original authors could not have anticipated. This maps with unusual directness onto the project's own method. Each agent inherits a thinker's research program and must extend it into new territory — cross-program connections, novel proposals — while remaining faithful to that program's internal logic. "Faithful improvisation" is, in effect, the job description of every tradition agent in the system.

Wright's work on Paul and his concept of inaugurated eschatology — the decisive event has already occurred, but its full implications are still unfolding — gives the project a temporal model for how research programs mature. The founding insight may already be present in the early texts, but its significance becomes visible only through patient elaboration and cross-traditional encounter, which is precisely the timescale the detector is meant to compress.

The questions his program raises are methodological and testable: does the five-act hermeneutic actually provide a workable model for how AI agents should extend the programs they inherit; is critical realism a near-relation of Friston's active inference, where inference inside a generative model is a formal version of perspectival-but-real knowing; can inaugurated eschatology serve as a model for program development; and where exactly does his account of resurrection as a historical event with cosmological significance challenge — or merely sit athwart — Carroll's physicalism. His recorded advances are the epistemological ones: that history and theology can be methodologically integrated, and that critical realism offers a genuine middle way between positivism and relativism. He connects to MacIntyre (his five-act hermeneutic as a narrative application of tradition-constituted rationality), Rohr (already/not-yet alongside order-disorder-reorder), Carroll (a direct, evidence-serious disagreement about whether the evidence permits claims exceeding naturalism), Stump (historically grounded theology engaging analytic philosophy on its own terms), and Loughran (the project's faithful-improvisation methodology as a direct application of his hermeneutic).
