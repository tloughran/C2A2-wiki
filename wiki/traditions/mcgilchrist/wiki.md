## Research Program Overview
**Thinker:** Iain McGilchrist (Psychiatrist, neuroscientist, literary scholar)
**Core claim:** Left hemisphere re-presents known fragments; right hemisphere attends to the living whole; modernity suffers catastrophic left-hemisphere dominance; attention is world-constituting
**Paradigm:** Divided brain, relational ontology, attention as ontological act, embodied cognition

**Summary (from RC Pilot document):**

Iain McGilchrist is the tradition's phenomenologist and neurologist of culture, whose divided-brain theory supplies the empirical foundation for the project's insistence on relational ontology over analytic fragmentation. His core argument in *The Master and His Emissary* (2009) is that the right hemisphere perceives context, relation, and the living whole, while the left hemisphere abstracts, categorizes, and manipulates — and that the left hemisphere's operations are parasitic on what the right hemisphere first discloses. The right hemisphere is the master; the left its emissary, which in modernity has usurped control. The diagnosis is not metaphor but measurable hemispheric asymmetry in cognitive style, confirmed across his clinical and literary corpus.

This has direct methodological consequences for the C2A2 project. Genuine engagement with a rival tradition requires right-hemisphere modes of attention — inhabiting the whole before analyzing the parts — which is precisely what "second-first-language" competence demands. McGilchrist's corpus-callosotomy evidence also does double duty: split-brain patients exhibit two independent conscious agents that integrate when reconnected, giving neurological support to Conscious Realism's mathematical claim that multiple conscious agents can compose a single unified self.

His more recent Sophia Lectures (Ralston College, 2026) push the program further. Synthesis is not the addition of divided parts but a "union at a higher level" that produces a third thing qualitatively beyond its components — a claim the project reads as a possible neurological twin of Levin's morphogenetic integration, where coordinated cells produce a whole no single cell contains. The same lectures treat paradox not as logical failure but as ontological depth, marking genuine asymmetric relations between levels of reality — a stance the project sets beside Wolfram's observer-dependent sampling of the Ruliad as two routes to the co-presence of the finite and the infinite.

The open questions this program is generating run from the empirical to the architectural: is left-hemisphere dominance measurable at a *cultural* level; does right-hemisphere engagement ground all propositional knowledge; can McGilchrist's attention theory be formalized in terms compatible with Friston's precision-weighted prediction; and what institutional practices cultivate right-hemisphere openness inside academic discourse — a question that bears directly on how the C2A2 communities should be run, not just theorized. In the architecture of the project, McGilchrist imports a cognitive and neurological foundation for the relational ontology that the Conscious Realism cluster (Hoffman, Levin, Friston) theorizes mathematically, and he names the failure mode — decontextualized abstraction — that the contemplative voices (Rohr) and the narrative voices (Wright, MacIntyre) are each trying, in their own registers, to remedy.
