## Research Program Overview
**Thinker:** Richard Rohr, OFM (Center for Action and Contemplation)
**Core claim:** Reality operates at multiple levels simultaneously; non-dualistic thinking — the capacity to hold contradictions without resolving them prematurely — is the hallmark of mature consciousness; the contemplative tradition offers an epistemology that complements and exceeds rationalist analysis
**Paradigm:** Contemplative epistemology, non-dual awareness, the Universal Christ, order-disorder-reorder, levels of consciousness development

**Summary (from RC Pilot document):**

Richard Rohr, OFM, is the tradition's contemplative epistemologist — the voice insisting that the C2A2 project cannot succeed through analysis alone. His central contribution is the recovery of contemplative knowing as a legitimate and necessary mode of inquiry, distinct from both scientific empiricism and philosophical argument. Where McGilchrist *diagnoses* the left hemisphere's tyranny of decontextualized abstraction, Rohr *prescribes* the remedy: a practice-based return to non-dual awareness, the capacity to perceive unity-in-distinction rather than collapsing reality into either/or categories.

His order-disorder-reorder framework maps closely onto MacIntyre's epistemological crisis. Traditions begin in a received order, pass through the disorder of internal contradiction and external challenge, and — if they are living — emerge into a deeper reorder that preserves what was true in the original while transcending its limits. Stated in this register, the contemplative tradition and the philosophy of inquiry are describing the same movement from two sides, which is exactly the kind of cross-traditional rhyme the detector is built to surface.

Rohr's theology of the Universal Christ — the claim that the divine pattern is not confined to one tradition but is present throughout reality — supplies a theological warrant for the project's multi-tradition architecture. If truth is genuinely universal, then encountering it simultaneously through Levin's biology, Friston's mathematics, and Stump's Thomism is not eclecticism but a contemplative practice of perceiving the same pattern at different scales. His Franciscan emphasis on incarnation and embodiment also connects to Fredrickson's positivity resonance: love is not an abstraction but a biological and spiritual event that occurs between embodied persons.

The questions this program generates are sharp precisely because they test the limits of formalization: can a non-dual epistemology be formalized at all, or does formalization necessarily distort it; is contemplative knowing a distinct epistemic mode or a special case of McGilchrist's right-hemisphere attention; how does order-disorder-reorder relate to Friston's surprise-update-equilibrium cycle; and can the contemplative stance — holding contradictions without premature resolution — be operationalized as an actual methodology for cross-program analysis rather than remaining a private disposition. His recorded advances are modest and honest about their status: that there is a mode of knowing beyond dualistic analysis (corroborated by McGilchrist's hemispheric research), and that the order-disorder-reorder arc credibly describes intellectual development (mapped onto MacIntyre and Kuhn, at medium confidence). He connects to McGilchrist (right-hemisphere knowing), Stump (a complementary Catholic intellectual tradition that also makes union the highest good), Fredrickson (embodied love as the substrate of spiritual encounter), MacIntyre (the contemplative dimension his rationalism lacks), and Kastrup (Universal Christ and universal consciousness as possibly one claim in two registers).
