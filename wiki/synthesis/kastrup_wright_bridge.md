## Why these sit at the intersection

Wright's central methodological question — "what does it mean that X *really happened*?" — is the exact question Kastrup's analytic idealism reframes. Under idealism, "physical event" is not a brute mind-independent fact but a representation on the dashboard of a dissociated alter; so "what is real" and "what really happened" are already ontologically loaded.

- **Wright** is a *critical realist*: there is a real, knowable past, but our access to it is mediated by genre, perspective, and interpretive framework — neither flat literalism nor mythologizing dismissal.
- **Kastrup** is an *idealist realist*: there is a real mind-at-large, but the physical world (including historical events) is its appearance across interfaces.

Both reject naive materialism *and* anti-realism. Wright's "transformed physicality" reading of the resurrection (PRS-CANDIDATE-02) is the seam: a resurrection that is real-but-not-resuscitation is precisely the kind of event whose "physicality" an idealist can affirm without committing to physicalist substance — transformed matter as a new mode of appearance.

## Synthesis claim

**Wright's genre-aware critical realism supplies the C2A2 network's mind-monist members with a disciplined, non-naive account of what "really happened" means for events (like the resurrection) that resist both literalist and dismissive readings — and his "transformed physicality" thesis is a candidate bridge concept for affirming the *reality* of such events while leaving the *substance* question open to idealist reframing. Critical realism (Wright) and idealist realism (Kastrup) converge on the methodological point that "real" is not the same as "mind-independent material fact," and diverge on whether the residual reality is historical-and-physical-but-genre-mediated or mental-and-represented. The pairing gives the syntheses a way to handle resurrection/new-creation claims without forcing a choice between flat historicity and metaphor.**
