## Does the interface alter what can be seen from inside a tradition?

**Orphaned page at the intersection:** `inbox/proposals/pending/2026-09-16_kastrup_headless-way-richard-lang.md` (PROP-2026-09-16-003, 0 backlinks). Member-gated, not heard; the item below comes from a chapter title and is offered as a question, not a claim.

**Why it sits here:** Chapter 48:00 of Kastrup's session with Richard Lang asks whether glasses — and specifically **AI smart glasses** — "get in the way" of first-person seeing. It is the only point in a two-hour session on the Headless Way where AI mediation of perception is on the agenda, and it arrives inside a tradition whose entire method is that a few minutes of direct first-person experiment beats years of reading.

**Synthesis claim.** The question generalizes to the accelerator's own premise in a way the project should be able to answer in its own voice rather than waiting to be handed. C2A2's instrument runs traditions through AI agents: an agent retrieves for a tradition, speaks in its idiom, and reports what the tradition says about a question. The Turing framing asks whether the agent is a competent *speaker* of the tradition. Chapter 48:00 points at a different property. **An agent could be a fully competent speaker and a systematically distorted observer at the same time, and the two are not distinguishable from outside.** If the interface through which a tradition is encountered shapes what can be seen from inside it, then fluency is evidence of fluency and nothing more — which is exactly the situation Harding's programme is built to expose, since its claim is that *what you say about* the first person and *what you see from* it come apart reliably.

This is not the familiar worry about hallucination or about training-data coverage. Those are failures the network already tests for and could in principle fix with better retrieval. This one survives perfect retrieval: a tradition's interior may contain things that are only available to a perspective that *occupies* it, and a system that only ever reports about traditions has no way to find out whether that is true. The honest position is that the accelerator's instrument is silent on its own blind spot, and that the traditions most likely to insist on this — contemplative ones, Rohr's and the Headless Way's alike — are the ones whose claims the instrument will systematically under-represent.

**Open question the wiki cannot yet answer:** Is there any test, conducted through the instrument, that would detect the instrument's own limitation of this kind? A test designed inside the interface cannot see past it, which is the structure of the problem. The only obvious route is external: find a claim a tradition makes that practitioners can verify and non-practitioners provably cannot, run the agent at it, and check. Whether such a claim exists in a form the network could score is itself unsettled, and worth more attention than the chapter title that prompted it.
