## Summary
Rohr distinguishes pain from suffering: pain is the physical or circumstantial discomfort itself, while suffering is what we add through resistance, denial, and a sense that the pain is unjust or wrong. On that distinction he reads the cross as Jesus's voluntary acceptance of undeserved pain as an act of solidarity with the pain of the world, and argues that a God who participates in human suffering rather than merely tolerating or observing it "changes everything" for anyone willing to look contemplatively. He closes by contrasting Christianity as a transformational system with Christianity as a belonging system.

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
It supplies a sharper, more operational version of a claim the wiki already holds from Rohr in several looser forms — that suffering is transformative — by naming the specific mechanism (resistance to pain, not pain itself) and by tying courage to its etymology, *cor-agere*, an action of the heart. It also gives an explicit statement of the participatory-God claim that his cruciform/coincidence-of-opposites material assumes but rarely states directly.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-01:
  Problem: Contemplative and theological accounts of suffering routinely collapse pain and suffering into one category, which makes "redemptive suffering" sound like an endorsement of harm and leaves no way to say which part of an affliction is optional.
  Resource: A two-term decomposition — pain as the given discomfort, suffering as the added layer produced by resistance, denial, and the judgment that the pain is unjust — with the corollary that pain is unavoidable for humans while suffering is usually not.
  Solution: Relocates the transformative work from the affliction to the response, so that spiritual practice targets the resistance rather than seeking or valorizing the pain. Gives a testable pastoral criterion: if a practice does not reduce resistance, it is not doing the work Rohr claims for it.
  Confidence: High
  Evidence: Rohr writes that pain is physical discomfort but suffering "comes from our resistance, denial, and sense of injustice or wrongness about that pain," and adds that pain is the rent we pay for being human while suffering is usually optional.

PRS-CANDIDATE-02:
  Problem: If God is impassible — outside suffering, observing it — then solidarity with the suffering is a human imitation of something God does not do, and the cross is a transaction rather than a disclosure.
  Resource: The "crucified God" read as revealing that God is on the side of suffering wherever it is found, including on both sides of any war and among oppressors as well as victims.
  Solution: Converts solidarity from imitation into participation: the disciple does not perform solidarity alongside a detached God but is taken into an act God is already performing. Rohr is explicit that this reading pleases very few people, which functions as a falsifier — a version of the claim that comforted every party would not be this one.
  Confidence: High
  Evidence: Rohr's conditional — if God is participating in human suffering rather than passively tolerating and observing it, that changes everything — placed against his claim that the cross was voluntary acceptance of undeserved suffering as total solidarity with the world's pain.

PRS-CANDIDATE-03:
  Problem: Religious traditions have no clean way to distinguish membership from transformation, so belonging can be mistaken for change indefinitely.
  Resource: The belonging-system / transformational-system contrast, anchored to a behavioural test — whether one accepts the invitation to solidarity with the world's pain, freely, and not in order to earn love.
  Solution: Makes the distinction diagnosable rather than rhetorical, and decouples the acceptance from merit: Rohr insists the invitation is optional (yes, no, or maybe are all live) and that God's love is not contingent on the answer. Solidarity is then motivated by love returned rather than by fear of exclusion.
  Confidence: Medium
  Evidence: Rohr's claim that we are either baptized into Christ's death and resurrection or Christianity is largely a mere belonging system rather than a transformational one that will change the world, alongside his insistence that none of this is done to make God love us.

## Cross-Tradition Signals
- **Fredrickson**: Rohr's pain/suffering split is structurally the appraisal move in affective science — the aversive stimulus versus the evaluation layered on it. Fredrickson's work on positive emotions as reappraisal resources, and on resilience as recovery speed rather than absence of adversity, is the empirical sibling of Rohr's claim that suffering is the optional term. A concrete probe: does contemplative practice of the kind Rohr prescribes move the same markers (vagal tone, positivity resonance) that her loving-kindness trials move?
- **McGilchrist**: "Contemplative gazing" as the mode in which the participatory-God claim becomes visible is a right-hemisphere epistemology in McGilchrist's terms — attending to the whole, in relation, without seizing. Rohr's claim that this pleases very few people maps onto McGilchrist's account of a left-hemisphere culture that prefers a God who solves problems to one who shares them.
- **Stump**: The most direct pairing in the corpus. Stump's *Wandering in Darkness* argues that the problem of suffering is answered by second-person knowledge of persons rather than by a defence, and her Dewey-lecture material on love and forgiveness turns on union with the sufferer. Rohr's "God is always on the side of suffering wherever it is found" is the same claim in homiletic register — including the hard case, that it holds for oppressors too, which Stump's account of the goodness of forgiveness has to reach as well. Worth flagging as a candidate Rohr-Stump bridge on divine solidarity, given that MacIntyre-style virtue framings sit behind both.
- **Wright**: Rohr's belonging-versus-transformation contrast is the pastoral form of Wright's insistence that the gospel is not a private escape plan but the renewal of creation, and both cite Romans 6 territory. They diverge on emphasis: Wright grounds the change in the resurrection and new creation; Rohr grounds it in the cross and descent. That divergence is itself a usable bridge question — whether the transformative moment is Good Friday or Easter, and whether the two accounts are complementary or in tension.
- **Levin**: Weaker, but worth noting — Rohr's claim that resistance to a perturbation, not the perturbation itself, produces the damage has a formal analogue in accounts where a system's stress arises from failed error-correction rather than from the insult. Speculative; do not build on it without a real mechanism.
