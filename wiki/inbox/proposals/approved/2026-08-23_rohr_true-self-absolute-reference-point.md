## Summary
Sunday opener for CAC Week 34 ("True Self and False Self"), adapted from *Immortal Diamond* (Jossey-Bass, 2013), 1–5. Rohr's operative distinction is not between self and no-self but between two things that both *feel* like self: the true self (which he glosses as soul) and the false or egoic self. His diagnostic point is that the confusion is structural rather than culpable — both are experienced from the inside as one's own selfhood, so introspection alone cannot separate them, and "going inside" is therefore necessary but not sufficient. The load-bearing formulation is that the true self, once found, functions as an absolute reference point that is *both* utterly within the person *and* utterly beyond them at the same time. Rohr then adds an anti-solipsism clause: inner authority so discovered must be balanced against the outer authority of Scripture and tradition, and one's own experience validated against others', or the result is fantasy rather than reality. He notes explicitly that the true/false self vocabulary comes from Winnicott via Merton, not from the Christian mystical tradition directly.

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
This is the most metaphysically precise statement in the recent Rohr corpus and it bears directly on the Summa 2026 central theme (perspective-limitation individuates conscious agents; loving unity is the telos). Rohr is asserting a locus that is simultaneously indexed to the individual and not exhausted by the individual — which is exactly the structure the central theme requires if individuation-by-limitation is to be compatible with a real unity rather than a merely aspirational one. The rohr/ wiki has the Universal Christ as the *cosmic* side of that structure and nothing on its *first-person* side; this supplies it. The anti-solipsism clause is independently valuable: it is Rohr supplying his own falsification condition, which is rare in this corpus and makes the claim citable rather than merely quotable.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-A:
  Label: true-self-as-within-and-beyond
  Problem: If perspective-limitation is what individuates conscious agents, an individuated agent appears to have no non-arbitrary reference point — every standard it could appeal to is either internal (hence its own limitation restated) or external (hence not its own).
  Resource: Rohr's formulation of the true self as an absolute reference point utterly within and utterly beyond at the same time (*Immortal Diamond*, 1–5), with Catherine of Genoa and Colossians 1:27 as the tradition's own witnesses to the same structure.
  Solution: The reference point need not be either wholly internal or wholly external. Indexed-but-not-exhausted is a coherent third option, and it is precisely what makes an individuated, limited agent's convergence toward unity non-arbitrary rather than imposed.
  Confidence: High
  Evidence: Rohr states the both/and structure directly and treats the balance as the thing most religion fails to achieve — i.e. he is aware it is the hard part, not asserting it casually.

PRS-CANDIDATE-B:
  Label: introspection-underdetermines-self
  Problem: Contemplative claims are commonly dismissed on the grounds that introspective reports cannot distinguish genuine insight from ego-serving construction.
  Resource: Rohr's concession that both true and false self feel like "self," so the interior turn alone is insufficient; paired with his requirement that inner authority be balanced by outer authority and one's experience validated against others'.
  Solution: Treat the contemplative claim as *not* purely first-person by design. The tradition's own method is triangulation — inner report, textual tradition, and other practitioners' experience — which is the shape of an intersubjective validation protocol rather than a private one.
  Confidence: High
  Evidence: Both halves are stated in the source, and the validation requirement is stated as what keeps the practitioner grounded in reality rather than fantasy.

PRS-CANDIDATE-C:
  Label: winnicott-merton-provenance
  Problem: True self / false self is often presented as ancient contemplative vocabulary, which inflates its evidential standing when cited across traditions.
  Resource: Rohr's own footnote: the terms were popularised by the paediatrician and psychiatrist D. W. Winnicott (1896–1971), and Rohr recalls first meeting "true self" in Thomas Merton.
  Solution: Log the provenance in the rohr/ wiki so that any cross-tradition citation of true/false self is dated to twentieth-century developmental psychology, not to Bonaventure or the Cappadocians. This is a housekeeping entry that prevents a category of misattribution.
  Confidence: High
  Evidence: Stated by Rohr in the source's own reference note.
