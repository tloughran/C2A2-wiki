## Summary
Friday meditation of CAC Week 34 ("True Self and False Self"), adapted from Rohr's own essay "True Self, False Self," *Radical Grace* 25.4 (2012), 39–41. This is the week's constructive close and it does three things the earlier days in the week do not.

First, it grounds the true self in a doctrine rather than a phenomenology: the true self is "who you are because of the divine indwelling," cited to Romans 8:9, 1 Corinthians 3:16, Colossians 1:17–20, 1 Corinthians 15:45–49 and Ephesians 1:13–14. What happened in Christ is read as "an announcement of what is happening in all of us" — a participatory rather than substitutionary frame.

Second, it gives an explicitly *functional* redefinition of sin: "The false self is characterized by separateness. Jewish and Christian traditions call this state of disconnectedness 'sin.'" Sin is a state of relation, not an act, and the addictive search for filler follows from the emptiness that separation produces.

Third — and this is the part that cuts against a simple two-halves-of-life reading — Rohr concedes that constructing the false self is necessary and unavoidable: "We have to create a false self to get started; the trouble is that we take it far too seriously." He also declines to claim stable attainment: "That doesn't mean we stay in the true self twenty-four hours a day. Life is three steps forward and two steps backward." The asymmetry he does claim is epistemic, not behavioural: "once we know the big picture, we will never be satisfied with the little picture."

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
This is the clearest recent statement of Rohr's participatory Christology and of sin-as-disconnection rather than sin-as-transgression, and it supplies the developmental caveat (the false self is a required construction stage) that keeps his scheme from collapsing into a simple good-self/bad-self dualism.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-01:
  Problem: A moral account of sin as transgression makes the remedy juridical (forgiveness of acts) and leaves unexplained why guilt-focused repentance so often fails to change the underlying condition.
  Resource: Sin redefined functionally as a *relational state* — "the result of being separate from the Whole" — with addictive substitution as its predicted downstream signature.
  Solution: Relocates the remedy from absolution of acts to restoration of connection, and yields a testable-ish prediction: interventions that reduce felt separateness should reduce compulsive filling behaviour more than interventions that increase guilt over acts.
  Confidence: Medium
  Evidence: "The false self is characterized by separateness. Jewish and Christian traditions call this state of disconnectedness 'sin.' When we're separated from our deepest being, we are in a state of sin or alienation. When we are disconnected from our true self in God, we look for various false and addictive ways to fill our emptiness."

PRS-CANDIDATE-02:
  Problem: Contemplative traditions that valorise the true self risk pathologising ordinary ego development, which makes their advice useless or harmful to people who have not yet built a functioning self.
  Resource: An explicitly developmental concession — the false self is a necessary construction ("We have to create a false self to get started"), assembled from "culture, education, class, race, friends, gender, clothes, and money," whose defect is temporal (it stays too long) rather than moral.
  Solution: Converts the true/false self teaching from a binary into a staged account with a timing failure mode, which is what makes it compatible with developmental psychology rather than opposed to it.
  Confidence: Medium
  Evidence: "It seems that we have to leave the garden. We have to create a false self to get started; the trouble is that we take it far too seriously."

PRS-CANDIDATE-03:
  Problem: Claims of transformation are usually stated as achieved states, which are falsified the moment the claimant behaves badly, so the tradition either overclaims or retreats into unfalsifiability.
  Resource: An asymmetry between behavioural stability (explicitly denied) and epistemic irreversibility (claimed) — you do not stay in the true self, but having seen the big picture you cannot be satisfied by the small one.
  Solution: Gives the tradition an honest success criterion — dissatisfaction with the smaller frame rather than continuous residence in the larger — which is closer to something a longitudinal measure could actually track.
  Confidence: Speculative
  Evidence: "That doesn't mean we stay in the true self twenty-four hours a day. Life is three steps forward and two steps backward. Yet once we know the big picture, we will never be satisfied with the little picture."

## Cross-Tradition Signals
- Wright: Rohr reads the Christ event as "an announcement of what is happening in all of us, too" and glosses being "the Christ" as putting the human and divine together (Colossians 1:17–20). Wright's participatory-but-covenantal reading of *en Christo* covers similar New Testament ground from a historical-exegetical direction; whether Rohr's universalising move is licensed by those texts is exactly the kind of disagreement worth staging rather than smoothing.
- Fredrickson: "Separateness" as the root pathology and connection as the remedy is the theological twin of the loneliness/positivity-resonance axis. Rohr's unit is person-to-Whole; Fredrickson's is person-to-person and measured. The interesting question is whether the two connections are substitutable or whether one is a precondition for the other.
- Levin: "We are children of heaven and earth, both at the same time. Much of the work of enlightenment is bringing those two identities together" is a claim about integrating nested levels of selfhood — adjacent to Levin's work on how a self at one scale is composed of selves at another.
- Stump: Rohr's sin-as-disconnection is close in shape to Stump's account of the will's internal division and of the shame that isolates a person from union with others and with God — a genuine convergence between an analytic Thomist and a Franciscan, worth checking rather than assuming.
