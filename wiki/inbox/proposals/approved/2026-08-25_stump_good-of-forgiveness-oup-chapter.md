## Summary
A new book chapter by Stump (pp. 351–375) in the Oxford University Press collection *The Virtue of Forgiveness*, published 2026-07-07. Stump takes the two goods forgiveness is usually said to produce — that forgiving a wrongdoer removes the wrongdoer's guilt and restores him to his prior standing and relationships, and that forgiving brings peace to the forgiver by dissolving wrath, resentment and the desire for vengeance — and argues that forgiveness *by itself* can do neither. The chapter's positive claim is that the good of forgiveness must therefore be located somewhere other than in these two standard effects. The chapter text is paywalled; only the publisher's abstract was retrievable.

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
Forgiveness sits at the junction of the two problems the stump/ wiki is built around — the problem of suffering (*Wandering in Darkness*) and the problem of guilt and shame (*Atonement*) — and this is Stump arguing that the ordinary account of what forgiveness accomplishes is wrong on both the wrongdoer's side and the forgiver's side. It is her first new statement on forgiveness since *Atonement* (2018) and it is a negative result, which makes it unusually load-bearing: it says where the good of forgiveness is *not*.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-01:
  Problem: Forgiveness is standardly credited with removing a wrongdoer's guilt and thereby restoring both his inner condition and his relationships to their pre-wrongdoing state. Can the act of forgiving actually do this?
  Resource: Stump's distinction (developed at length in *Atonement*) between guilt as an objective standing incurred by the wrongdoer and the relational rupture that wrongdoing produces — two things that come apart, and only one of which is in the victim's power to alter.
  Solution: On the abstract's report, Stump argues that forgiveness alone cannot remove guilt or effect restoration, so the standard account misdescribes what the forgiver is doing. The positive account of where the good lies is stated in the chapter, which was not retrieved.
  Confidence: Medium
  Evidence: Publisher abstract, verbatim: "it is commonly thought that forgiveness of a wrongdoer removes the wrongdoer's guilt. With guilt removed, the wrongdoer is then restored to the same status he had before his wrongdoing... This chapter argues that forgiveness alone can do neither of these things and that the good of forgiveness has to be sought elsewhere." PRS-CANDIDATE-02:
  Problem: Forgiveness is also promoted as therapy for the forgiver — it is said to bring peace by removing wrath, resentment and the desire for vengeance. Is the peace a *product* of forgiving?
  Resource: The same abstract's second target: the psychological-benefit rationale for forgiveness.
  Solution: Stump denies that forgiveness alone produces this peace. The likely direction of her argument — that the disturbing attitudes are removed by something else (on her *Atonement* framework, by the healing of the forgiver's own relation to the good), and that forgiveness follows rather than causes that removal — is INFERENCE from her prior work and is not stated in the abstract.
  Confidence: Speculative
  Evidence: Abstract states the target and the denial ("forgiveness brings peace to the person who forgives, because it removes... wrath, resentment, desire for vengeance, and so on... forgiveness alone can do neither of these things"). It does not state Stump's replacement account. Nothing should be attributed to her here beyond the denial without reading the chapter.

## Cross-Tradition Signals
- **Rohr**: Rohr's Week 34 material (true self / false self) treats letting go of the egoic self as what makes non-defensive relation possible. If Stump is right that forgiveness does not itself dissolve resentment, the two programs converge on the same ordering claim — something prior to the act of forgiving has to change first — from analytic and contemplative directions respectively. This is a testable point of agreement, not a mood match.
- **Fredrickson**: the "forgiveness brings peace to the forgiver" claim is an empirical claim about affect, and it is the kind of claim Fredrickson's episode-level measurement could adjudicate. Stump's denial is a philosophical challenge to a literature that generally assumes the causal arrow runs from forgiving to well-being.
- **McGilchrist**: Stump's method here — refusing to let a practice be defined by its instrumental payoff — is the same move McGilchrist makes against instrumentalised accounts of attention.
