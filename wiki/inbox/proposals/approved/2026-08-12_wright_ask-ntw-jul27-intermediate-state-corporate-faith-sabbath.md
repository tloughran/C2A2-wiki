## Summary
A 26-minute main-feed episode taking three listener questions. First, whether someone else's faith can bring a person to Jesus — the description reports Wright treating this under "corporate faith," which is his standing objection to reading *pistis* as an individual mental state. Second, what happens to Christians after death, handled via "the Christian hope of resurrection" rather than via an account of the intermediate state alone. Third, why Jesus repeatedly healed on the Sabbath, framed as "how Jesus fulfils Israel's vision of God's new creation" — that is, as a claim about what the Sabbath *signified* rather than as a dispute about rule-breaking. The episode cites two Wright books on air: *God's Homecoming* and *For All The Saints*.

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
Two of the three questions land on the load-bearing seams of Wright's program. "Where do Christians go when they die?" is the question his whole eschatology exists to re-pose — his position (life after death, then *life after life after death*, the bodily resurrection into new creation) treats the popular question as asking about the waiting room while calling it the destination. And the Sabbath-healing question is where his "Jesus enacts Israel's story reaching its goal" method is easiest to see working: the healings are read as new-creation signs performed on the day that signified creation's completion, which explains why the Sabbath specifically was the recurring site of conflict. The corporate-faith answer, meanwhile, sits on his long-running argument about what *pistis* means — the same lexical territory as the already-captured "works of the law" episode (PROP-2026-07-05-002).

PRS-CANDIDATE-02:
  Problem: Can another person's faith be efficacious for someone who does not have it — and if so, what does that imply about what faith is?
  Resource: Corporate faith — the claim that *pistis* in the New Testament is constituted partly at the level of a community rather than being exclusively an individual's inner assent.
  Solution: If faith is partly a community-level property, then intercession and the faith of others are not exceptions requiring special explanation but the ordinary case (the episode's framing question is prompted by the paralytic lowered through the roof, Mark 2:5, "when Jesus saw *their* faith"). This is continuous with Wright's argument that *pistis Christou* and covenant-membership language resist individualist reduction.
  Confidence: Medium

PRS-CANDIDATE-03:
  Problem: Why does the Sabbath in particular recur as the site of conflict in the Gospel healing narratives?
  Resource: Reading the healings as new-creation signs rather than as halakhic provocations — "how Jesus fulfils Israel's vision of God's new creation."
  Solution: Explains the day-specificity: the Sabbath is the sign of creation completed and of the age to come, so a restorative act performed on it is a claim that the age to come has arrived. On this reading the conflict is not about rule-breaking at all but about a contested claim regarding what time it is in Israel's story.

## Cross-Tradition Signals
- **Rohr**: corporate faith (PRS-CANDIDATE-02) is the individual-level counterpart of the claim Rohr makes at the community level in the Week 24 material (PROP-2026-08-12-042) — that the early church "was *a living organism* that communicated the gospel primarily through relationships," not a set of individually held beliefs. Both traditions resist locating the unit of religious life in the single mind.
- **Levin**: a property (faith) that is constituted at the collective level and is not simply the sum of its members' individual states is structurally the same claim Levin makes about cognition in cell collectives — the competency is real at the higher level of organization and is not reducible to the parts' individual states.
- **Friston**: corporate faith would be described as a shared generative model maintained across a group, where the group's Markov blanket, not the individual's, is the relevant boundary.
- **Fredrickson**: the same seam appears in her work on positivity resonance, where the unit of the phenomenon is a dyad or group rather than a person.
