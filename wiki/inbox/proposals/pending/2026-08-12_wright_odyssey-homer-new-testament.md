## Summary
A 24-minute subscriber-only bonus episode pegged to the release of Christopher Nolan's *The Odyssey* (which the episode description reports took $264 million worldwide in its opening weekend). Wright and Mike Bird use the film as an occasion to examine what the New Testament's first readers, steeped in Homer, would have heard in it. The description names four contact points: how Luke's accounts of Jesus's and Paul's journeys "would have resonated with readers steeped in Homer's world"; the biblical meaning of hospitality; temptation and suffering; and spiritual conflict. The closing question is whether "the Gospel presents the true fulfilment of humanity's greatest epic."

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
Wright's method rests on reconstructing the worldview of the New Testament's first audiences — usually meaning Second Temple Judaism. This episode does the same work on the *other* half of that audience's cultural formation, the Greco-Roman literary world, and specifically on Homer, who functioned as the shared schooling text of the ancient Mediterranean. That matters because the standing objection to Wright's Jewish-context emphasis is that it under-weights the Hellenistic side. It also puts him adjacent to a real and contested area of New Testament scholarship — Dennis MacDonald's "Homeric mimesis" hypothesis, which argues Mark and Acts deliberately imitate Homeric episodes and which most scholars regard as overreaching. Where Wright stands on that spectrum is genuinely informative about his method, and this is the only captured item that touches it.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-01:
  Problem: The New Testament's first hearers were formed by Homer as well as by Israel's scriptures. Does that formation change what the Gospel narratives communicated to them, and can that effect be recovered?
  Resource: Reading Luke-Acts against the Homeric journey narrative — the sea voyages, the returns, the encounters with hospitality and its violation.
  Solution: Adds a second interpretive context alongside the Second Temple Jewish one, so that a Gospel episode can be doing simultaneous work in two literary worlds without either reading being reduced to the other. This is a strengthening of Wright's worldview method rather than a departure from it: the method's premise is that meaning is a function of the hearer's furnished world, and Homer is part of that furniture.
  Confidence: Speculative
  Evidence: The four contact points are named in the publisher-written description on the verified Apple Podcasts episode page ("They examine how Luke's account of Jesus and Paul's journeys would have resonated with readers steeped in Homer's world"). Audio NOT retrieved (subscriber-only). Critically, the description does NOT indicate how strong a claim Wright makes — whether it is resonance (weak, widely accepted) or deliberate literary imitation (strong, contested, MacDonald's position). The distinction determines whether this triplet is substantive or trivial. The reference to MacDonald above is this proposal's scholarly framing and is not mentioned in the source.

PRS-CANDIDATE-02:
  Problem: Hospitality is a minor virtue in modern ethics but a structuring institution in both Homer (*xenia*) and the New Testament. What is lost when it is read as mere kindness?
  Resource: The Homeric background to biblical hospitality — a reciprocal, quasi-legal guest-host obligation whose violation is the engine of the epic's plot.
  Solution: Recovers hospitality as a load-bearing social institution rather than a personal disposition, which changes the force of the New Testament's hospitality material (and of Luke's meal scenes in particular) from moral encouragement to a claim about how a community constitutes itself.
