## Summary
The American Philosophical Association released the audio recording of Eleonore Stump's John Dewey Lecture, "Dilige et quod vis fac," on 2026-07-28. The lecture was delivered at the 2023 APA Central Division Meeting; this is its first public release. The title is Augustine's maxim from his homilies on 1 John — "Love, and do what you will" — and the Dewey Lecture genre is explicitly reflective: the APA describes the series as philosophers communicating "the ideas and experiences that they felt are most important for people in the field to know."

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
Stump's whole program treats love as an ontological and epistemic category, not merely an ethical one — union with a person as a mode of knowing (`Wandering in Darkness`, `Philosophical Theology and the Knowledge of Persons`). A Dewey Lecture built on Augustine's love maxim is the closest thing in her corpus to a first-person statement of what the Thomistic-Augustinian account of love has been doing across her career, delivered to a mostly secular analytic audience. That combination — a premodern maxim defended in the APA's flagship reflective venue — is exactly the "living tradition engaging its rivals" evidence the C2A2 vitality criterion looks for.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-01:
  Problem: Analytic ethics treats love as a motive or attitude that must be constrained by independently specified moral rules; Augustine's "love and do what you will" appears either trivially true or dangerously permissive under that reading.
  Resource: Augustine's `dilige et quod vis fac` read through the Thomistic account of love as willing the good of the other plus willing union with the other — so that love is not a motive needing constraint but a structure that already carries its own norms.
  Solution: If love is the two-desire structure rather than an affect, the maxim is neither trivial nor permissive: it says a rightly ordered will needs no external rule, because the norms are internal to what loving is.
  Confidence: Speculative
  Evidence: TITLE AND GENRE ONLY — the full text sits behind APA member sign-in and the audio was not transcribed for this proposal. This triplet reconstructs the argument from the maxim plus Stump's published account of love; it must be checked against the actual lecture before ingestion.

PRS-CANDIDATE-02:
  Problem: How does a philosopher inside a premodern tradition give an account of her own work to a professional audience that does not share the tradition's commitments — without either translating the commitments away or refusing to translate?
  Resource: The Dewey Lecture as a genre — retrospective, first-person, addressed to the whole discipline — occupied by an explicitly Augustinian-Thomistic thesis.
  Solution: A worked instance of MacIntyrean second-first-language competence: the tradition speaks in its own idiom (Latin maxim, patristic source) inside the rival's venue and on the rival's terms of address.
  Confidence: Medium
  Evidence: APA blog post, 2026-07-28; APA's own description of the Dewey Lecture series as communicating "the ideas and experiences that the renowned philosophers who delivered them felt are most important for people in the field to know."

## Cross-Tradition Signals
- **Fredrickson**: Augustine's maxim and Fredrickson's positivity resonance both locate love in a relational structure rather than in an individual's inner state. Stump's two-desire analysis (good-of-the-other + union) and Fredrickson's amplifying trio (shared positive affect + mutual care + cross-person synchrony) are candidate descriptions of the same phenomenon at different levels. This is CROSS-005 and this source is a fresh instance of it.
- **McGilchrist**: "Love, and do what you will" as a claim that rightly ordered attention makes rule-following unnecessary — close to McGilchrist's argument that right-hemisphere attention grasps wholes that left-hemisphere procedure can only approximate through rules.
- **MacIntyre / C2A2 core**: See PRS-CANDIDATE-02. The lecture is itself a data point about tradition vitality, not merely a claim about it.
