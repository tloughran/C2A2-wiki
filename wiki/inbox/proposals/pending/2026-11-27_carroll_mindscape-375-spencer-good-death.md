## Summary
Carroll interviews medical sociologist Karen Lutfey Spencer (University of Colorado Denver) about her book *Your Good Death: Why End-of-Life Healthcare Is So Difficult and How to Make It Work for You*. They discuss how dying became a medical matter in the mid-1900s, the hospice "terrible choice" (to get Medicare hospice coverage you give up coverage for curative treatment), advance directives, family disagreements, and patient autonomy during cognitive decline.

**Weak-signal flag:** most of the substance is the guest's medical sociology. Carroll contributes his framing (0:00:00–0:04:30) and several questions (0:10:59, 0:28:47, 0:50:41, 0:58:25). The proposal is offered so the reviewer can decide. Denying it is a reasonable outcome.

Retrieval note: the official transcript on the episode page was read through targeted extraction. The intro, Carroll's longest turns and the passages on the "good death" were read. The full 1h+ transcript was not read line by line.

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
Carroll opens with a naturalist framing: death is "part of the fabric of biology", and a large multicellular organism lasts only a finite time. From there he moves straight to what "makes it all meaningful to me" (0:28:47). That pairing (no afterlife, and meaning still built at the human level) is poetic naturalism applied to the hardest case. The book's own argument is that a good death is plural, "*Your* Good Death, not *A* Good Death". On that view it is defined by each person's values and community, not by a single medical norm.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-01:
  Problem: If death is a purely biological endpoint, with no afterlife and with every person's maximum lifespan near 120 years, where do the norms for dying *well* come from?
  Resource: Carroll's framing: mortality is "part of the fabric of biology", average lifespan rises while maximum lifespan barely moves (0:00:00). He then asks the listener to decide "what it is that makes it all meaningful to me" and to tell the people who will be in the hospital room (0:28:47).
  Solution: The norms come from the person's own values and from their conversations with family and caregivers. Physics and biology do not supply them. This is the standard poetic-naturalist move: meaning is real at the level of human description. Carroll applies it in practice here but does not name the framework in the episode.
  Confidence: Medium
  Evidence: Transcript 0:00:00.8, 0:28:47.4.

PRS-CANDIDATE-02:
  Problem: Institutions built to cure (hospitals, insurers) shape the end of life badly, because their incentives point only at treatment.
  Resource: Spencer's account of the mid-1900s medicalization of death, which replaced community-centred dying with hospital dying. Also the Medicare hospice rule: two physicians certify an expected death within six months, and the patient gives up curative coverage (0:09:53, 0:15:13).
  Solution: Make end-of-life choices explicit and early, through advance directives and repeated family conversations, so that individual values can override institutional defaults. Carroll's closing question (0:58:25) asks whether a cultural reorientation, being "more grown-up about all this", is needed as well as rule changes. The question stays open.
  Confidence: Medium
  Evidence: Transcript 0:09:53.1–0:10:35, 0:15:13, 0:58:25.

PRS-CANDIDATE-03:
  Problem: How much autonomy should a person keep at the end of life, especially during cognitive decline, when family members disagree about what is good for them?
  Resource: Carroll states his own position: "the parent or any old person should have the right to choose what they're going to do, even if the rest of us think it's very silly, as long as it's not hurting anybody else" (0:50:41). This is a Millian harm-principle stance. He then asks whether end of life is a special case.
  Solution: Unresolved. Carroll offers a liberal default and flags cognitive decline as the test case where it may fail.
  Confidence: Speculative
  Evidence: Transcript 0:50:41.3.

## Cross-Tradition Signals
- **Stump (Thomistic):** the sharpest contrast in the network. Stump's account of suffering and of a good death is ordered to union with God. Carroll's account is ordered to self-chosen values within one finite life. Both reject a purely medical definition of a good death. A comparison here would test whether two rival traditions can agree on practice while disagreeing on metaphysics.
- **Fredrickson (positive psychology):** Spencer's emphasis on being accompanied, and on conversations with loved ones, fits Fredrickson's work on connection and positivity resonance. That work treats connection as a component of wellbeing, which could extend to the end of life.
- **McGilchrist:** the medicalization story (a person reduced to the presenting condition, with community context stripped away) reads as a left-hemisphere-dominance case study. That reading is the proposer's mapping, not a claim made in the episode.
- **Thread within the tradition:** continues the medical-humanities run of the November episodes (374 Fitzharris, PROP-2026-11-20-001) and the free-will and value episodes (369 Caruso).
