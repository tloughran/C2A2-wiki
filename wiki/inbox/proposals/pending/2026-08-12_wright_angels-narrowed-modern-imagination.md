## Summary
A 19-minute subscriber-only bonus episode. Wright and Mike Bird work through the biblical presentation of angels against the popular one ("fluffy wings, greeting cards and Hollywood clichés"). Four questions are named in the published description: why angels so often open with "Do not be afraid"; whether guardian angels are real; what Jesus means in saying the angels of the "little ones" see the face of God (Matthew 18:10); and whether angelic activity should be expected now. Wright reflects on C.S. Lewis's imaginative portrayal of angels and relays a friend's reported encounter during acute distress. The claim of most interest to this project is the closing one: that "the modern Western imagination may have become too narrow to recognise the unseen dimensions of God's creation." That is not a claim about angels — it is a claim about a historically specific contraction in what counts as perceptible, offered as the explanation for why the biblical material now reads as fantastical.

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
Wright's larger project turns on the claim that heaven and earth are overlapping and interlocking spheres of a single created order, not a natural realm plus a supernatural one stacked above it — the framework behind *Surprised by Hope* and *God's Homecoming*, both already in the wiki. Angels are the awkward test case for that framework, because they are the most straightforwardly "supernatural"-sounding item in the New Testament furniture. What makes this episode worth capturing is that Wright's response is not primarily exegetical but *diagnostic of the reader*: the difficulty is located in a narrowed modern imagination rather than in the text. That is a claim about the historical conditioning of perception, and it is the same move the network's perception-side traditions make from other directions.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-01:
  Problem: Why does biblical material about angels read as embarrassing or fantastical to modern readers who accept the rest of the text?
  Resource: The narrowed-imagination diagnosis — the claim that the modern Western imagination has contracted in a way that makes "the unseen dimensions of God's creation" unrecognizable rather than merely unbelieved.
  Solution: Relocates the problem from the ontology of angels to the perceptual and imaginative repertoire of the reader. This makes the question historical and investigable (when and how did the repertoire narrow?) rather than a standoff between belief and disbelief, and it is consistent with Wright's broader thesis that the natural/supernatural split is a post-Enlightenment import rather than a biblical category.

## Cross-Tradition Signals
- **McGilchrist**: the narrowed-imagination claim is very close to McGilchrist's central historical thesis in *The Master and His Emissary* — that the modern West has come to inhabit a left-hemisphere-dominant mode that cannot register what it has no representation for. Wright arrives at a contraction-of-perception diagnosis from New Testament scholarship; McGilchrist arrives at it from neuropsychology and cultural history. If they are describing the same contraction, that is a substantial convergence; if not, the difference should be nameable.
- **Kastrup**: Kastrup argues that physicalism's exclusion of non-material agencies is a metaphysical commitment mistaken for an empirical result. Wright's "too narrow to recognise" is the same objection in different vocabulary.
- **Hoffman**: an interface-theoretic reading would say the question is not whether angels exist behind the interface but whether the modern interface has dropped a category of icon. Wright's diagnosis is compatible with that framing without requiring it.
- **Rohr**: directly parallel to Rohr's "we alone desecrate them by our willful blindness and lack of reverence" (PROP-2026-08-12-040, 2026-08-10) — two traditions in the same week locating the failure to perceive the sacred in the perceiver rather than the world.
