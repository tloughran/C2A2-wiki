## Summary
Carroll interviews historian Andrea Wulf across three of her books: *The Invention of Nature*
(Alexander von Humboldt — nature as a living, interconnected web "from the smallest insect to
the tallest tree"), *Magnificent Rebels* (the Jena circle of Romantics — Goethe, Schiller,
the Schlegels, Novalis, Fichte — and the invention of the modern self), and *The Traveller*
(George Forster, early naturalist-ethnographer and champion of human equality). The episode
is a sustained engagement with the historical moment when scientific rigor and Romantic
holism were briefly unified — and then split.

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
This is Carroll doing genealogy on his own position. Poetic naturalism claims that meaning-level
and physics-level descriptions are both legitimate stories about one substrate; Humboldt and
the Jena circle represent the last major attempt to hold rigorous empiricism and the
subjective/aesthetic apprehension of nature in a single practice. How Carroll handles
Humboldt's "everything hangs together" holism tests whether poetic naturalism can absorb the
Romantic insight or must deflate it.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-01:
  Problem: Can a physicalist naturalism accommodate Humboldt's claim that nature is an interconnected living whole — and that the observer's aesthetic and emotional response is itself part of scientific understanding — without either dismissing it as pre-scientific or conceding ontological holism?
  Resource: Wulf's historical reconstruction of Humboldt's unified science (empirical measurement fused with aesthetic apprehension) as discussed with Carroll.
  Solution: A genealogical case study for poetic naturalism: the Enlightenment/Romantic split is shown to be a contingent historical bifurcation, not a forced choice — supporting Carroll's claim that meaning-level descriptions are legitimate stories rather than casualties of rigor.
  Confidence: Medium
  Evidence: Wulf on Humboldt: "everything in nature is connected... a living organism where everything hangs together from the smallest insect to the tallest tree" — discussed as the concept of nature "that very much shapes us today."

PRS-CANDIDATE-02:
  Problem: Where does the modern concept of the autonomous self come from, and is it a discovery or an invention — a question Carroll's naturalism (cf. PRS-12, the self as constructed narrative) needs history to answer.
  Resource: Wulf's *Magnificent Rebels* account of the Jena circle (Fichte's "Ich," Novalis, the Schlegels) as the site where the modern self-positing subject was articulated.
  Solution: Historical grounding for the constructed-self thesis of PRS-12: the self-model has a datable intellectual genealogy, strengthening the naturalist reading of selfhood as an emergent, historically conditioned real pattern.
  Confidence: Speculative
  Evidence: Episode segment on *Magnificent Rebels* and the Jena Romantics' philosophy of the self.
