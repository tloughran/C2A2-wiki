## Summary
Zhou, West, Lee, Choi, Hitokoto, Otake, Fredrickson & Salvador (2024) ask whether positivity resonance — a co-experienced, kind-hearted positive emotion — occurs at different levels across cultures. Across two studies (N = 5,711), East Asian participants reported less positivity resonance with strong social ties than European American participants, and the gap was partly explained by lower perceived relational mobility (how much freedom people feel they have to choose and change relationships). No comparable difference appeared for weak ties (strangers, acquaintances).

**Recency note:** this is not new in the 30-day window. It is proposed under the "significant work not yet captured" clause: the wiki already cites relational mobility as a collective-level condition (from the 2025 UBC colloquium framework), but the primary paper that supplies the cross-cultural evidence for it is not in `prs_triplets.md` or any prior proposal. Fredrickson is seventh of eight authors; the paper is a lab collaboration (West is a Fredrickson-lab member), consistent with previously approved West/Fredrickson lab papers. Details verified from the abstract on the Duke faculty database (Salvador, last updated 2026-10-07); full text not read.

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
It is the first cross-cultural test of positivity resonance, and it moves the theory away from treating resonance as a culture-free human constant. Instead, how often resonance occurs depends on a social-structural variable, which supports the multi-level "conducive conditions" framework with evidence from more than one culture.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-01:
  Problem: Positivity resonance theory was built almost entirely on US samples; it was unknown whether resonance levels are a human constant or vary by culture.
  Resource: Two-study cross-cultural comparison (N = 5,711) of everyday positivity resonance with strong vs. weak ties, East Asian vs. European American participants, with perceived relational mobility as a candidate mediator.
  Solution: Resonance with strong ties is lower in East Asian samples, and lower relational mobility partly mediates the difference. This makes relational mobility an empirically supported collective-level conducive condition, not just a theoretical slot in the framework.
  Confidence: High (for the reported difference and partial mediation; causal direction not established by a cross-sectional design)
  Evidence: Abstract: "compared with European American participants, East Asian participants showed lower levels of positivity resonance with strong social ties. Such differences were in part explained by lower levels of perceived relational mobility."

PRS-CANDIDATE-02:
  Problem: Do the culture effects on resonance apply equally to close relationships and to encounters with strangers?
  Resource: The strong-tie vs. weak-tie contrast within the same cross-cultural samples.
  Solution: The cultural gap appears for strong ties only, not weak ties. Read alongside West & Fredrickson's weak-tie work, this suggests stranger encounters may be a culturally more even channel for resonance than close relationships.
  Confidence: Medium (it is a null difference for weak ties; the absence of an effect is weaker evidence than its presence)
  Evidence: Abstract: "Comparable effects were not present for weak social ties."

## Cross-Tradition Signals
- **C2A2 core (flag strongly):** this gives the first evidence that the "emotional bandwidth" available for resonance differs across cultural traditions and that a measurable social-structural variable carries part of the difference. For inter-tradition dialogue, this predicts that resonance-based bridging will need to adjust to each tradition's relational mobility. The weak-tie result also points to a possible lever: encounters between strangers from different traditions may be less culturally constrained than close-tie channels.
- **McGilchrist:** cultural variation in relational, context-embedded attention (the East Asian holistic-attention literature) is a natural point of contact with McGilchrist's hemispheric account of cultures. This is speculative; the paper does not test attention.
- **Friston:** relational mobility can be read as a shared prior about how replaceable one's partners are, which shapes how much social prediction-error people will risk in shared positive affect. This is an interpretive bridge, not a finding.
- **Stump / MacIntyre:** this is empirical support for the claim that the conditions for love-as-event are partly formed by tradition, which bears on MacIntyre's thesis that practices are embedded in traditions.
