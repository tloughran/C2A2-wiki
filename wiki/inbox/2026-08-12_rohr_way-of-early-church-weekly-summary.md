## Summary
The closing page of CAC Week 24. The wiki holds only this week's Sunday entry ("A New Way of Living", PROP-2026-06-21-001, which was ingested as no-net-new / citation upgrade); the Saturday summary is a distinct document and carries material the Sunday entry does not. Rohr's own contribution across the week is a structural claim about early Christianity as an organizational form: "During Paul's lifetime, the Christian church was not yet an institution or a centrally organized set of common practices and beliefs. It was *a living organism* that communicated the gospel primarily through relationships." He pairs this with a normative claim about the future: "My vision of any future church needs to be much flatter and much more inclusive… less 'churchy,' surely less patriarchal, and more concerned with fulfilling its mission statement than with endlessly reciting its heavenly vision and philosophy statement." Guest voices extend the mechanism: Brian McLaren proposes that Christianity "has been most vital when it has been energized by movements of self-organizing—or perhaps we should say 'Spirit-organizing'—cells" that "grown, multiplied, and borne fruit"; Huston Smith notes the early Christians were few, poor, and endangered yet held "an inner peace that found expression in a joy that was uncontainable"; Diana Butler Bass frames the transmission medium as embodied love reshaping lives. The Week Twenty-Four Practice is Stephanie Spellers on beloved community (*The Church Cracked Open*, Church Publishing, 2021, 25–26), including a claim with an empirical flavor: "we humans are created with a homing device that begins to hum and light up when we see individuals and communities driven not by ego but by self-giving love."

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
This is the most explicitly *organizational* week the Rohr tradition has produced in the captured window. The wiki's Rohr material is heavily weighted toward individual transformation (nondual mind, two halves of life, Universal Christ) and thin on how a community that holds those commitments should be structured. Here Rohr commits to a specific answer — flat, decentralized, relationship-transmitted, self-organizing cells rather than a centrally organized institution — and dates the claim historically (the pre-Constantinian church) so that it is a description of something that existed, not only an aspiration. For the C2A2 project, which is itself an attempt to build a distributed community of practice, this is the tradition speaking directly to the project's own architecture.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-01:
  Problem: What organizational form transmits a way of life without a central authority to enforce it?
  Resource: The "living organism" model of the pre-institutional church — transmission "primarily through relationships" rather than through codified practice or belief.
  Solution: Proposes relationship density, not doctrinal specification, as the load-bearing transmission mechanism. This predicts that fidelity is preserved by the network's connectivity rather than by its rule set, and that formalizing the rule set is a symptom of the network having thinned.
  Confidence: High
  Evidence: Verbatim from the retrieved summary (Monday entry, Richard Rohr): "During Paul's lifetime, the Christian church was not yet an institution or a centrally organized set of common practices and beliefs. It was *a living organism* that communicated the gospel primarily through relationships."

PRS-CANDIDATE-02:
  Problem: What structural condition predicts vitality versus decline in a religious movement?
  Resource: McLaren's self-organizing-cell model — "Spirit-organizing" cells that "grown, multiplied, and borne fruit—fruit in just and vibrant institutions, fruit in thriving, peaceful, joyful communities."
  Solution: Names a measurable structural variable (cell autonomy and replication) and a two-part yield criterion (institutional justice, community flourishing). Because it makes vitality a function of decentralization, it predicts the fourth-century centralization Rohr flags as the inflection point where the church stopped being "countercultural and non-imperial."
  Confidence: Medium
  Evidence: Retrieved summary, Thursday entry, attributed to Brian McLaren — a guest teacher, so this is tradition-adjacent rather than Rohr's own voice. The correlation asserted between decentralization and vitality is offered historically, not demonstrated.

PRS-CANDIDATE-03:
  Problem: Why should a distributed, self-giving community attract people who do not share its beliefs?
  Resource: Spellers's "homing device" — a claimed innate human responsiveness that "begins to hum and light up when we see individuals and communities driven not by ego but by self-giving love," explicitly held to operate whether or not the observer is religious.
  Solution: Grounds the movement's growth in a perceptual/affective recognition capacity rather than in argument or authority, which explains transmission to people who were "attractive to those at the bottom" (Rohr, Sunday) without positing prior assent.
  Confidence: Speculative
  Evidence: Retrieved summary, Week Twenty-Four Practice, quoting Spellers, *The Church Cracked Open*, 25–26. Marked Speculative because the "homing device" is asserted as a universal human feature with no supporting evidence offered, and because Spellers is a guest voice rather than Rohr.

PRS-CANDIDATE-04:
  Problem: What does the tradition itself say it should look like going forward, and is that claim specific enough to check?
  Resource: Rohr's five-part specification for a future church — flatter, more inclusive, less "churchy," less patriarchal, oriented to mission execution over statement recitation.
  Solution: Gives the tradition a falsifiable self-description: an institution claiming this lineage can be checked against five named properties, and the last one (mission execution over statement recitation) is close to an auditable criterion.
  Confidence: High
  Evidence: Verbatim from the retrieved summary (Friday entry, Richard Rohr).

## Cross-Tradition Signals
- **Levin**: the "living organism" and "self-organizing cells" language is not merely metaphorical overlap — both Rohr's account of the early church and Levin's account of multicellular collectives describe how autonomous units cohere into a larger agent without a central controller, and both treat the loss of that cohesion as pathology rather than as reorganization.
- **Friston**: a network that maintains identity through relationship density rather than codified rules is describing a system whose Markov blanket is enacted by ongoing interaction. The fourth-century centralization Rohr flags would, on this reading, be the substitution of a rigid prior for active inference.
- **Wolfram**: "self-organizing cells" that grow and multiply from local rules with no global specification is the computational-irreducibility picture in ecclesial dress — the claim being that the community's form cannot be shortcut by writing down its philosophy statement.
- **Loughran / C2A2 architecture**: this week is the closest thing in the Rohr corpus to a design brief for the C2A2 community model itself (self-first articulation, opt-in visibility, distributed cells). It should be read against `architecture/swarm-contract.md` rather than only filed as tradition content.
