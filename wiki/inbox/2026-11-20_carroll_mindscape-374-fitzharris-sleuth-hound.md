## Summary
Carroll interviews medical historian Lindsey Fitzharris about her book *Sleuth-Hound: The Case of the Real Sherlock Holmes*. The book is about Joseph Bell, the Edinburgh surgeon who taught Arthur Conan Doyle and inspired Holmes. Bell also worked unofficially as a consulting detective alongside police surgeon Henry Littlejohn. The second half covers *The Facemaker*: Harold Gillies and the birth of reconstructive surgery in World War I.

**Weak-signal flag:** most of the substance comes from the guest's history of medicine, not from Carroll. Carroll's own contributions are a framing remark and three questions, listed below. The proposal is offered so the reviewer can decide. Denying it is a reasonable outcome.

Retrieval note: the official transcript on the episode page was read in full (0:00:00–1:11:20).

## Why This Matters for This Tradition
Carroll frames the episode as "data, not theory": "normally we talk about theory, but this is talking about data, the data of what actually happened in history" (0:03:45). He presents historical case material as what "feeds in to the big theories of life." That fits his pattern of using Mindscape to collect evidence for the emergence and level-of-description view behind poetic naturalism. It also connects to the November AMA answer (PROP-2026-11-13-001) about how theories gain or lose credence.

## Candidate PRS Triplets

PRS-CANDIDATE-01:
  Problem: Is inference from small clues (Bell's "method," Holmes's deductions) a repeatable epistemic skill, or a literary trick that only works when the author plants the clues?
  Resource: Bell's documented clinical cases, as told by Fitzharris. Examples include the army deserter identified by a "D" tattoo and the pipe smoker identified by a burn and a lip sore. Bell described his method as recognizing a disease "as you would recognize your best friend in a group of men."
  Solution: The question stays open. Carroll asks it directly: "how literary conceit the method is, how repeatable it is... Aren't some people just looking normal?" (0:27:04). Fitzharris answers that Bell could do it but that she knows no one today with the full skill. The episode states the question about abductive inference (reasoning to the best explanation) but does not settle it.
  Confidence: Speculative
  Evidence: Transcript 0:24:45–0:28:38.

PRS-CANDIDATE-02:
  Problem: Reductionist clinical practice isolates the presenting symptom and can miss causal information carried at higher levels of description, such as occupation, habits, dress, and patient testimony.
  Resource: Bell's holistic diagnostic method. Carroll's question at 0:33:50 calls it "thinking a little less reductionistically... how someone smokes or walks or dresses might have something to do with their ailments in a way that maybe we're even today not very good at accepting."
  Solution: A practical case for poetic naturalism's claim that higher-level descriptions are real and useful. Diagnosis that draws on person-level patterns beats symptom-level reduction, even though the underlying physiology is not in dispute. This reading is the proposer's mapping onto Carroll's framework. Carroll does not draw the connection explicitly.
  Confidence: Speculative
  Evidence: Transcript 0:33:50–0:35:09.

PRS-CANDIDATE-03:
  Problem: Medicine repeatedly attaches moral judgment to treatment decisions: pain as necessary in the pre-anesthesia era, cosmetic surgery as vanity, opioid limits for terminal patients. These judgments are often out of step with what patients actually value.
  Resource: Carroll's remark at 0:56:42 on the "underlying current... of moralizing" in medical history, and his comment at 1:06:59 that "we're not supposed to care about our looks, even though it's one of the things most people care about the most." Fitzharris adds Gillies's view that people "had a right to control their identity," including his 1940s gender-affirming surgery.
  Solution: Carroll implicitly sides with patient-centered values over imposed moral norms. That fits the move away from moral realism that he described as a change of mind in the November 2026 AMA. Carroll offers it as a remark, not an argument.
  Confidence: Speculative
  Evidence: Transcript 0:56:42–0:57:14; 1:06:59.

## Cross-Tradition Signals
- **Friston (FEP):** Bell's method is a clear historical example of perception as inference. Small sensory clues update a generative model of the patient's history. This is a possible teaching example for active inference.
- **McGilchrist:** Bell's holistic "gestalt" recognition versus fragmenting, reductive diagnosis lines up with McGilchrist's contrast between right-hemisphere and left-hemisphere attention. This is the strongest signal in the episode, but it is the proposer's reading.
- **Stump / Fredrickson:** Gillies's work treats medicine as restoring identity and the ability to flourish ("medicine isn't just about survival; it's about what comes after surviving"). It could serve as a bridge case on personhood and embodiment.
