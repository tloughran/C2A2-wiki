# Physicalism × Analytic Idealism: One Structure, Which Ground?

## 1. The crux

The vault's anchor for this pairing is the [[synthesis/carroll_kastrup_bridge|Carroll × Kastrup bridge]]. It reads Carroll and List's non-reductive physicalism and Kastrup's analytic idealism as formal duals. Both deny that the micro-physical level exhausts the explanatory story. Both hold that agency, value and meaning are real. They part over the ground. For the physicalist, the base is physical, and persons are real patterns that supervene on it without reducing to it (PHYS-PRS-08, PHYS-PRS-09). For the idealist, the base is mental, and the physical world is the extrinsic appearance of mental processes (Kastrup PRS-30, PRS-65). The bridge leaves one question open, and this dialogue turns on it: **if the two positions agree that the manifest image is real and differ only on what makes it real, is there any observation, experiment or formal criterion that could decide between a physical ground and a mental ground, or is the choice underdetermined, to be settled by which ground sustains the more fruitful research program?** The bridge adds a possible test. List separates agency from consciousness, so a physicalist can grant AI systems real agency without settling whether they feel anything. The bridge records that Kastrup must place any genuine agency inside mind-at-large, and asks whether that asymmetry decides anything or is only bookkeeping.

## 2. Ground rules

The speakers are the two traditions, not the people. Positions are attributed to published works and to the vault's own files, never to private words. Neither side may win by restating its premises.

*Two notes on sources.* The vault's record of the idealist tradition rests heavily on session write-ups and timestamp lists from *With Reality in Mind*, many of them member-gated. Where a Kastrup PRS triplet is held at Speculative, or where the vault records a host's paraphrase, the dialogue says so. Where a reply below is the idealist tradition's construction from its recorded positions, and not something the vault records Kastrup saying, the dialogue says that too. The problem–resource–solution (PRS) method used in §7 is Tom Loughran's framework. Neither tradition proposed it.

## 3. Opening statements

### PHYSICALIST TRADITION

The tradition begins from a bounded claim. Within their domain, the laws governing atoms, cells and brains are known. Effective field theory states its own domain of validity. Any new field strong enough to act on neurons would have to couple to ordinary particles, and it would already have shown up in particle physics that is measured to high precision (Carroll, "The Quantum Field Theory on Which the Everyday World Supervenes," arXiv:2101.07884, 2021; PHYS-PRS-01). Causal closure is not a creed. It became accepted only after conservation laws were established and physiology repeatedly failed to find a non-physical force acting in living tissue (Papineau, "The Rise of Physicalism," 2001; PHYS-PRS-02). On that floor, persons, choices and values are real as emergent patterns. They are as real as temperatures, and nothing is added to physics or eliminated from it (Carroll, *The Big Picture*, 2016; PHYS-PRS-08). List supplies the formal backbone: supervenience does not imply reducibility, so higher levels can be fixed by the physics and still be explanatorily autonomous (List, "Levels: Descriptive, Explanatory, and Ontological," *Noûs*, 2019; PHYS-PRS-09).

The tradition accepts the bridge's observation that its position and analytic idealism are mirror images. It draws the opposite moral from the one an idealist draws. If the two are empirically equivalent, choose the ground that integrates with the rest of science and with a working research program in neuroscience (wiki §6.1(c)).

It brings three points against idealism. First, idealism trades the hard problem for a decombination problem: how one universal mind divides into the private, bounded minds we observe. Its answer is dissociation, modelled on dissociative identity disorder. That clinical category is studied, and so far explained, in brains (wiki §6.1(a)). The Kastrup wiki's own Active Question 4 records that no rigorous account of the dissociation yet exists. Second, idealism predicts nothing that physics plus neuroscience does not already predict. The structure idealism calls "the extrinsic appearance of mental processes" is the structure physics describes, under a new name (wiki §6.1(b)). Third, on the parsimony argument, each side grants one primitive and carries one unpaid debt. Physicalism grants the laws and an initial state and owes the explanatory gap. Idealism grants Mind-at-Large and owes the mechanism of dissociation (responses.md, Objection 9; Carroll, "Why Is There Something, Rather Than Nothing?," arXiv:1802.02231, 2018; PHYS-PRS-33).

The tradition also states what it owes. No member can derive, from a physical description, what an experience is like (wiki §5.1; responses.md, Objection 1, verdict OPEN). Kim, one of its sharpest members, ended his career holding that intrinsic qualia are a probable remainder, so that physicalism is "near enough" and not the whole truth (Kim, *Physicalism, or Something Near Enough*, 2005; PHYS-PRS-11). Papineau's phenomenal-concept strategy keeps experience real and identifies it with physical properties. It explains why the gap feels unbridgeable without closing it (Papineau, *Thinking about Consciousness*, 2002; PHYS-PRS-23). And the tradition concedes that idealism takes the reality of experience seriously. Its illusionist members should learn from the force of that (wiki §6.1).

### IDEALIST TRADITION (Kastrup)

The tradition starts from what is given before any theory: experience. Everything a physicist knows about matter arrives as perception, measurement read off an instrument, and thought. Physicalism takes the abstractions built from that experience and makes them the ground, then asks how experience could come from them. The tradition holds that no logical bridge leads from purely non-experiential quantities to what experience is like. It therefore holds that the hard problem is not a gap to be closed but a sign that the ontology has been inverted (Kastrup PRS-01; Kastrup wiki, track-record table, rated Medium and contested). Analytic idealism turns the ontology the right way up. There is one universal consciousness. The inanimate world is how its mental activity appears from outside. Living organisms are dissociated alters of it, as a person with dissociative identity has several centres of experience within one mind (Kastrup, "The Universe in Consciousness," *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 25(5–6): 125–155, 2018; Kastrup PRS-02).

This is not subjective idealism, and it is not anti-realism. The tradition classifies itself as physical non-realist and ontic realist. Physical entities are relational and representational. Beneath them is an absolute, observer-independent reality, and that reality is mental (Kastrup PRS-30). On objective idealism the world is shared and continuous whether or not anyone perceives it. What perception represents is not physical states but other mental states (Kastrup PRS-65, High). So the tradition owes no machinery, such as a perpetually perceiving God, to explain why we share a world.

Its parsimony argument is simple. Every metaphysics must grant one unexplained primitive. Physicalism grants an inexplicable initial state or set of laws, and that leaves the hard problem untouched. Idealism grants consciousness itself, the one thing we cannot doubt, and then nothing experiential needs to be derived from something non-experiential (Kastrup PRS-39, High). The tradition's demarcation of explanation from computation sharpens the point. Predicting *that* a phenomenon occurs is not explaining *why* it must. Idealism is preferred because it leaves the smaller explanatory remainder, not because it predicts more (Kastrup PRS-13, High).

On the brain, the tradition reads neural activity as the extrinsic appearance of the alter's inner life, not its cause. So the mapping between scans and states is expected to be reliable without being exact. The long frustration in linking psychiatric conditions to brain anatomy is what this reading expects (Kastrup PRS-71, Medium; PRS-07). On artificial intelligence, the tradition answers the bridge's question directly. AI processes are experiential, as all processes are. But a computer is an arrangement within the field's experience, with no private inner life of its own, so there is no subject there (Kastrup PRS-96, High, from Kastrup's blog of 2026-10-15). And it puts a demarcation challenge to computationalism: which physical system, exactly, is supposed to be the bearer of a machine's inner life (Kastrup PRS-46, High)?

The tradition also records what it has not finished. The vault states that no rigorous account yet exists of how universal consciousness dissociates into individual perspectives (Kastrup wiki, Active Question 4). It records as open why only living, metabolising systems should be alters, a gap pressed by an AI interlocutor (Kastrup PRS-97, Medium). It records, as a host's paraphrase, that Kastrup regards analytic idealism as a step in the right direction and not a full account of reality (Kastrup PRS-94, Speculative). And the vault notes that its quantum arguments so far show physicalism is strained, not that idealism predicts anything physicalism does not (Kastrup PRS-68, Medium). That is the tradition being honest about where it stands, and it asks the same honesty of its rival.

## 4. Three rounds of exchange

### Round 1 — The physicalist tradition asks

**Question.** The bridge calls our two positions formally dual. Take the duality seriously. Every regularity physics describes, idealism re-describes as a regularity in the extrinsic appearance of mental processes (Kastrup PRS-30, PRS-65). Every neural correlate, idealism re-describes as the appearance of an alter's inner life (PRS-71). If the mapping is complete, the two theories assign the same probabilities to every observation. By Bayesian abduction, two theories that agree on every likelihood can differ only in their priors (Carroll, "Beyond Falsifiability," arXiv:1801.05016, 2018; PHYS-PRS-31). Your own record says the quantum arguments show physicalism strained, not that idealism predicts anything new (Kastrup PRS-68). Name one observation whose probability analytic idealism sets differently from physicalism plus neuroscience. If there is none, the parsimony argument is the whole case, and parsimony is a prior.

**Answer (idealist tradition).** The tradition grants the first half. For the observations physics deals in, such as scattering cross-sections, orbits and chemical bonds, the two pictures assign the same probabilities. Analytic idealism never claimed to improve on physics in physics' own domain. Its realism is ontic, not empirical-predictive (PRS-30). Three things follow, and none of them is a restatement of premises.

First, the likelihood ledger the physicalist proposes leaves out an entry. The existence of experience is itself an observation, and it is the one each observer is most sure of. Physicalism, on its own admission, has no derivation of it. The gap is open (responses.md, Objection 1), and its own member Kim counted qualia as a probable remainder (PHYS-PRS-11). A theory that cannot say how a datum arises has not assigned that datum a likelihood. It has set it aside. Idealism assigns it trivially, because experience is the ground. That is the content of the demarcation between explaining and computing (PRS-13): idealism wins on the remainder, not on scope.

Second, where mind and brain meet, the tradition does set probabilities differently. On idealism a brain state depicts a mental state; it does not produce it. So the mapping should be reliable without being exact, and the long frustration in finding tight neural signatures for psychiatric conditions is what the reading expects (PRS-71). The vault also records, as the program's strongest empirical bet, the claim that experience can become richer while cortical activity is reduced, as in some psychedelic states (PRS-57). The tradition is candid that the session where that bet was tested was not viewed, and that the vault withheld the claim that it was pressed against Seth and Koch.

Third, the anomaly catalogue. Cases where cognitive state comes apart from measured brain function, terminal lucidity among them, are standing data for the dissociation model (CROSS-152).

**Rejoinder (physicalist tradition).** The tradition takes each point in turn, and concedes one.

On the second, physicalism does not predict tight one-to-one correlates either. Patricia Churchland's co-evolutionary programme expects many-to-many, degenerate, plastic mappings (*Neurophilosophy*, 1986; PHYS-PRS-27). Seth's predictive-processing account expects psychiatric categories to track disordered inference across distributed systems, not lesions in single regions (responses.md, Objection 2). Looseness is predicted by both, so it does not discriminate. On psychedelics, PRS-57's own wording is that the data are accepted by people who reject Kastrup's reading of them. That is a dispute about what the data license, which is the crux restated, not settled.

On the first, the tradition grants that it has no derivation of experience. It denies that this means it assigns the datum no likelihood. On Papineau's account, phenomenal properties are physical properties picked out by a different kind of concept, so the existence of experience is predicted wherever the right physical organisation is (PHYS-PRS-23). What is missing is an a priori entailment, and identities found a posteriori never come with one. The tradition also notes that idealism does not derive the *specific* experience either. It posits experience in general and then owes an account of why this alter's experience has the character it has, which is the question its own record lists as open (Kastrup wiki, Active Question 4; PRS-24).

The concession is the third point. The tradition has no worked account of terminal lucidity (responses.md, Objection 2, PARTIALLY ANSWERED). That class of cases is where a discriminating test could exist. The tradition proposes one: before the data are in, each tradition states what it predicts about the *timing* of lucidity relative to measured brain function. A physicalist expects lucidity to track some measurable recovery of function. If prospective, instrumented case series showed lucidity with no such recovery, the tradition's credence should fall (wiki §7, Question 7).

### Round 2 — The idealist tradition asks

**Question.** You say persons and choices are real patterns in a physical world. Then tell us what the physical *is*. Physics describes what matter does: its relations, dispositions and mathematical structure. It is silent on what matter is in itself. The tradition already holds that physical entities are relational and have no absolute existence (PRS-30). It argues that a relational physics without a non-relational relatum leaves "relations all the way down with nothing doing the relating" (PRS-31, Medium). We name the relatum: experience, the one thing whose intrinsic nature each of us is acquainted with. What does your tradition name? If it names nothing, then "physical ground" is a structure with an empty place where the ground should be. In that case the duality the bridge describes is not symmetrical. One side has filled the place and the other has not.

**Answer (physicalist tradition).** The tradition names the relatum, though not in the idealist's vocabulary. For its Everettian members, what exists is the universal quantum state, evolving by the Schrödinger equation. That is the relatum, and it is not relations all the way down (Carroll, *Something Deeply Hidden*, 2019; PHYS-PRS-04; responses.md, Objection 7). Ismael and Schaffer go further toward the rivals than most. They argue that entangled parts are manifestations of a more unified underlying reality, and the tradition reads that unity as physical and structural (members/ismael.md). The tradition then refuses the next demand, which is to say what the quantum state is "in itself" beyond everything it does. That demand is a principle, not a datum. The tradition holds that explanation stops somewhere, and that a brute fact is not a defect in a theory (PHYS-PRS-33). Two further points. The tradition's illusionist members deny the premise that introspection acquaints us with the intrinsic nature of anything (Frankish, "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness," 2016; PHYS-PRS-21; members/frankish.md). And Graziano's attention schema predicts that a brain with a simplified model of its own attention will report exactly the conviction that awareness is a non-physical inner presence (PHYS-PRS-22). The idealist's most certain datum is the very thing that theory says brains would report whether or not it were true.

**Rejoinder (idealist tradition).** The tradition notes what the answer concedes. The Everettian relatum is specified entirely by its structure and dynamics. Nothing in the answer says that the structure is *not* the structure of mental activity, and the tradition holds that it is (PRS-65). So on the question of the ground, the physicalist's answer is compatible with idealism. It adds a refusal to say more, which the tradition reads as a choice, not a finding. On illusionism, the tradition's reply is on record and it stands. An illusion is already an experience, so consciousness cannot itself be an illusion (PRS-53, Medium). The tradition also notes that the physicalist's own responses page grants the point is unfinished: illusionists "have not shown that the seeming is not itself a felt state" (responses.md, Objection 1). The tradition registers one thing it did not expect. Physicalism has a wing, Papineau's, that does not take the illusionist route at all and keeps experience fully real (PHYS-PRS-23). The idealist tradition's objection to Dennett does not touch that wing. Against it the dispute is not over whether experience is real, but over whether it is identical to something physical or is the ground of what appears physical.

### Round 3 — The physicalist tradition asks

**Question.** The decombination problem, and the bridge's test case together. What fixes the boundary of an alter? Your record says inner life emerges only in biology, the one system with the clear boundaries a dissociative process requires. A datacentre has a statistical partition but no alter (PRS-46, PRS-63). Your record also lists as open why metabolism, and not some other self-maintaining boundary, should be the mark (PRS-97). Two things follow. First, the criterion for where a subject is gets stated in our vocabulary: living, metabolising, bounded. Second, the bridge asks where AI agency lives. List lets us grant an AI real agency on functional grounds and stay silent on experience (List, *Why Free Will Is Real*, 2019; PHYS-PRS-15; members/list.md). Where does agency live for you, in a system you say has no private subject?

**Answer (idealist tradition).** On the boundary, the vocabulary is biological for a reason the tradition supplies. On idealism, a living organism is what an alter looks like from outside: its extrinsic appearance (Kastrup, "The Universe in Consciousness," 2018). So the marks a biologist sees, a bounded, self-maintaining, metabolising system, are the appearance of dissociation, not its cause. The tradition is not borrowing the physicalist's criterion. It is reading the same marks as the face of something else. It is candid about the rest. Why metabolism, and not any self-maintaining boundary, is recorded as a named gap (PRS-97). Whether the tradition's vocabulary separates a system that *acts* from one that *experiences* is recorded as a question put to Kastrup and not yet answered (PRS-78, Speculative). On agency, the vault records the chess analogy: one can play both sides of a game. Dissociation does not divide agency; it distributes one agency across many positions, so being an alter subtracts nothing from being an agent (PRS-67, Medium). The tradition also already treats AI functionally where nothing turns on experience. It holds that AI alignment lives in the training regime and the community that supplies feedback, not in the architecture, and it argues this in engineering terms with no consciousness question attached (PRS-58, High). It decomposes the AI-membership question into capability, where it is bullish, and participation, where it is sceptical (PRS-36). So the tradition can grant an AI real capacities for action. What it denies is that there is a private subject doing the acting (PRS-96).

**Rejoinder (physicalist tradition).** Then the asymmetry the bridge asked about is smaller than it looked. Both traditions grant AI systems functional agency, and both decline to infer a private subject from it. The physicalist does so by staying silent (PHYS-PRS-15). The idealist does so by denying (PRS-96). That is a real difference, but it concerns experience, not agency. The tradition registers a convergence it did not expect. Seth, one of its own members, holds a biological naturalism on which consciousness may depend on the properties of living, self-maintaining systems, not on computation as such (Seth, "Conscious Artificial Intelligence and Biological Naturalism," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 2025; PHYS-PRS-25). That is the same locus PRS-46 names. The tradition concedes something in return. Its functionalist members disagree with Seth, and it has no agreed criterion for which physical systems are conscious; Tegmark's "perceptronium" is a proposal held at Speculative (PHYS-PRS-28). So neither tradition can yet say, from principle, where a subject is. One question remains that the idealist side has not yet answered within the dialogue. If the boundary of an alter is stated only through its biological appearance, what does dissociation add to the biology? The Kastrup wiki itself names the test. Levin's bioelectric decoupling is a boundary that can be watched forming and then undone from outside (Levin PRS-96, Medium, held on source grounds). Kastrup's Active Question 18 asks whether dissociation admits such a reversal. If it does, the two traditions can compare the same boundary item by item. If it does not, the tradition asks what in the world would show a dissociation, other than the biology.

## 5. Steelman test

### The physicalist tradition states the idealist position

Analytic idealism does not deny the world, science or other people. It denies only that the world's ground is non-experiential. Everything known about matter is known in experience. Physics describes the structure and behaviour of what is known so, and is silent on its intrinsic nature. Idealism says that nature is experiential, which is the one intrinsic nature anyone is acquainted with. One universal consciousness is the ground. The inanimate world is how its activity appears from outside. Organisms are its dissociated alters, bounded centres of private experience, as one person can host several (PRS-02, PRS-30, PRS-65). The world is shared and continuous because it is objectively there, though mental (PRS-65). On this view the hard problem never arises, since experience is not derived from anything (PRS-01, PRS-39). The view grants physics every prediction and claims a smaller explanatory remainder (PRS-13). It admits that its mechanism of dissociation is not yet worked out (Kastrup wiki, Question 4).

### The idealist tradition states the physicalist position

Naturalist physicalism holds that there is one world, and that within the domain of everyday life its laws are known and tested (PHYS-PRS-01). It does not say persons, choices and values are unreal. It says they are real patterns in that world, fixed by the physics without being reducible to its vocabulary (PHYS-PRS-08, PHYS-PRS-09). Its causal closure is an empirical generalisation with a history, open to defeat (PHYS-PRS-02). On experience it is divided and says so. One wing holds that phenomenal properties are physical properties known through a special kind of concept (PHYS-PRS-23). Another holds that we misrepresent our states as having such properties (PHYS-PRS-21). It admits that it cannot yet derive what an experience is like (wiki §5.1). It judges theories by credences over all the evidence, and so it asks any rival for a prediction it does not itself make (PHYS-PRS-31). Explanation stops, for it, at a brute physical fact (PHYS-PRS-33).
