# Keith Frankish — Physicalism Member

*Part of the [[rival/physicalism/wiki|Physicalism tradition]]. Written from inside the tradition.*

## 1. Who he is and his role in the tradition

Keith Frankish is an Honorary Reader at the University of Sheffield, a Visiting Research Fellow with The Open University, and an Adjunct Professor in the Brain and Mind programme at the University of Crete. His earlier work was on belief and dual-process theories of reasoning. He is now best known for illusionism, a name he gave to a family of views (with [[members/dennett]] as its most famous earlier holder) and which he developed in a 2016 target article that drew some twenty replies. Inside this tradition he has two roles. He states the most thoroughgoing response to the hard problem: there is no phenomenal property to explain, only a powerful tendency to represent our states as having one. And he is the tradition's most direct critic of panpsychism, which he has debated for years with Philip Goff on their joint YouTube channel "Mind Chat" (from 2021).

## 2. Core positions

1. **Illusionism.** Phenomenal consciousness as usually conceived is illusory. We systematically misrepresent our experiences as having phenomenal properties. Frankish's abstract puts the result this way: "the hard problem is replaced by the illusion problem." "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness," *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 23 (11-12) (2016); reissued with the replies as *Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness* (Imprint Academic, 2017).
2. **Illusionism is not denial of consciousness.** Illusionists do not deny that people are conscious in the ordinary sense of being aware and responsive; what they deny is the philosopher's notion of intrinsic, ineffable phenomenal properties. (Wikipedia's article on Frankish reports a 2020 statement to this effect. [UNVERIFIED: the original 2020 source was not located.])
3. **The zombie argument can be turned around.** If we can conceive a zombie world (physically like ours, no consciousness), we can equally conceive an "anti-zombie" world (physically like ours, purely physical, yet with consciousness), which undercuts the zombie argument against physicalism. "The Anti-Zombie Argument," *Philosophical Quarterly* 57 (2007).
4. **Two levels of mind.** Conscious, deliberate thinking is a "virtual" system realised in the activity of a non-conscious, intuitive one. *Mind and Supermind* (2004); co-edited with Jonathan Evans, *In Two Minds: Dual Processes and Beyond* (2009).
5. **The distortion thesis.** Introspection presents complex physical features of our states in distorted, simplified form. With Maxim Gorbachev, "Illusionism and the Distortion Thesis," *Synthese* 205 (2025).

## 3. Where he disagrees with fellow members

- **With [[members/papineau]]:** This is the clearest internal argument about consciousness. Papineau is a realist about phenomenal consciousness and an identity theorist; he holds that the problematic intuitions are mistaken but that consciousness itself is real, and has called the claim that consciousness does not exist "absurd" (his 2019 *JCS* response to Chalmers). Frankish replies that illusionism denies a theory of consciousness, not consciousness, so the charge misses. Whether "phenomenal realism plus identity" or "illusionism" is the better physicalism is a live dispute.
- **With [[members/seth]]:** Seth works on the properties of experience as real targets of explanation, and on his own account holds a pragmatic physicalism. He does not adopt illusionism.
- **With [[members/graziano]]:** Graziano's attention schema theory is, by Graziano's own admission in *Rethinking Consciousness* (2019), "a kind of illusionism," but Graziano is uneasy with the label. Frankish and Susan Blackmore have urged that the theory be called illusionism outright. The disagreement is about framing, not mechanism.
- **With [[members/carroll]]:** Carroll speaks of consciousness as real and emergent, not as an illusion. The tradition reads them as compatible at the level of physics and different at the level of philosophy of mind.
- **With [[members/list]]:** List keeps first-person realism and gives up "one world"; Frankish gives up first-person realism. They are on opposite sides of List's quadrilemma.

## 4. What he offers against the conscious-realist rivals

- **Against [[traditions/kastrup/wiki|Kastrup]] and panpsychism:** Frankish's argument targets the shared starting point. Idealists and panpsychists assume that in experience we are directly acquainted with the intrinsic nature of something mental. Frankish says we are acquainted only with how our brains represent our states, and that this representation is simplified and misleading. He has granted that *if* that mental essence were real, the case for panpsychism would be strong (as reported in a 2021 Philosophy Break write-up of Mind Chat). That concession shows how much depends on the starting point. The illusion problem is the tradition's way of explaining why idealism feels obvious without its being true.
- **Against [[traditions/hoffman/wiki|Hoffman]]:** Hoffman's conscious agents are defined by experiences as primitive. Illusionism denies that there are primitive experiential properties to build from. Hoffman's mathematics may survive, but its interpretation as a theory of consciousness would not.
- **On [[traditions/mcgilchrist/wiki|McGilchrist]]:** McGilchrist warns against a left-hemisphere tendency to replace lived reality with representation. An illusionist will say that the felt immediacy McGilchrist defends is itself a representation; the tradition notes this as a deep and possibly irreconcilable difference. ## 5. The weak point a fair rival would press

The objection most often raised is that an illusion of experience is itself an experience, so illusionism undermines itself. Frankish's reply is that the misrepresentation does not need to be phenomenally conscious, only a representation that disposes us to judge that it is. Many find this reply unsatisfying. The objection also has a practical side: if the felt badness of pain is a representation, why does it matter morally? Frankish answers that pain matters because of what it is and does, not because of an intrinsic phenomenal quality. A MacIntyrean, interested in the goods internal to human life, will press whether illusionism can sustain the moral weight we give to suffering and joy.
