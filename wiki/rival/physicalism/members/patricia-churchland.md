# Patricia Churchland — Physicalism Member

*Part of the [[rival/physicalism/wiki|Physicalism tradition]]. Written from inside the tradition.*

## 1. Who she is and her role in the tradition

Patricia Smith Churchland is Professor Emerita of Philosophy at the University of California, San Diego. Her 1986 book *Neurophilosophy* gave a name to the project of doing philosophy of mind in close contact with brain science. Inside this tradition she plays two roles. She is its strongest voice for method: philosophical questions about mind should be pursued by learning and doing the neuroscience, not by consulting intuitions about what is imaginable. And she extends physicalism into ethics, arguing that morality has a neurobiological basis in mammalian attachment and care. That second role matters a great deal at a meeting of Catholic philosophers, because it is where the tradition makes claims about the good and not just about the mind.

## 2. Core positions

1. **Neurophilosophy.** The mind is the brain's activity, and progress on the mind-body problem will come mainly from neuroscience, with philosophy and science informing each other. *Neurophilosophy: Toward a Unified Science of the Mind-Brain* (1986).
2. **The hard problem is not specially hard.** The case for treating consciousness as uniquely intractable rests on arguments from ignorance and failures of imagination, which reveal facts about our psychology, not the world. Her recommendation, verbatim: "Learn the science, do the science, and see what happens." "The Hornswoggle Problem," *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 3 (5-6) (1996).
3. **Computational neuroscience.** Brain function can be understood as computation in neural networks, studied at multiple levels of organisation. With Terrence Sejnowski, *The Computational Brain* (1992).
4. **The self as brain.** The self, free will and the soul are best approached as neurobiological topics; she treats the self-model as a brain function. *Touching a Nerve: The Self as Brain* (2013).
5. **The neurobiological platform of morality.** Morality grows out of mammalian care for offspring, extended to mates, kin and wider groups, and supported by neurochemistry such as oxytocin; neither religion, absolute rules nor pure reason is its primary foundation. *Braintrust: What Neuroscience Tells Us about Morality* (2011); *Conscience: The Origins of Moral Intuition* (2019).

## 3. Where she disagrees with fellow members

- **With [[members/frankish]] and [[members/dennett]]:** Churchland does not frame her view as illusionism. Her line is not that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion but that it is a brain phenomenon we do not yet understand. The disagreement is about strategy: deny the datum, or explain it.
- **With [[members/papineau]]:** Papineau grants that there is a real explanatory gap (phenomenal facts cannot be derived a priori from physical facts) and explains it by the special nature of phenomenal concepts. Churchland treats the gap as a temporary artefact of ignorance. Both are physicalists; they disagree about whether the gap is permanent.
- **With [[members/sapolsky]]:** In *Touching a Nerve* Churchland treats self-control as a real, graded, neurally implemented capacity, which is closer to compatibilism. Sapolsky concludes there is no free will at all. - **With [[members/carroll]]:** Carroll's case starts from physics; hers starts from biology. She seldom relies on the Core Theory argument, and her physicalism does not depend on any interpretation of quantum mechanics.

## 4. What she offers against the conscious-realist rivals

- **Against [[traditions/kastrup/wiki|Kastrup]] and panpsychism:** "The Hornswoggle Problem" is the tradition's standard reply to the move from "we cannot imagine how matter yields experience" to "experience must be fundamental." On her diagnosis, that move treats a fact about our imagination as a fact about nature. Kastrup's argument that materialism makes the hard problem insurmountable is, on this view, a prediction about the future of neuroscience that has not been earned.
- **Against [[traditions/hoffman/wiki|Hoffman]]:** Hoffman's theorem that fitness-tuned perception hides the truth is a claim about perception; neurobiology studies how perception is actually built. Her method asks what the visual system's measurable properties show about the relation between perception and the environment, rather than accepting a model-theoretic result. That answers the earlier, fitness-beats-truth stage. Against the current trace program (wiki §6.2), her method recasts the question of who restricts as an empirical one. Neurobiology can say which of a nervous system's states are physically sealed off from which, such as sensory and interoceptive boundaries. [UNVERIFIED: no direct Churchland response to Hoffman located; both applications are the tradition's.]
- **On [[traditions/fredrickson/wiki|Fredrickson]] and [[traditions/stump/wiki|Stump]]:** Her neurobiology of attachment and care offers a naturalistic account of the bonding and love that these traditions place at the centre of the good life. The tradition presents this as an explanation that does not need final causes.

## 5. The weak point a fair rival would press

Her strongest critics press the is/ought gap. If morality's platform is care and oxytocin, why ought anyone to extend care beyond kin, and why is extending it better than not? Churchland's answer appeals to social problem-solving within a community, which a MacIntyrean will recognise as an appeal to a practice with its own standards. Those standards are not themselves neurochemical. On consciousness, the "Hornswoggle" argument shows that the hard problem has not been proved permanent; it does not show that the problem is easy, and thirty years later it has not been solved.
