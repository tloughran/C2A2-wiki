# Daniel Dennett — Physicalism Member

*Part of the [[rival/physicalism/wiki|Physicalism tradition]]. Written from inside the tradition. Dennett died on 19 April 2024; this page uses the past tense for his life and the present tense for arguments that remain in play.*

## 1. Who he was and his role in the tradition

Daniel C. Dennett was a philosopher at Tufts University for most of his career, co-director of its Center for Cognitive Studies, and the most widely read philosopher of mind of his generation. Inside this tradition he did two jobs. He built the middle level: an account of how talk of beliefs, reasons and selves can be true of physical systems without being written into physics (the intentional stance and "real patterns"). And he took on consciousness directly, arguing that what seems to resist physical explanation is the way experience seems to us, not some extra property. Carroll's poetic naturalism ([[members/carroll]]) owes a good deal to Dennett's "real patterns" idea, and the illusionist wing of the tradition ([[members/frankish]], [[members/graziano]]) treats him as its senior figure.

## 2. Core positions

1. **The intentional stance.** Ascribing beliefs and desires to a system is a predictive strategy, and it is objectively warranted when it works. *The Intentional Stance* (1987).
2. **Real patterns.** Higher-level patterns are real in the sense that they support compression and prediction that lower-level descriptions cannot match in practice. "Real Patterns," *Journal of Philosophy* 88 (1991).
3. **Consciousness without a Cartesian theatre.** There is no single place in the brain where "it all comes together"; parallel processes produce multiple drafts, and the sense of a unified inner show is a product of these processes. *Consciousness Explained* (1991).
4. **Against qualia.** The concept of qualia (ineffable, intrinsic, private, directly known properties of experience) is too confused to be used coherently. "Quining Qualia," in Marcel and Bisiach (eds.), *Consciousness in Contemporary Science* (1988).
5. **Illusionism as the default.** Late in life he endorsed Keith Frankish's label: phenomenal consciousness as philosophers conceive it is an introspective illusion, and explaining why it seems otherwise is the real task. "Illusionism as the Obvious Default Theory of Consciousness," *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 23 (11-12) (2016); *From Bacteria to Bach and Back* (2017), where consciousness is described as a user-illusion (Thomas Nagel's review, "Is Consciousness an Illusion?", *New York Review of Books*, 2017, summarizes this reading).
6. **Compatibilist free will.** The freedom worth wanting is the evolved capacity for self-control and responsiveness to reasons, which determinism does not threaten. *Elbow Room* (1984); *Freedom Evolves* (2003); with Gregg Caruso, *Just Deserts: Debating Free Will* (2021).

## 3. Where he disagreed with fellow members

- **With [[members/paul-churchland]] and [[members/patricia-churchland]]:** Dennett was not an eliminativist about beliefs. The intentional stance keeps folk psychology as a true description of real patterns; the Churchlands expected it to be replaced by neuroscience.
- **With [[members/papineau]]:** Papineau thinks phenomenal consciousness is real and identical to brain processes, and has called the claim that consciousness does not exist absurd (Papineau's 2019 *JCS* response to Chalmers' meta-problem paper). He reads Dennett and Frankish as making that claim. Dennett would say he denied only a philosophers' theory of consciousness, not consciousness.
- **With [[members/sapolsky]]:** Dennett defended compatibilism against free-will scepticism for forty years. Sapolsky's *Determined* (2023) is the kind of view he argued against, and the *Just Deserts* exchange with Caruso is the tradition's best record of that argument.
- **With [[members/seth]]:** Seth works on the "real problem" (explaining, predicting and controlling the properties of experience) and does not adopt illusionism. The disagreement is mostly about labels and emphasis.
- **With [[members/kim]]:** Kim's exclusion argument puts pressure on any view where higher-level patterns do causal work. Dennett's "real patterns" answer is that the higher level adds predictive and explanatory power, not extra causes. Whether that answers Kim is contested inside the tradition.

## 4. What he offers against the conscious-realist rivals

- **Against [[traditions/kastrup/wiki|Kastrup]]:** Kastrup's argument depends on taking experience as the one thing we know directly. Dennett's work on heterophenomenology and illusion attacks that starting point: our confidence about what experience is like is itself a product of the brain and can be wrong. If introspection is unreliable about the nature of experience, it cannot be the foundation for an idealist metaphysics.
- **Against [[traditions/hoffman/wiki|Hoffman]]:** Dennett and Hoffman share a word ("user-illusion" / "interface") but use it in opposite directions. For Hoffman the interface hides a world of conscious agents; for Dennett the user-illusion is the brain's simplified model of its own workings. The tradition treats Dennett's reading as the one that needs no new ontology. Against the current trace program (wiki §6.2), the tradition adds the intentional stance. Calling a traced subset of states an "observer" is a stance that may earn its keep in prediction. It is not a discovery that the subset is conscious. Dennett died before trace logic was published; the application is the tradition's.
- **On [[traditions/levin/wiki|Levin]]:** This is a point of overlap rather than opposition. Dennett co-wrote "Cognition All the Way Down" with Michael Levin (*Aeon*, 2020), arguing that goal-directed vocabulary is properly applied to cells and tissues. For Dennett this was the intentional stance applied to biology, not evidence of mind as a fundamental feature. The tradition's position is that Levin's findings fit Dennett's framework and do not require conscious realism.
- **Against panpsychism:** His standard reply is that panpsychism takes the intuition of an inner light at face value. On his account, that intuition is what needs explaining.

## 5. The weak point a fair rival would press

Critics from Nagel onward have asked whether Dennett explained consciousness or changed the subject. If experience is an illusion, an illusion is itself an experience. Dennett's reply was that "illusion" means a misleading representation, which does not have to be experienced in the phenomenal sense, but many readers, including some physicalists such as [[members/papineau]], remain unconvinced. The intentional stance also raises a question for a MacIntyrean: if ascribing reasons is a predictive strategy, what makes one ascription of reasons *correct* rather than merely useful? That is the same pressure point as in [[members/carroll]]'s poetic naturalism.
