# David Papineau — Physicalism Member

*Part of the [[rival/physicalism/wiki|Physicalism tradition]]. Written from inside the tradition.*

## 1. Who he is and his role in the tradition

David Papineau is Professor of Philosophy at King's College London (per his British Academy fellow profile), with work spanning philosophy of mind, metaphysics, philosophy of science and decision theory. [UNVERIFIED: a concurrent CUNY Graduate Center appointment is from memory and is not stated here as fact.] He plays two roles in this tradition. First, he wrote the standard history and statement of the argument from the causal closure of physics: physics has turned out to be causally complete, so mental causes must be physical. Second, he is the tradition's leading phenomenal *realist*. Unlike [[members/frankish]] and [[members/dennett]], he holds that conscious experiences are real and identical to brain states. He explains why they seem otherwise by the special concepts we use to think about them. He is also, like [[members/carroll]], a long-time defender of the Everett interpretation, which gives the tradition a philosopher's voice on that question.

## 2. Core positions

1. **The argument from causal closure.** Physical effects have sufficient physical causes; mental states have physical effects; so mental states must themselves be physical, unless physical effects are systematically overdetermined. The premise of closure became credible only in the twentieth century, as physiology found no evidence of special mental or vital forces. "The Rise of Physicalism," in *The Proper Ambition of Science* (2000), reprinted in Gillett and Loewer (eds.), *Physicalism and Its Discontents* (2001); with David Spurrett, "A Note on the Completeness of Physics," *Analysis* (1999).
2. **Phenomenal concepts explain the gap.** We can think about conscious states in two ways: with ordinary physical or functional concepts, and with special phenomenal concepts that use the experience itself. Because the two kinds of concept are independent, no physical description will ever *feel* like a description of experience. That explains the explanatory gap without any non-physical property. *Thinking about Consciousness* (2002); "Phenomenal and Perceptual Concepts," in Alter and Walter (eds.), *Phenomenal Concepts and Phenomenal Knowledge* (2006).
3. **The intuition of distinctness.** Much of the resistance to physicalism comes from a brute intuition that mind and brain are distinct, which is a psychological fact about us and not evidence. "Consciousness, Physicalism and the Antipathetic Fallacy," *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* (1993); *Thinking about Consciousness* (2002).
4. **Realism, not illusionism.** Consciousness exists; what is illusory is a set of false ideas about it. He called the illusionist claim that consciousness does not exist "absurd" and favoured a weak illusionism about the intuitions only. "Response to Chalmers' 'The Meta-Problem of Consciousness'," *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 26 (9-10) (2019).
5. **Everettian decision theory.** In a many-worlds universe, rational agents should still weight outcomes by the Born rule; this is no worse off than probability in a single world. "A Fair Deal for Everettians," in Saunders, Barrett, Kent and Wallace (eds.), *Many Worlds?* (2010).
6. **Sensory experience as intrinsic qualitative properties.** Sensory experiences are intrinsic qualitative properties of subjects that represent external things only contingently, depending on how the subject is embedded in its environment; he rejects both naive realism and essentialist representationalism. *The Metaphysics of Sensory Experience* (2021). (The book says little about which physical properties ground these states, as its *Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews* reviewer notes.)

## 3. Where he disagrees with fellow members

- **With [[members/frankish]] and [[members/dennett]]:** See above. Papineau keeps phenomenal consciousness and explains the *appearance* of a gap; the illusionists deny the phenomenal properties themselves. Papineau's complaint is that illusionism sounds like denying the obvious; the illusionists' complaint is that phenomenal concepts smuggle the problem back in.
- **With [[members/patricia-churchland]]:** Papineau grants that phenomenal truths may never be derivable a priori from physical truths, and holds that such a conceptual gap would be harmless, since conceptual independence does not show ontological distinctness. Churchland treats the gap as a sign of current ignorance that neuroscience will close.
- **With [[members/kim]]:** Both accept causal closure and use it against dualism. Kim concluded that phenomenal qualities resist functional reduction and may be the residue that keeps physicalism only "near enough." Papineau holds that identity, unlike functional reduction, needs no derivation, so qualia are not a residue at all.
- **With [[members/list]]:** List and Menzies argue that higher-level mental properties can be causes in their own right under a difference-making account. Papineau's closure argument is usually read as pushing toward identity rather than causal autonomy. [UNVERIFIED: no Papineau text located that responds to List and Menzies directly.]
- **With [[members/weinberg]]:** On Everett, Papineau argues that the many-worlds view handles probability no worse than single-world views; Weinberg thought the probability problem remained serious.

## 4. What he offers against the conscious-realist rivals

- **Against [[traditions/kastrup/wiki|Kastrup]]:** Kastrup's argument takes the explanatory gap as proof that matter cannot give rise to mind. Papineau's phenomenal-concept strategy accepts the gap and explains it as a feature of our concepts: two ways of thinking about one thing will always seem to be about two things. If that explanation works, the gap is no evidence for idealism. His "intuition of distinctness" also diagnoses why idealism and dualism feel compelling.
- **Against [[traditions/levin/wiki|Levin]] and downward causation:** The causal-closure argument is the tradition's main reason to resist any claim that mind (or non-physical mathematical facts, as Levin has suggested) pushes physical systems around. If physics is causally complete, such causes would be either physical after all or redundant. The C2A2 Carroll wiki (Active Question 13) records a Levin claim that physicalism "has been dead for a long time" as a tension, not a bridge [UNVERIFIED: quotation taken from the vault's own record; primary source not re-checked for this page]; Papineau's argument is the reason the tradition treats it that way.
- **Against [[traditions/hoffman/wiki|Hoffman]]:** Papineau's 2021 view that sensory states represent the world only contingently, depending on how a subject is embedded, overlaps with Hoffman's point that perception need not mirror the world. The tradition's response is that this is compatible with physicalism: a mismatch between perception and world does not imply that the world is made of experience. Against the current trace program (wiki §6.2), the tradition deploys his causal-closure argument instead. If every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, then reading the same Markov kernels as networks of conscious agents adds no new causes. The reading either relabels the physical causes or conflicts with closure, and the formalism alone cannot say which. [UNVERIFIED: both applications are the tradition's, not Papineau's.]

## 5. The weak point a fair rival would press

The phenomenal-concept strategy faces a well-known dilemma (pressed by Chalmers and others): either phenomenal concepts can be explained in purely physical terms, in which case they cannot explain why zombies seem conceivable, or they cannot, in which case the hard problem returns at the level of concepts. Causal closure is also an empirical generalisation, and rivals will ask whether it has been tested in the systems that matter most (brains, and, if Levin is right, developing tissues). A Thomist will add that closure as stated assumes efficient causation is the only kind; formal and final causes are not competitors for the same causal role, so closure may not tell against them.
