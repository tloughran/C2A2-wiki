# Jaegwon Kim — Physicalism Member (argument, not partisan)

*Part of the [[rival/physicalism/wiki|Physicalism tradition]]. Written from inside the tradition. **Status note:** the tradition uses Kim's supervenience and causal exclusion argument; it does not claim Kim as a partisan. In his own late work Kim concluded that phenomenal qualities resist physical reduction and that physicalism is, in the words of his book title, the truth "or something near enough." Kim died on 27 November 2019; this page uses the past tense for his life.*

## 1. Who he was and his role in the tradition

Jaegwon Kim was a Korean-American philosopher who taught for many years at Brown University and was the most influential metaphysician of mind of his period. His work on supervenience in the 1980s and 1990s set the terms for debates about how mental properties relate to physical ones. His most famous result, the causal exclusion (or supervenience) argument, says that if the physical world is causally closed, mental properties can make a causal difference only if they are reducible to physical properties. The tradition uses that argument in two directions. Against dualism and against conscious-realist claims of mental causation, it shows what such claims cost. Inside the tradition, it presses non-reductive members ([[members/list]], [[members/carroll]], [[members/dennett]]) to say how higher levels can matter causally without competing with physics. Kim's own verdict, however, was not a full physicalism. He thought intentional states could be functionally reduced, and that the intrinsic qualities of experience could not.

## 2. Core positions

1. **Supervenience.** Mental properties supervene on physical properties: no mental difference without a physical difference. Kim gave the notion its precise forms (weak, strong, global). *Supervenience and Mind: Selected Philosophical Essays* (1993).
2. **The myth of non-reductive materialism.** Non-reductive physicalism, which accepts supervenience, irreducibility and mental causation together, is unstable. "The Myth of Nonreductive Materialism," *Proceedings and Addresses of the American Philosophical Association* 63 (1989).
3. **The causal exclusion argument.** Given closure (every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause) and exclusion (no systematic overdetermination), a supervening mental property has no causal work left to do unless it is identical to, or reducible to, its physical base. The non-reductivist must choose between reduction and epiphenomenalism. *Mind in a Physical World: An Essay on the Mind-Body Problem and Mental Causation* (1998).
4. **Functional reduction.** A mental property is reduced when it is defined by its causal role and the physical realiser of that role is found. Beliefs and desires are reducible in this way. *Mind in a Physical World* (1998); *Physicalism, or Something Near Enough* (2005).
5. **Qualia as the residue.** The intrinsic felt qualities of experience are not functionally definable, so they cannot be functionally reduced, and Kim held that if functional reduction fails for qualia, nothing will work. He concluded that their relational features (similarities and differences between qualia) are reducible and causally relevant, while their intrinsic character is left out. Physicalism is therefore not the whole truth but "near enough." *Physicalism, or Something Near Enough* (2005).

## 3. Where he disagreed with fellow members

- **With [[members/list]]:** List and Peter Menzies wrote directly against the exclusion principle ("Non-Reductive Physicalism and the Limits of the Exclusion Principle," *Journal of Philosophy*, 2009), arguing that under a difference-making account a higher-level property can be the cause. This is the main live disagreement about Kim inside the tradition.
- **With [[members/carroll]] and [[members/dennett]]:** Poetic naturalism and "real patterns" both treat higher-level descriptions as legitimate without reducing them. Kim's argument asks whether those descriptions pick out *causes* or only useful summaries of physical causes. The tradition's answer, broadly, is the second, which partly concedes Kim's point.
- **With [[members/papineau]]:** Both accepted causal closure. Papineau resolves the qualia problem by identity (no derivation needed); Kim thought identity without functional reduction left the qualia question open. Papineau is a full physicalist; Kim stopped short.
- **With [[members/frankish]]:** Kim's residue (intrinsic qualia) is exactly what illusionism denies exists. An illusionist can read Kim's conclusion as a reason to adopt illusionism: if the only obstacle to physicalism is intrinsic qualia, deny the qualia. Kim did not take that route.

## 4. What his argument offers against the conscious-realist rivals

- **Against [[traditions/levin/wiki|Levin]]'s downward causation:** Levin describes tissues and organisms as pursuing goals and treats higher-level patterns as causally effective. Kim's argument asks: if every physical change in the tissue has a sufficient physical cause, what extra causal work does the goal do? Either the goal is realised by and reducible to physical states (no conflict with physicalism) or it is a second cause of the same effects (overdetermination). The tradition uses this to resist reading Levin's results as evidence against closure. The C2A2 Carroll wiki's Active Question 12 (whether manipulable causal emergence is real or bookkeeping) is in effect a test of Kim's argument.
- **Against [[traditions/kastrup/wiki|Kastrup]] and [[traditions/hoffman/wiki|Hoffman]]:** If mind is fundamental and physics is its appearance, then either the mental level does the causal work (and physical causation is only apparent), or physics does it (and mind is idle). Kim's argument forces the idealist to say which. The tradition's reading is that idealism keeps mental causation only by making physical causation derivative, which is a heavy price given the success of physical explanation. The tradition also turns the exclusion argument on the current Hoffman-Prakash trace program (wiki §6.2). As the Hoffman wiki records it, an observer's dynamics is the trace kernel, uniquely fixed by the larger kernel it restricts (Hoffman PRS-34). Whatever the observer level does is then already settled one level down. So the observer-level agency the program describes (policies over observer windows, Hoffman PRS-08) faces Kim's choice: reduce it to the ambient dynamics or count it idle. Kim died before trace logic was proposed; the application is the tradition's.
- **The honest caveat:** Kim's argument cuts both ways. Kastrup and others can use Kim's conclusion about qualia as an admission from a leading metaphysician that experience resists physical explanation. The tradition should cite Kim's argument, not Kim's verdict.

## 5. The weak point a fair rival would press

Kim's own. He concluded that physicalism cannot accommodate the intrinsic qualities of experience, and that our best option is a physicalism that leaves them causally idle. A rival will say that a theory which makes the felt quality of pain epiphenomenal has not explained pain. A second objection, pressed by List and Menzies, is that the exclusion principle assumes a production model of causation that the special sciences do not use. A third, from a Thomist, is that Kim's framework recognises only efficient causes competing for the same effect; formal and final causes are not in competition with efficient ones, so the exclusion argument may not touch them.
