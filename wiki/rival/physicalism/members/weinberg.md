# Steven Weinberg — Physicalism Member

*Part of the [[rival/physicalism/wiki|Physicalism tradition]]. Written from inside the tradition. Weinberg died on 23 July 2021; this page uses the past tense for his life and work.*

## 1. Who he was and his role in the tradition

Steven Weinberg shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physics for the unified theory of the weak and electromagnetic interactions, a cornerstone of the Standard Model, and spent the later part of his career at the University of Texas at Austin. Through *The First Three Minutes* (1977), *Dreams of a Final Theory* (1992) and many essays in the *New York Review of Books*, he was the leading physicist to argue in public that nature has a layered explanatory structure with fundamental physics at its base. Inside this tradition he is the elder statesman whose reductionism [[members/carroll]] inherits and softens. He also models a virtue the tradition prizes: he said plainly when physics had not solved a problem, including the interpretation of quantum mechanics and the place of consciousness.

## 2. Core positions

1. **Reductionism as an attitude toward nature.** Explanations in science point downward: chemistry is explained by atomic physics, and atomic physics by particle physics. The "arrows of explanation" converge on a set of fundamental laws. Weinberg called reductionism, in his words, "an attitude toward nature itself" rather than a research rule. *Dreams of a Final Theory* (1992), chapter "Two Cheers for Reductionism."
2. **The prospect of a final theory.** Physics is moving toward a set of principles from which all other explanations follow, though it may not be reached soon. *Dreams of a Final Theory* (1992).
3. **Consciousness as the hardest case.** Weinberg conceded that consciousness presents what he called "the greatest difficulty" for linking every phenomenon to physics by arrows of explanation. He nonetheless expected the link to be made through physical information-processing. *Dreams of a Final Theory* (1992). (Quotations checked against reader highlights of the text, not against a page-numbered edition.)
4. **Scepticism about philosophy's help to physics.** Philosophy of science had, in his experience, done physics little good and sometimes harm (his example was Mach's resistance to atoms). *Dreams of a Final Theory* (1992), chapter "Against Philosophy."
5. **No satisfactory interpretation of quantum mechanics.** He judged both instrumentalist (Copenhagen-style) and realist (Everettian) readings to have serious problems; for Everett, the problem was explaining probability in deterministic evolution. "The Trouble with Quantum Mechanics," *New York Review of Books* (January 2017), reprinted in *Third Thoughts* (2018); see also "Quantum Mechanics Without State Vectors," *Physical Review A* 90 (2014), where he proposed taking the density matrix rather than the state vector as the description of reality.
6. **Science and religion.** He saw no room in nature for supernatural intervention, argued that a final theory would give no sign of a God who cares about human life or value, and was an outspoken critic of religion's public role. *Dreams of a Final Theory* (1992), closing chapters.

## 3. Where he disagreed with fellow members

- **With [[members/carroll]]:** On quantum mechanics, Carroll is a confident Everettian, while Weinberg never accepted that Everett had solved the probability problem. On reductionism, Carroll's poetic naturalism gives higher-level vocabularies more standing than Weinberg's "arrows of explanation" picture, in which the important explanations all point toward fundamental physics.
- **With [[members/list]] and [[members/ismael]]:** List defends the explanatory autonomy of higher levels; Ismael treats laws as descriptions of patterns rather than rules that push events along. Both positions loosen the strict top-down ordering of explanation that Weinberg favoured.
- **With [[members/tegmark]]:** Tegmark's Level IV multiverse and mathematical universe go well beyond what Weinberg thought physics could test. Weinberg discussed anthropic reasoning about the cosmological constant seriously, but the tradition does not read him as endorsing Tegmark's metaphysics.
- **With the philosophers in the tradition generally:** His "Against Philosophy" chapter sits uneasily with a tradition that includes [[members/dennett]], [[members/papineau]], [[members/kim]] and others. The tradition's reconciliation is that Weinberg was criticising philosophy that tells physicists what to do, not philosophy that interprets results.

## 4. What he offers against the conscious-realist rivals

- **Against [[traditions/kastrup/wiki|Kastrup]] and [[traditions/hoffman/wiki|Hoffman]]:** Weinberg's arrows of explanation make a structural point. In science, the most successful explanations run from the simpler and more general toward the complex and particular. Conscious-realist programmes reverse the arrow by taking minds as basic and deriving physics from them. The tradition presses Weinberg's question: has any explanation running in that direction yet produced a quantitative prediction comparable to the Standard Model's? For the Hoffman-Prakash program, the question now attaches to the trace program's eight published Conjectures (wiki §6.2).
- **On quantum mechanics as an entry point for mind:** Weinberg's honesty cuts both ways. He agreed that the interpretation of quantum mechanics is unresolved, which rivals cite. But his own proposals (the density-matrix approach of 2014) looked for a physical solution rather than a role for consciousness. The tradition's lesson is that an unsolved problem in physics is not evidence for idealism.
- **Against [[traditions/arkanihamed/wiki|Arkani-Hamed]]-inspired mind-first readings:** The amplituhedron work that Hoffman hopes to derive from conscious agents is a reformulation of quantum field theory, the framework Weinberg did much to build. The tradition's position is that this is new mathematics for physical scattering amplitudes, not evidence that spacetime comes from mind.

## 5. The weak point a fair rival would press

Weinberg himself named it: consciousness. He admitted that no arrow of explanation had yet been drawn to experience, and his expectation that one would be is a promissory note. A MacIntyrean will add a second point. Weinberg's sharp separation of fact from value, and his view that the universe offers no sign of caring about value, leaves the authority of scientific virtues unexplained. Honesty, rigour and the willingness to admit failure, which he practised, are goods internal to a practice, and his own worldview has difficulty saying why they bind.
