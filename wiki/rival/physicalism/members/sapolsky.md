# Robert Sapolsky — Physicalism Member

*Part of the [[rival/physicalism/wiki|Physicalism tradition]]. Written from inside the tradition. Sapolsky is the member most other members disagree with on free will, and this page says so.*

## 1. Who he is and his role in the tradition

Robert M. Sapolsky is the John A. and Cynthia Fry Gunn Professor of Biology, Neurology and Neurosurgery at Stanford University and a MacArthur Fellow. His research has combined laboratory neuroendocrinology (especially the effects of stress hormones on the brain) with decades of field study of wild baboons in Kenya. In this tradition he brings the most detailed account of the biological causes of behaviour, from the seconds before an action back through hormones, development, genes and culture. From that account he draws the tradition's most radical conclusion. Free will does not exist, and neither does the kind of moral responsibility that would justify blame and retributive punishment. Most philosophers in the tradition are compatibilists and reject this conclusion, but Sapolsky's work keeps the question honest. It shows how much of behaviour biology already explains.

## 2. Core positions

1. **Behaviour has causes at every timescale.** To explain any act, one must look at neural activity a second before, hormonal states hours before, experience and development over years, genes, and the culture and ecology of one's ancestors. *Behave: The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst* (2017).
2. **Hard incompatibilism: there is no free will.** Since every act results from causes the agent did not choose, there is no room for free will in the sense that would ground desert. A review describes his picture as an unbroken chain of causes, "turtles all the way down." *Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will* (2023).
3. **Chaos, emergence and quantum indeterminacy do not help.** Chaos and emergence concern unpredictability, not freedom from causes, and quantum randomness, even if it reached behaviour, would make actions random, not free. *Determined* (2023), several chapters.
4. **Against blame and retributive punishment.** Society should treat dangerous behaviour the way it treats contagious illness: by protective quarantine without blame. Sapolsky points to how attitudes to epilepsy and schizophrenia changed as their biology was understood. *Determined* (2023), drawing on Derk Pereboom's quarantine model.
5. **Stress and social rank.** Chronic stress and low social status damage health in primates and humans, showing how social conditions act through physiology. *Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers* (1994; later editions); *A Primate's Memoir* (2001).

## 3. Where he disagrees with fellow members

- **With [[members/dennett]]:** This is the tradition's main internal argument about agency. Dennett spent forty years defending compatibilism (*Elbow Room*, *Freedom Evolves*, *Just Deserts* with Gregg Caruso). Sapolsky treats compatibilism as the main position he has to defeat and names Dennett as its best-known defender. Critics, including the compatibilist John Martin Fischer in *Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews*, argue that *Determined* defines free will so that it requires uncaused causes, which assumes compatibilism false instead of arguing against it.
- **With [[members/carroll]], [[members/list]] and [[members/ismael]]:** All three are compatibilists who place free will at the level of agents. List argues that alternative possibilities are real at that level; Ismael argues that physical laws describe patterns and do not compel. Sapolsky's view is that higher-level descriptions do not create freedom, because they leave the causal history unchanged. On a point where they agree, a review of *Determined* reports that Carroll advised Sapolsky on the physics chapters. [UNVERIFIED: the advice is reported in one review (Inquisitive Biologist, 2024); not checked against the book's acknowledgements.]
- **With [[members/patricia-churchland]]:** Both draw on neurobiology. Churchland treats self-control as a real, graded brain capacity, which supports some notion of responsibility; Sapolsky's conclusions are more radical.

## 4. What he offers against the conscious-realist rivals

- **Against quantum and emergence arguments for freedom:** A common move among rivals is to say that quantum indeterminacy or emergent levels give mind room to act freely, and that this supports a non-physical or fundamental mind. Sapolsky's chapters on chaos, emergence and quantum mechanics are the tradition's sustained reply. Unpredictable is not uncaused, and random is not free.
- **Against [[traditions/levin/wiki|Levin]] and [[traditions/friston/wiki|Friston]] as evidence for teleology:** Sapolsky's multi-timescale explanations of behaviour show how goal-directed behaviour can be fully explained by prior causes. The tradition reads Levin's goal-directed tissues and Friston's predictive agents the same way: as systems whose goals are realised in causal machinery. [UNVERIFIED: Sapolsky has not been located discussing Levin or Friston by name; this is the tradition's application.]
- **On [[traditions/fredrickson/wiki|Fredrickson]] and [[traditions/stump/wiki|Stump]]:** Sapolsky's work on stress and social status shows the biology underneath flourishing and suffering, which these traditions treat in moral and theological terms. The tradition offers this as the physiological side of their findings, while noting that Sapolsky draws conclusions about responsibility they would reject.

## 5. The weak point a fair rival would press

Philosophers on many sides, including compatibilists inside this tradition, say *Determined* sets the bar for free will so high (an uncaused cause) that nothing could meet it, and then reports that nothing does. A rival will also press a consistency problem. Sapolsky argues that we ought to stop blaming people, but if no one is responsible for what they do, it is unclear what the "ought" means, and the same applies to his own conclusions. A MacIntyrean will add that the quarantine model replaces the practices of accountability, forgiveness and moral education, which sustain communities, with public-health management, and that this is a change in the kind of community we would be, not just a policy.
