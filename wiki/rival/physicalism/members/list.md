# Christian List — Physicalism Member

*Part of the [[rival/physicalism/wiki|Physicalism tradition]]. Written from inside the tradition. List already appears in this vault through [[synthesis/carroll_kastrup_bridge|the Carroll × Kastrup bridge]], which is anchored on his Mindscape episode 354 (18 May 2026). This page records honestly that his membership is partial.*

## 1. Who he is and his role in the tradition

Christian List is Professor of Philosophy and Decision Theory at LMU Munich, where he co-directs the Munich Center for Mathematical Philosophy; he was previously at the London School of Economics. His work spans social choice theory, group agency, free will and the metaphysics of levels. In this tradition he does a specific job: he supplies a rigorous account of how higher levels can have real explanatory autonomy without any break in physical causation. [[members/carroll]] has adopted that account as part of the backbone of poetic naturalism, and their Mindscape conversation is the vault's main record of the alliance. But List is not a physicalist about consciousness. On the same episode he said he had expected to be one and ended up with a view he described as more radically non-physicalist than Chalmers'. The tradition counts him as a member on agency and levels, and as a sympathetic dissenter on mind.

## 2. Core positions

1. **Supervenience without reduction.** High-level facts are fixed by low-level facts, but that does not make high-level concepts reducible to low-level ones. Levels can be descriptive, explanatory and ontological, and the distinctions matter. "Levels: Descriptive, Explanatory, and Ontological," *Noûs* 53 (2019). On Mindscape 354 he summed this up in his words: "supervenience does not imply explanatory reducibility."
2. **Naturalistic free will.** Free will requires intentional agency, alternative possibilities and causal control; all three are real at the agential level even if microphysics is deterministic, because determinism and indeterminism are level-relative. *Why Free Will Is Real* (2019); "Free Will, Determinism, and the Possibility of Doing Otherwise," *Noûs* 48 (2014).
3. **The exclusion argument does not show what it claims.** With Peter Menzies he argued that Kim's causal exclusion principle, properly stated, does not make mental causes redundant: a higher-level cause can be the difference-maker. "Non-Reductive Physicalism and the Limits of the Exclusion Principle," *Journal of Philosophy* 106 (2009); "My Brain Made Me Do It," in *Making a Difference* (2017).
4. **Group and artificial agency.** Agency can be ascribed on functional criteria to groups and, potentially, AI systems, independently of whether they are conscious. With Philip Pettit, *Group Agency* (2011); "Can AI Systems Have Free Will?" *Synthese* (2025).
5. **Consciousness requires irreducible first-personal facts.** No theory of consciousness can keep all four of: first-person realism, non-solipsism, non-fragmentation and one world. List's own response is to give up "one world," realising each subject's experience in a first-personally centred world. "The Many-Worlds Theory of Consciousness," *Noûs* 57 (2023); "A Quadrilemma for Theories of Consciousness," *Philosophical Quarterly* 75 (2025).

## 3. Where he disagrees with fellow members

- **With [[members/kim]]:** This is List's most important disagreement inside the tradition. Kim argued that non-reductive physicalism collapses into reduction or epiphenomenalism. List and Menzies argue that a difference-making account of causation lets higher-level properties be genuine causes. The tradition relies on both: Kim's argument against mind-first causation, and List's reply to keep agency real.
- **With [[members/sapolsky]]:** Sapolsky argues there is no free will; List argues it is real at the agential level. The two read the same neuroscience with different accounts of what "could have done otherwise" means.
- **With [[members/carroll]]:** On free will and levels they agree closely. On consciousness they part: Carroll treats it as emergent and physical; List requires irreducible first-personal facts.
- **With [[members/paul-churchland]] and [[members/patricia-churchland]]:** List's explanatory autonomy for intentional vocabulary is the opposite of eliminativism.
- **With [[members/frankish]] and [[members/dennett]]:** First-person realism is one of the four claims in List's quadrilemma, and he keeps it. Illusionism gives it up. ## 4. What he offers against the conscious-realist rivals

- **Against [[traditions/kastrup/wiki|Kastrup]]:** As [[synthesis/carroll_kastrup_bridge|the Carroll × Kastrup bridge]] puts it, List's non-reductive physicalism and Kastrup's analytic idealism are structural mirror images: both deny that the micro level exhausts the explanatory story. List's contribution is to show that this anti-reductionism does not require a mental base. Agency, choice and value can be real higher-level features of a physical world. That removes one motive for idealism.
- **On AI agency and C2A2's membership question:** List's separation of agency from consciousness lets the tradition grant real agency to AI systems on functional grounds while staying silent on whether they feel anything. The bridge page notes that Kastrup has to place any genuine agency inside mind-at-large, so this is a real asymmetry between the traditions.
- **The honest caveat:** On consciousness itself List is closer to the rivals than to the tradition. A conscious realist can cite his quadrilemma as a serious argument that physicalism cannot account for first-personal facts. The tradition should expect that citation and not pretend List is on its side there.

## 5. The weak point a fair rival would press

From outside: List's free will lives at the agential level, but a sceptic can ask whether "level-relative possibility" is real possibility or a fact about descriptions. From inside the tradition the deeper problem is his consciousness work. If first-personal facts are irreducible, then the physical facts do not fix everything, and his non-reductive *physicalism* about agency sits next to a non-physicalism about experience. The tradition has not yet worked out how to hold both.
